Keep an Affiliate-Link Record as a Household Shopper

Published by Petpalz Malaysia.

A practical guide for Malaysian pet owners on keeping a simple record of affiliate links, product references, named merchants, price-check dates and order destinations so household shopping stays traceable.

Why a Link Record Beats Remembering

A link you tapped on a phone is easy to lose. The page closes, the chat scrolls away, and a week later the household is trying to remember which shop had the harness in the right size, or whether the food price seen last month included delivery. A link record is simply a short written trail that keeps four things together: the product reference, the named merchant, the date you checked the price, and where the order was actually placed.

This matters more in a shared household than it first appears. One person may browse on a laptop, another may order from a phone, and a third may pay. Without a record, each person holds a different fragment of the same decision. The record does not need to be elaborate. A single note file, a shared document or a paper sheet on the fridge all work, as long as everyone can see it and add to it.

It also helps to be clear about what an affiliate link is. Petpalz Malaysia is a pet affiliate website owned by Blackstone Intelligence Sarawak, and it does not own the products it promotes. That means an editorial page may introduce a product and point to a seller, but the seller named at checkout is the party handling payment, delivery and returns. Keeping the record separates those two roles in your own notes, so a later question about a parcel goes to the right place.

A useful habit is to write the record before paying, not after. The moment of checkout is when the merchant name, variant and price are all visible on screen. Copying them down takes under a minute and saves a much longer reconstruction later.

The Four Fields Every Entry Needs

A workable record has four core fields, and each one answers a question that tends to come up later.

Product reference. Write what the item is in your own words plus the variant details shown on the listing: pack size, flavour, colour, size label, or model name. If the listing uses a product code, note it. The goal is to be able to tell two similar listings apart without reopening either one.

Named merchant. Record the seller as displayed, not the platform you started from. A marketplace listing, a brand-operated store and a specialist retailer are different sellers, and the name that appears at checkout is the one that matters. If the page redirects during checkout, note the final shop name you land on.

Price-check date. Write the date you looked, and the amount in ringgit with the pack size and variant beside it. A price without a date and a pack size is hard to compare later, because the same figure can mean very different things across sizes.

Order destination. Note where the order was placed and, if relevant, the delivery address used. This is the field that prevents the classic household mix-up where two people order the same bag of food to two different addresses.

Two optional fields earn their place quickly: a short note on what still needs checking, and the order reference once a purchase is made. Neither is essential on day one, but both make the record more useful on the second read.

Separating the Introduction From the Seller

The most common confusion in affiliate shopping is treating the page that introduced a product as the shop that sold it. They are different parties with different responsibilities, and the record should show that clearly.

On the editorial side, a page may explain what a product is designed for, where its limits lie, and what a buyer should verify before purchasing from a third-party seller. That is guidance, not a sale. On the seller side, the merchant named at checkout handles the transaction: the price you actually pay, the delivery coverage, the return conditions and any buyer protection that applies.

A simple way to keep this straight in the record is a two-column layout. In the left column, note where you found the product and what the page said about it. In the right column, note the merchant, the price at checkout and the terms shown there. When a question arises later, you will know whether to reread the guide or contact the seller.

This separation also stops a subtle error: assuming that because a page discussed a product, the page is responsible for the parcel. It is not. Returns, warranties and payment conditions belong to the actual merchant, and the record should attribute them there. If you are comparing several candidate stores before a first order, the shop section of this site is a reasonable place to start gathering the names you will later write into the record.

A Household Worksheet You Can Copy

The following layout is an example format, not a standard anyone must follow. Adapt the columns to what your household actually argues about.

Column one: date checked. Column two: product reference, including variant and pack size. Column three: named merchant as shown at checkout. Column four: price in ringgit with pack size noted. Column five: delivery terms noted in brief, such as coverage area and any charge shown. Column six: order destination or address. Column seven: status, for example browsing, ordered, delivered or query open. Column eight: order reference once available.

A worked fictional example shows how it reads. On a Tuesday, one household member notes a bag of dry dog food, a named merchant, a price in ringgit for a stated pack size, and delivery to a Sarawak address. On Thursday, a second person sees the same product at what looks like a lower figure, but the pack size is smaller. Because both entries carry pack size and date, the comparison is honest rather than misleading.

Two practical rules keep the worksheet alive. First, one row per product variant, not one row per shopping trip, so repeat purchases stack up in a readable way. Second, a short review every few weeks: close rows that are finished and archive the rest. A record that grows without pruning stops being read.

If several people order pet supplies for the same household, agree on who updates the status column. That single convention prevents most duplicate orders.

Recording Prices Without Fooling Yourself

Price notes are only useful when they are comparable. Three habits make the difference.

Always pair the amount with the pack size and variant. A figure for a small bag and a figure for a large bag are not the same measurement, and writing them side by side without context invites a wrong conclusion. Where it helps, note the unit basis you used, such as price per kilogram, and say that it is your own calculation rather than something the seller stated.

Always date the observation. Prices and promotions change, and a figure from months ago should not be treated as current. The date tells the next reader how much weight to give the entry.

Separate the item price from everything else. Shipping charges, minimum spend thresholds, vouchers and checkout-dependent promotions can change the total, so record them as separate notes rather than folding them into one number. If a discount only appears at checkout, write that down as a condition rather than as the price.

One more caution: do not treat a commission arrangement as evidence of quality. An affiliate introduction may earn a commission from qualifying purchases, but that says nothing about whether a product suits your pet. The record should capture what you verified, not what a page implied.

When you are weighing where to look for lower prices, treat any claim of cheapness as something to check against your own dated entries rather than something to accept.

When a Link Redirects or a Listing Changes

Links move. A listing may redirect to a different page, a seller may revise the wording, or a product may be relisted under a new name. The record is what lets you notice the change instead of absorbing it silently.

If a link takes you somewhere unexpected, pause before paying and note three things: the page you started from, the page you landed on, and the merchant name displayed at the final checkout step. If those do not line up in a way you can explain, treat it as a question to resolve rather than a step to rush. Writing the discrepancy down is often enough to make the right decision obvious.

If a listing's wording changes between your first look and your second, compare it against your saved entry. Note what was removed or reworded, and whether the change affects a detail you cared about, such as a stated dimension or an included part. Then decide whether to ask the seller a specific question or to look elsewhere.

This is also where the order destination field earns its keep. If a redirect sends you to a different shop than the one in your record, the delivery and return terms may differ too. Update the record only after you have confirmed the new merchant's terms, not before.

Keep the original entry rather than overwriting it. A short line noting the revision date and what changed preserves the trail, which is exactly what a record is for.

A Short Pre-Check Before You Pay

Before committing an order, run through a brief check against your record. This is a suggested routine, not a rule, and it should take a couple of minutes at most.

First, confirm the product reference matches what you intend to buy, including variant and pack size. Second, confirm the named merchant at checkout matches the one in your entry, and update the entry if a redirect changed it. Third, confirm the price and note the date, keeping shipping and any conditions separate. Fourth, confirm the delivery destination, especially where the household has more than one address in use. Fifth, note the return conditions as the seller states them, attributed to that seller rather than to any guide you read.

If any of the five cannot be confirmed, that is the item to resolve before paying. A missing detail is easier to chase now than after a parcel is on its way.

Finally, once the order is placed, add the order reference and set the status. That single update turns a browsing note into a usable record for the next reorder, and it gives everyone in the household the same picture.

Used consistently, the record becomes a quiet household asset: a dated trail of what was considered, from which merchant, at what price, and where it was sent. It will not make decisions for you, but it keeps the facts of each decision where you can find them.
