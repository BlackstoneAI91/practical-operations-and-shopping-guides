Write a Short Seller Enquiry When a Product Video Omits the Part You Need

Published by Petpalz Malaysia.

A practical Malaysian buyer's guide to drafting a short, specific seller enquiry when a product video leaves out the detail you need, using timestamps, variant references and a fictional pet toy example.

Why a Video Leaves Gaps You Must Fill in Writing

Product videos are edited for persuasion, not for completeness. A clip may show a toy being lifted, turned and placed down, yet never show the closure, the inside of a compartment or the part that attaches one piece to another. That is not necessarily dishonest; it is simply a short format. The buyer's job is to notice the gap and ask about it in writing before ordering.

That matters here: the seller who answers your enquiry is the merchant, not the publication. Treat any reply as the seller's statement about their own listing.

A useful enquiry does three things. It identifies the exact product and variant, it points to the moment in the video where the information stops, and it asks one clear question about the missing part. Keep it short enough that a support agent can answer it in a single reply. Long messages with several unrelated questions tend to produce partial answers, and partial answers are hard to compare later.

Before you type anything, decide what you would do with each possible answer. If the reply will not change your decision, the question is probably not worth sending.

Build a Reference Block Before You Write the Question

The most common reason an enquiry fails is that the seller cannot tell which item you mean. Listings often carry several variants under one page, and a video may be reused across them. Start with a small reference block you can paste into any message.

Use these fields, filled in from the listing as it appears when you look:

Product name exactly as written, including any model or code shown. Variant selected in the dropdown or option field, not the variant in the main photo. Listing reference or item number, if the page displays one. Date and time you viewed the listing, so a later change can be traced. Video location, meaning where the clip sits on the page and its approximate timestamp.

The timestamp is the part most buyers skip. Write it as a plain note, for example: at about 0:14 the clip shows the toy being held, then cuts to a different angle. This tells the seller precisely where your question begins. It also creates a shared reference if the video is later replaced.

Keep this block in a note on your phone or in a document. When you compare several sellers, identical reference blocks make the replies easier to read side by side.

A Fictional Example: The Missing Attachment Loop

Suppose you are looking at a listing for a fabric puzzle toy for a small dog. The video shows the toy being lifted, squeezed and placed on a floor. At around 0:20 the clip cuts away just as the presenter turns the toy over. You want to know whether a spare attachment loop is included, because the listing photos show one loop but the text does not mention a spare.

A short enquiry might read:

Hello, I am looking at [product name], variant [variant], listing reference [reference], viewed on [date]. In the product video at about 0:20 the toy is turned over and the clip cuts away before the reverse side is shown. Could you confirm whether the pack includes one attachment loop or a spare loop as well? A photo of the contents laid out would help. Thank you.

Notice what the message does not do. It does not ask whether the toy is safe, durable or suitable for a particular animal. It does not describe the toy as tested. It asks a factual question about pack contents, which a seller can answer from their own records. That keeps the exchange within what the seller actually knows.

If you are browsing an online pet store for toys and accessories, this same pattern works for clips, cords, covers and small parts. The subject changes; the structure does not.

Word the Question So the Answer Is Usable

A vague question invites a vague reply. Compare these two openings. The first asks whether the toy is good. The second asks whether the pack contains a spare loop. Only the second produces something you can record and compare.

Useful question shapes include:

Does the pack include X, and if so, how many? Is the part shown at [timestamp] the same part supplied with the selected variant? Which variant in the dropdown matches the item in the video? Can you confirm the contents list as supplied, separate from the photos?

Avoid stacking conditions. A message that asks about contents, delivery timing and returns at once may get an answer to only one of them. Send the contents question first, then follow up once you have a reply. If delivery timing matters to you, ask it as a separate short message after the first answer arrives, so each reply maps to one question.

Keep your tone plain and specific. You are not accusing the seller of hiding anything; you are noting that the video does not show a particular view. That framing tends to get a more helpful response than a complaint.

Record the Reply Alongside the Listing Evidence

When a reply arrives, save it with the reference block rather than leaving it in a message thread. A simple record has four columns: what you asked, what the seller answered, the date of the reply, and what remains unconfirmed. This is the same discipline you would use when comparing pet supplies listings generally.

Mark each line as confirmed, unclear or unanswered. A seller who says the pack includes one loop has confirmed one point. A seller who replies that the item is as pictured has not answered the question, because the picture itself is what you were asking about. Note that as unclear and decide whether to ask again once, in simpler words.

Keep the record factual. Do not write that the seller guaranteed anything unless the reply uses that word. Do not treat a friendly reply as evidence that the product suits your pet. The reply tells you about pack contents and listing details; it does not tell you how the toy will perform at home.

If you later compare two sellers, this record lets you see which one answered the specific question and which one only sent a general description.

Decide Whether to Order, Wait or Walk Away

Once you have a reply, make a small decision rather than drifting into a purchase. A short worksheet helps:

Did the seller answer the exact question about the missing part? Yes or no. Did the answer name the variant or reference you supplied? Yes or no. Is the missing part something you need, or merely something you were curious about? If it is missing, does the listing still meet your reason for buying? Is there a second question you still need answered before ordering?

Score each line as clear, partial or absent. This is an author heuristic, not a tested method; use it as a prompt, not a formula. If the answer is clear and names your variant, you can proceed with the seller's stated terms. If it is partial, send one short follow-up quoting your original reference block. If it is absent after a reasonable wait, treat the listing as unresolved and compare elsewhere.

A reasonable wait is your own judgement. There is no universal response time, and this guide does not claim one. What matters is that you decide in advance how long you will wait, so the decision does not depend on mood.

Keep the Enquiry Short and the Record Complete

The whole point of a short enquiry is to make replying easy. Aim for a message a support agent can read in under a minute: reference block, one timestamp, one question, one request for a photo or contents list if that would settle it. Sign off plainly.

Before sending, check three things. First, that the variant in your message matches the variant selected on the page. Second, that the timestamp points to the moment the information stops, not to a random part of the clip. Third, that your question can be answered with facts the seller holds, rather than an opinion about quality or suitability.

After sending, save your message with the reference block. If the listing changes, your saved copy shows what you saw and when. That record is useful whether you order, wait or move on.
