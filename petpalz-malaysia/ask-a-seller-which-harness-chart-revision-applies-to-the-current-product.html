Ask a Seller Which Harness Chart Revision Applies to the Current Product

Published by Petpalz Malaysia.

A buyer-side guide for Malaysian pet owners comparing third-party dog harness listings: how to ask which chart revision applies, compare revision and variant fields, record the answer beside the listing, and keep a screenshot for exchange decisions.

Why the Chart Revision Question Matters Before You Buy a Dog Harness

A harness listing often carries a size chart, and that chart is a document with a history. Sellers update charts when a manufacturer changes a measurement convention, adds a variant, or corrects an earlier figure. When you compare two listings, or return to one you saved weeks ago, the chart you are reading may not be the chart the seller used when the stock was described.

The practical question is not whether the chart is accurate in the abstract. It is whether the chart attached to the listing you are viewing is the revision that applies to the current product and the variant you intend to order. A chart can be perfectly reasonable and still be the wrong revision for the pack or model now being sold.

Separate the Revision Field From the Variant Field

Buyers often collapse two different questions into one message, and sellers answer only half of it. Keep them apart.

The revision field asks: which version of the size chart is published with this listing, and when was it last changed? The variant field asks: for the colour, model series or pack option I am selecting, does that same chart apply, or does the variant have its own figures?

A useful message names both. For example, a fictional buyer might write: "I am looking at the listing for model A in the sand colour. Can you confirm which revision of the size chart is shown on this page, and whether the same revision applies to the sand variant?" That phrasing gives the seller two clear items to answer rather than an open request for reassurance.

When you compare several sellers, note that a variant label is not a revision label. A listing that says "updated" in the title tells you nothing about which chart is attached. Ask for the revision as a separate answer, and record it separately. A Short Question Set to Send the Seller

You do not need a long message. Four questions, asked plainly, usually produce a usable answer.

First, which revision of the size chart is currently shown on this listing? Second, does that revision apply to the specific variant I have selected? Third, if the chart has changed recently, what changed between the previous revision and this one? Fourth, if I order now, which revision will the seller rely on if I later raise a question about the item?

The fourth question matters for exchange decisions. It invites the seller to state, in their own words, the basis on which they will discuss a mismatch later. You are not asking for a guarantee, and you should not read the answer as one. You are asking which document the seller considers current.

Keep the wording neutral. Avoid asking the seller to confirm the harness will fit your dog; that is a different conversation and one this page does not attempt. Stay with the chart revision and the variant, and let the seller answer in their own terms.

Record the Answer Beside the Listing, Not in Your Head

An answer that lives only in a chat thread is easy to lose. Build a small record next to the listing itself.

A workable pattern is a single note per listing with five lines: the listing reference or product name as the seller writes it, the variant you selected, the revision the seller named, the date and time you asked, and the seller's exact wording. Copy the wording rather than paraphrasing it, because a paraphrase drifts toward what you expected to hear.

If the seller replies with a screenshot of the chart, save that image with the note. If they reply with a link to a chart page, record the date you opened it. Charts change, and a dated record is what lets you say later that the answer you relied on was the answer given at that time.

This is a buyer-side habit, not a seller requirement, and no seller is obliged to keep the same record. The point is that your own file stays consistent even when the listing page is edited.

Keep a Screenshot for Exchange Decisions

A screenshot is the simplest way to preserve what a listing said at the moment you relied on it. Capture the listing page with the size chart visible, the variant selector showing your choice, and the date visible on your device if your screen allows it.

Store the screenshot with the note from the previous section, and name the file so you can find it later without opening it. A name that includes the listing reference and the date is enough; you do not need a filing system.

When an exchange question arises, the screenshot lets you compare three things side by side: what the listing showed, what the seller told you in writing, and what you received. That comparison is the useful part. It keeps the conversation on the record rather than on recollection.

Be careful not to overstate what a screenshot proves. It shows what a page displayed at a moment; it does not establish that the seller intended a particular outcome, and it does not decide an exchange. Treat it as evidence you can point to, and let the seller's stated terms govern the discussion.

Compare Two Listings Without Mixing Their Charts

When you compare two sellers, keep each listing's chart revision attached to that listing. The common mistake is to read seller A's chart, then apply its logic to seller B's variant, and conclude that one of them is wrong. They may simply be publishing different revisions.

A compact comparison works better than a long one. For each listing, record the seller name as shown, the variant, the revision named, the date checked, and whether the seller answered the variant question directly. Leave a column for open questions and fill it only with what is still unresolved.

If one seller does not answer, mark that as unanswered rather than assuming a revision. An unanswered question is a real finding about the listing, and it belongs in your record.

This comparison stays at the listing level. It does not tell you which harness suits a dog, and it should not be read as a ranking of sellers. Its only job is to keep revision and variant information from bleeding between listings.

A Worked Example and a Handoff Note

Consider a fictional case. A buyer in Malaysia saves a harness listing in February and notes that the chart is labelled revision 2. In April the buyer returns and sees the same listing with a chart labelled revision 3 and a new variant name. The buyer asks which revision applies to the new variant and receives a written reply naming revision 3 for that variant only.

The buyer records the reply, screenshots the listing, and keeps the February note rather than deleting it. If an exchange question arises later, the two notes show what changed and when, and the seller's written answer sits between them.

A short handoff note helps if someone else in the household completes the order. It can say which listing, which variant, which revision the seller confirmed, the date, and where the screenshot is stored. That is enough for another person to continue without re-asking.
