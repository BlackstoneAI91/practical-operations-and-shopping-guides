A Pet Food Listing Comparison Worksheet for Two Near-Identical Listings

Published by Petpalz Malaysia.

A practical one-page worksheet for Malaysian pet owners comparing two third-party pet food listings on pack size, ingredient panel wording, seller details and delivery terms, so the decision rests on facts rather than photos.

Why a Worksheet Beats a Side-by-Side Scroll

Two pet food listings for what looks like the same product rarely sit still long enough to compare properly. One tab shows a bag photographed at an angle, the other shows a different pack size with a similar name, and both carry a price that may or may not include delivery. Scrolling between them, you end up comparing the photos and the headline price rather than the details that decide whether the two offers are actually equivalent.

A worksheet fixes that by forcing both listings into the same fields, in the same order, filled in from each listing's own text. The aim is not to pick a winner in a hurry. It is to reach a point where you can say either "these are the same product on different terms" or "these are not the same product, and I now know why."

One working rule before you start: fill the worksheet from the listing text, not from memory or from the product photo. If a field cannot be filled from the listing, write "not stated" rather than guessing. A blank you can see is more useful than a plausible number you invented.

Setting Up the One-Page Grid

Keep the worksheet to a single page with two columns, one per listing, and a narrow notes column down the right. Label the columns by seller and listing reference rather than "A" and "B", so you can tell later which shop each figure came from. A simple header row works: field, listing one, listing two, notes.

The rows, in the order you should fill them:

Product identity: exact product name as written, variant or recipe name, pack size with unit, pack count, and any barcode or product code shown.

Ingredient and label wording: the feeding purpose or life-stage wording as printed, the first few ingredients in the order given, and any statement about whether the product is presented as complete or complementary.

Seller details: the seller or store name, whether the listing is on a marketplace or a store's own site, and what contact route the listing offers.

Price basis: the displayed price, the pack size it refers to, and whether delivery is included, excluded or unclear.

Delivery terms: stated coverage for your area, any cutoff time mentioned, and what the listing says happens if a delivery is missed.

Return terms: the window, the condition requirement, and who covers return postage, quoted from the listing's own wording.

Fill identity and label rows first. If those two rows already show the listings are different products, the price and delivery rows become a separate question and you can stop treating them as a like-for-like pair. That early exit saves time and prevents the most common error, which is comparing a price per bag against a price per kilogram without noticing.

Pack Size and Price Per Unit: The Row Most People Skip

Pack size is where near-identical listings diverge most often, and where the headline price misleads most reliably. A listing showing a lower total price may simply be a smaller bag. Before any price comparison, convert both listings to the same basis: price per kilogram, or price per litre for wet formats, using the pack size printed on each listing.

Work it through in the worksheet's notes column. Write the displayed price, the pack size, and the arithmetic in plain form, for example: displayed price divided by pack weight in kilograms equals price per kilogram. Do the same for the second listing. Only then compare the two results. If one listing is a multipack, divide by the total weight of all packs, not by the number of packs.

Two cautions belong on the worksheet itself. First, record the date you captured each figure, because listings change and a comparison is only valid for the moment it was made. Second, note the currency and whether the displayed amount includes any delivery charge, since a lower unit price can be overtaken by a delivery fee that the other listing folds in or waives above a threshold.

Reading the Ingredient Panel Wording Without Overreading It

The ingredient panel is the part of a listing most likely to be copied loosely, paraphrased in marketing language, or shown in a photo too small to read. Treat it as a transcription task, not an interpretation task. Copy the wording exactly as the listing presents it, including the order of the first few ingredients and any qualifier attached to them.

Three specific things to capture:

Feeding purpose and life stage. Record the exact phrase the listing uses, such as a life-stage label or a statement of intended feeding purpose. If the listing uses a marketing phrase instead of a label statement, note that the label wording was not shown.

Complete versus complementary. These are different claims and a listing may blur them. Write down which one the listing actually states, or "not stated" if neither appears.

Ingredient order. The order matters more than the presence of any single ingredient, so record the sequence rather than a highlight.

What the worksheet should not do is turn this into a nutrition judgement. Deciding whether a food suits a particular animal is a question for a veterinarian, and a listing comparison is the wrong tool for it. The worksheet's job is narrower: to establish whether the two listings describe the same formulation in the same words. If the wording differs, that is a finding worth noting, and it may mean the two listings are not the same product at all, whatever the photos suggest.

Where a listing's label wording is unclear or missing, record that as a gap and consider asking the seller directly before treating the listing as comparable.

Seller Identity, Contact Route and Delivery Terms

Two listings can show identical product details and still be different purchases, because the seller and the delivery terms differ. This section of the worksheet is about who you would actually be buying from and what happens after checkout.

Seller identity. Record the store name exactly as shown, and whether the listing sits on a marketplace alongside other sellers or on a single store's own site. Note whether the listing makes clear who is responsible for the sale.

Contact route. Note what contact options the listing offers, such as a message function or a published enquiry route. You do not need to record personal contact details in your worksheet; the point is simply whether a route exists and where it is described.

Delivery coverage and timing. Copy the stated coverage for your area and any cutoff time mentioned, rather than assuming a standard. If the listing is silent on your area, write "not stated" and treat delivery as unconfirmed.

Missed delivery. Record what the listing says happens if nobody is available to receive the parcel. This single row often separates two otherwise identical offers.

Return terms. Quote the window, the condition requirement and the postage responsibility from the listing's own policy text. A comparison of return policies across pet supply stores is a useful companion read if you want to apply the same rows across more than two sellers.

A Worked Example With Fictional Listings

The following example is invented to show the worksheet in use. None of the figures describe a real product or seller.

Suppose listing one shows a 3 kg bag at a displayed price, and listing two shows a 2 kg bag at a lower displayed price. Filled in, the identity rows show different pack sizes but the same recipe name. The label rows show the same life-stage wording and the same first three ingredients in the same order, so the products appear to be the same formulation in different sizes.

The price row settles it. Dividing each displayed price by its pack weight gives a price per kilogram for each. In this fictional case the larger bag works out cheaper per kilogram, but the smaller bag's listing states that delivery is included above a threshold the buyer would meet, while the larger bag's listing does not mention delivery at all. The notes column records that the delivery position for listing one is unconfirmed.

The seller rows show listing one on a marketplace and listing two on a single store's site, with different stated return windows. The missed-delivery row is blank for listing one and filled for listing two.

The worksheet has not declared a winner. It has produced three concrete findings: the products appear equivalent in formulation, the unit prices differ, and the delivery and return positions are not comparable as written. The next step is a question to the seller about delivery, not a purchase. That is the correct output of a comparison worksheet, and it is a more honest result than a ranked verdict.

Scoring the Worksheet and Deciding What to Do Next

A worksheet does not need a score, but a light scoring pass helps when you have filled several. The following is an author heuristic for illustration, not a validated method, and you should adjust the weights to suit your own priorities.

Give each listing one point for each of the following being clearly stated on the listing itself: exact pack size with unit, feeding purpose or life-stage wording, complete or complementary status, seller identity, delivery coverage for your area, missed-delivery handling, and return window with postage responsibility. That gives a maximum of seven points per listing.

Read the result carefully. A high score means the listing is well documented, not that the product is better. A low score is a prompt to ask the seller a question or to look elsewhere, not proof of a bad seller. Record the score alongside the date, because listings are updated and a score is only true for the day it was taken.

A short decision sequence to close the page:

If the identity rows differ, stop comparing and treat the listings as different products.

If the label wording differs, note the difference and do not assume equivalence.

If the unit prices differ but delivery is unclear on one side, resolve delivery before deciding.

If both listings are fully documented and equivalent, choose on the terms you actually value, such as delivery coverage or return window.

If either listing leaves key rows blank, ask the seller before committing.

Keep the completed worksheet. When you next compare two listings, whether for food or for something like a dog harness online, the same rows apply and the earlier sheet becomes a record of what you checked and when. That habit, more than any single comparison, is what makes repeat ordering calmer.
