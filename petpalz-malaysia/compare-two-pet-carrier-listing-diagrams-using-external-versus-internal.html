Compare Two Pet Carrier Listing Diagrams Using External Versus Internal Dimensions

Published by Petpalz Malaysia.

A buyer's guide to reading pet carrier listing diagrams, separating external dimensions from internal space, and recording what a third-party seller actually states before you order.

Why Two Diagrams Can Describe the Same Carrier Differently

A pet carrier listing usually shows a diagram with a handful of numbers: length, width, height, sometimes a folded depth or a handle height. The trouble is that two sellers can draw the same shape and label it with numbers that differ by several centimetres, because one is measuring the outside shell and the other is measuring the space inside. Neither diagram is wrong. They answer different questions.

External dimensions describe the object as it sits in a room, a car boot or a delivery box. They include the frame, the fabric bulge, the wheels, the handle and any trim that sticks out. Internal dimensions describe the compartment a pet would occupy, and they exclude walls, padding, liners and the space taken by a door panel or mesh window.

For a Malaysian buyer comparing third-party listings, the practical risk is mixing the two. You read a height of 30 cm on one listing, assume it matches a 30 cm figure on another, and later discover one was measured to the top of the shell and the other to the top of the interior floor. This guide is about reading the diagrams carefully, not about deciding whether a carrier suits a particular animal or a particular journey. Build a Two-Column Reading Sheet Before You Compare

The simplest way to stop mixing conventions is to force every listing into the same two columns. Make a sheet with a row for each candidate carrier and two groups of columns: one headed External, one headed Internal. Under each, leave blank cells for length, width and height, plus a cell for the unit and a cell for the source of the number.

Then read each diagram once, slowly, and fill only what the seller actually states. If a listing shows a single set of three numbers with no label, write "unlabelled" in the source cell rather than guessing which convention applies. If the diagram shows arrows that stop at the outer edge of the shell, that is external. If the arrows sit inside a cutaway view, that is likely internal, but confirm from the caption.

A worked fictional example helps. Suppose Listing A shows 50 x 32 x 30 cm with arrows running along the outside of a soft-sided bag. Listing B shows 44 x 28 x 27 cm inside a cutaway drawing with a shaded floor. On the sheet, A goes in the External columns and B in the Internal columns. They are not yet comparable, and the sheet makes that visible before you spend time on price or delivery terms.

Keep the sheet short. Three carriers and six numbers each is enough for most household decisions, and a small table is easier to re-check than a page of notes.

Reading the Diagram Itself: Arrows, Cutaways and Captions

Diagrams carry meaning in their drawing style, not only in their numbers. A few conventions are worth recognising when you scan a listing.

Solid outline with arrows outside the shape. This is the most common external measurement. The arrows usually run from the widest point to the widest point, which may include a bulge in soft fabric rather than a rigid frame.

Cutaway or ghosted view with arrows inside. The seller is showing the compartment. Check whether the floor is drawn flat or whether a padded base is included in the height.

Dashed lines. These often mark a folded or collapsed state, or a removable section. Read the caption before assuming they describe usable space.

Multiple views. A side view and a front view may use different scales. Do not measure one against the other on screen.

Captions matter more than the picture. A caption that says "approximate outer dimensions" tells you the convention; a caption that says nothing leaves the question open. When the caption is missing, note it as an open question for the seller rather than filling the gap yourself. This is the same discipline you would apply to any pet supplies listing where the seller's wording is the only evidence available.

Where the Two Conventions Change the Shopping Decision

Once external and internal figures are separated, the comparison usually shifts in one of three ways.

First, storage and transport planning. External dimensions tell you whether a carrier fits a shelf, a cupboard or the space behind a seat. Internal dimensions tell you nothing about that, so a carrier with generous internal space can still be awkward to store.

Second, like-for-like comparison between sellers. Two listings that look similar in a photo can differ by several centimetres once you align the conventions. A carrier quoted at 50 cm externally and one quoted at 50 cm internally are not the same size, and the difference is usually the wall and padding thickness.

Third, the questions you send the seller. If a listing gives only external figures, the useful question is whether internal dimensions are available. If it gives only internal figures, ask for the outer footprint. A short, specific message is easier to answer than a general request for "more info".

None of this tells you whether a carrier is suitable for a particular animal or a particular trip. It tells you whether you are comparing the same kind of number, which is the part a buyer can control from a listing page.

A Small Decision Worksheet for Two Listings

Use this worksheet when you have narrowed the field to two carriers. It is a reading aid, not a scoring system, and the thresholds below are author heuristics rather than research findings.

Step 1. Copy the exact numbers from each diagram, with units, into the External and Internal columns. Do not convert or round yet.

Step 2. Mark each number as stated, derived or unknown. Derived means you calculated it, for example by subtracting a stated wall thickness. Unknown means the listing is silent.

Step 3. Count the unknowns per listing. As a rough guide, a listing with more than two unknowns in the dimensions it does state is worth a seller question before you go further.

Step 4. Compare only matching conventions. External against external, internal against internal. If one listing has no internal figures, write "not comparable" rather than substituting the external number.

Step 5. Record the date you read the listing and the variant you looked at. Sellers edit pages, and a note with a date is easier to trust later than a memory.

Step 6. Keep the sheet with your order records so that a delivery query can be checked against what the listing said at the time.

Delivery, Returns and What to Confirm With the Seller

Dimensions affect the practical side of an order as well as the comparison. A larger external footprint can change parcel size, which in turn can affect delivery arrangements and, in some cases, whether a courier will handle the item at all. Read the seller's delivery notes for stated size or weight limits rather than assuming a standard parcel service applies.

Returns are the other place where dimensions matter. If a carrier arrives and the outer size differs from the diagram, the useful evidence is the listing as it appeared when you ordered, plus your reading sheet. Photograph the packaging and the item against a tape measure if you need to raise a query, and keep the correspondence specific: which figure, which convention, which date.

Before ordering, confirm three things in writing where possible: the variant you selected, the dimensions as the seller states them, and the return window that applies to that variant. Terms differ between sellers and platforms, so check the current wording rather than relying on a previous order.

Common Reading Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

A short list of habits that cause most dimension mix-ups.

Assuming the first number is length. Some diagrams lead with height, especially for upright carriers. Read the axis labels, not the order.

Treating a photo as a scale drawing. Perspective and lens choice distort proportions. Use the stated numbers, not a visual estimate.

Averaging two listings. If one gives external and the other internal figures, an average is meaningless. Keep them in separate columns.

Ignoring the unit. A listing that mixes centimetres and inches without labelling is a red flag for a seller question.

Forgetting the variant. A listing with several sizes may show one diagram for the range. Check which size the numbers belong to.

Over-reading a caption. "Roomy interior" is marketing language, not a measurement. Note it as a description, not a figure.

The overall discipline is simple: separate what the seller states from what you infer, and keep the two visibly apart on the page. That habit carries over to any online pet store listing where dimensions, pack sizes or included parts decide whether a comparison is fair. When a figure is missing, the honest entry is a blank and a question, not a guess.
