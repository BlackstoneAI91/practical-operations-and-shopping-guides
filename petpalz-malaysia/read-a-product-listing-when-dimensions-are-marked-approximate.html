Reading a Product Listing When Dimensions Are Marked Approximate

Published by Petpalz Malaysia.

A practical guide for Malaysian pet owners comparing third-party listings: what an approximate dimension means, what to measure at home, and how to record a tolerance question without treating it as a fit guarantee.

What the word approximate is actually telling you

When a listing says a dimension is approximate, the seller is signalling that the figure is a guide rather than a measured commitment. That single word changes how you should use the number. A stated 40 cm is a point; an approximate 40 cm is a range with no published edges. The listing rarely tells you how wide that range is, whether it covers manufacturing variation, how the item was measured, or whether the figure refers to the outside of the product or the space inside it.

For a buyer, the practical response is not to distrust the number but to stop treating it as a fit decision on its own. Approximate figures are still useful for sorting: they let you rule out an item that is clearly far too small or far too large. They are weak for confirming a close call. If your own measurement sits within a centimetre or two of the stated figure, the listing has not answered your question, and no amount of re-reading will change that.

This matters across pet care products generally, because the same convention appears on carriers, bowls, mats, harnesses and storage items. Treat the qualifier as a prompt to gather one more piece of evidence, not as a reason to abandon the comparison. Measure the space, not just the product

The most common comparison error is measuring the product you already own and comparing it to the new listing. That tells you how two objects differ, not whether the new one suits your situation. Measure the constraint instead: the shelf gap, the cupboard depth, the floor area you are willing to give up, the internal space of the bag you carry. Write that number down before you open the listing again.

Then decide which of the seller's figures corresponds to your constraint. A carrier listing may give external length and internal length; a bowl listing may give outer diameter and stated capacity; a folded accessory may give packed dimensions only. If the listing gives one figure and you need the other, you have found a genuine gap rather than a size mismatch.

A simple home worksheet, offered here as an author heuristic rather than a tested method:

1. Record the constraint in centimetres, with the date you measured it. 2. Record each dimension the listing states, and label it external, internal, folded or assembled. 3. Mark any dimension you need but the listing does not state. 4. Note whether the figure carries an approximate qualifier. 5. Leave the fit column blank until the seller answers.

Keeping the constraint and the listing figures in separate columns stops you from quietly assuming the two numbers describe the same thing.

Compare listings on the same basis before you choose

Approximate figures become far more useful when you compare several listings using one consistent record. If one seller quotes internal width and another quotes external width, the numbers are not comparable, and a table that places them side by side will mislead you. Build the comparison so each row states what was measured and by whom.

A workable record for a pet supplies comparison has a column for the seller's exact wording, a column for the dimension type, and a column for whether the figure is marked approximate. Add the date you checked, because listings change. When you later revisit a shortlist, you can see whether a difference comes from the product or from a revised listing.

This is also where an online pet store listing earns or loses trust: a seller who labels dimensions clearly, states the measurement basis and separates packed from assembled sizes gives you something to compare. A seller who prints one unlabelled number does not. Neither is proof of quality, but the first is easier to work with.

Keep the shortlist short. Three or four candidates with well-labelled figures will serve you better than a dozen rows of numbers you cannot interpret.

Write a tolerance question the seller can actually answer

Once you know which figure is missing or approximate, the next step is a question, not a purchase. A good question is specific, quotes the listing, and asks for one thing. A poor question invites a yes or no that protects the seller and tells you nothing.

Weak: Will this fit my dog? Stronger: The listing states an approximate internal length of 45 cm. Can you confirm the internal length in centimetres for this variant, and whether the figure includes the frame?

A useful structure, again an author heuristic rather than a standard:

- Name the listing and variant you are asking about. - Quote the approximate figure exactly as printed. - State the measurement basis you need. - Ask for a number, not an assurance. - Ask whether the answer applies to the variant you selected.

Record the reply next to your worksheet, with the date. If the seller answers with a range, note the range. If the seller answers with a promise that it will fit, note that too, because a promise is not a measurement. Neither outcome is a guarantee, and you should not treat a seller's reassurance as confirmation that the item suits your pet or your home.

Keep the record neutral and separate from the decision

A tolerance record is most useful when it stays factual. Write what the listing said, what you asked, what came back and when. Avoid adding conclusions such as the seller is unreliable or the product is misdescribed. Those judgements belong to a later decision, and mixing them into the record makes it harder to compare sellers fairly.

Store the record where you will find it again: alongside the saved listing, the order reference and any correspondence. If you later need to discuss an exchange, a dated note showing the approximate figure, your question and the reply is more useful than a recollection. Keep personal details out of any note you might share, and keep the seller's wording intact rather than paraphrasing it into something stronger.

This habit also protects you from your own earlier assumptions. A note written before checkout is a check on what you believed at the time, and it makes it obvious whether a later disappointment came from a changed listing or from a gap you had already identified and chosen to accept.

Where approximate dimensions sit in the buying journey

Approximate dimensions are one field among many, and they should not crowd out the others. A listing comparison that fixes the size question but ignores pack size, variant, seller identity and delivery terms is only partly done. The affiliate journey described in the brand's own foundation material runs from reading content, to checking a third-party product offer, to buying from the merchant identified at checkout, so the seller and the variant are part of the decision, not an afterthought.

When you are ready to move from comparison to a shortlist, the shop section is a reasonable place to see how listings are presented and what each one states. Check the current offer and seller details before relying on any figure, and treat the approximate qualifier as an open question until the seller answers it in writing.

A closing checklist, offered as an example rather than a scored test:

- Constraint measured and dated. - Listing figures labelled by measurement basis. - Approximate qualifiers flagged, not smoothed over. - One specific question sent and its reply recorded. - Seller, variant and delivery terms noted separately.

If any line is blank, the comparison is not finished, and that is a useful thing to know before you order.
