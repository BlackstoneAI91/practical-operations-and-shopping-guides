Checking Dog Harness Listings When the Product Photos and Sizing Chart Disagree

Published by Petpalz Malaysia.

A practical guide for Malaysian dog owners comparing third-party harness listings: how to read the manufacturer sizing chart, spot when photos show a different variant, what to ask the seller, and when to skip a listing entirely.

Why Photos and Sizing Charts Drift Apart

A harness listing is assembled from several pieces of information that may not have been updated together. The sizing chart is often copied from a manufacturer's document, while the photos may come from a brand shoot, a previous season's colourway, or a different size in the same range. When the two disagree, neither is automatically wrong; they may simply describe different things.

Your job as a buyer is not to decide which one is 'true'. It is to work out which specific product variant the seller will actually ship, and whether that variant matches the dog in front of you. Treat the listing as a set of claims to reconcile, not a single description.

Read the Sizing Chart as a Measurement Document

Start with the chart itself and ignore the photos for a moment. A usable harness chart should tell you which body measurement it refers to, the unit, and the range for each size. Common reference points are neck circumference and chest or girth circumference, measured around the widest part of the ribcage. Some charts also list weight, but weight is a weak substitute for a tape measurement because two dogs of the same weight can have very different chest depths.

Check three things before you trust a chart:

First, confirm the unit. A chart in centimetres and a tape in inches will produce a confident, wrong decision. Second, confirm whether the ranges are inclusive and whether there is a gap between sizes. A chart that jumps from 40 to 45 centimetres leaves a dog measuring 42 in an ambiguous position. Third, confirm whether the measurement is taken on the dog or on the harness laid flat. Those are different numbers, and mixing them is one of the most common sources of a poor fit.

If the chart has no unit, no measurement label, or no size ranges at all, you do not have a sizing chart. You have a picture of a table.

A Fictional Measurement Example

Suppose a Malaysian owner has a medium-sized mixed-breed dog. Measured with a soft tape at the deepest part of the chest, just behind the front legs, the dog reads 58 centimetres. The neck measures 34 centimetres.

The listing's chart shows three sizes: Small at 30 to 40 centimetres chest, Medium at 40 to 52 centimetres, and Large at 52 to 64 centimetres. On paper, the dog sits inside Large, close to the lower end. That is a reasonable starting point, but it is not the end of the check.

The photos, however, show a harness with a wide padded chest plate and a label reading 'M' on the strap. The chart says Large. The photo says Medium. This is exactly the disagreement this guide is about. The owner should not guess. They should ask the seller which size the photographed item is, whether the photo is of the exact variant being sold, and whether the chart applies to that same model. Until those answers arrive, the listing is unresolved.

A second useful check is the adjustment range. If the Large harness adjusts from 52 to 64 centimetres, a 58-centimetre dog has room in both directions. If it adjusts from 56 to 60, the fit is tight and unforgiving. Record the adjustment range alongside the size name.

Deciding Whether the Photos Show a Different Variant

Photos can mislead in ways that are easy to miss. Look for these signals that the image may not represent the item you will receive:

Colour and trim differences between the main photo and the thumbnail gallery often indicate mixed stock from different seasons. A visible size tag or letter on a strap that contradicts the chart is a direct conflict. Hardware differences, such as plastic versus metal buckles, matter because they change weight and durability. A photo showing a handle, a front clip, or reflective stitching that the description never mentions may belong to a different model in the same range.

Also check whether the photos show the harness on a dog or flat on a surface. On-dog photos are useful for seeing proportion but poor for measurement, because the dog's size is unknown. Flat photos are better for counting straps and checking buckle placement.

If the photos and the chart describe different variants, the listing has an unresolved conflict. You can still proceed, but only after the seller confirms in writing which variant is being sold.

What to Ask the Seller Before You Commit

A short, specific message resolves most disagreements faster than browsing more listings. Ask only what changes your decision:

Which exact size and colour will be shipped for this listing? Does the sizing chart on the page apply to that exact model? Can you confirm the chest measurement range in centimetres? Is the item in the photos the same variant as the one in stock? What is the return or exchange position if the measured size does not fit?

Keep the answers. A written reply that names the size and model is far more useful than a phone call you cannot refer back to. If the seller answers vaguely, or replies with a generic 'please refer to the chart' when the chart is the thing in doubt, treat that as a signal about how a return query will be handled later.

When to Skip a Listing Entirely

Some listings are not worth the effort of resolving. Skip a listing when any of the following is true:

The chart has no units or no measurement labels and the seller will not clarify. The photos clearly show a different model from the description and the seller cannot confirm which is sold. The size range has a gap that your dog falls into and no adjustment range is published. The listing gives no return or exchange information at all, which makes a sizing error expensive. The seller cannot name the model or manufacturer, which makes it impossible to check the original sizing document.

None of these are automatic proof of a bad seller. They are simply conditions under which you cannot make an informed decision from the page, and an unresolved listing is a poor basis for a purchase.

A Short Checklist You Can Reuse

Before you order any harness from a third-party listing, run through this sequence. It is an author heuristic, not a tested scoring system, and you should adapt it to your own dog.

One: measure the dog's chest and neck with a soft tape and write the numbers down with the unit. Two: read the chart and confirm the unit, the measurement label, and whether ranges are inclusive. Three: note the adjustment range, not just the size name. Four: compare the photos against the chart and flag any conflict in size tag, hardware, or features. Five: message the seller with the specific questions above and keep the reply. Six: check the return or exchange position before paying. Seven: if any step remains unresolved, skip the listing.

A worked example of the whole sequence: a dog measuring 58 centimetres chest, a chart offering Large at 52 to 64 centimetres, a photo showing a Medium tag, and a seller who confirms in writing that the shipped item is Large and the chart applies. That listing is resolved and can be compared on equal terms with others.
