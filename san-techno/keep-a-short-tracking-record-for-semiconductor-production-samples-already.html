Keeping a Short Tracking Record for Semiconductor Production Samples Already Sent for Review

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building a compact, auditable tracking record for semiconductor materials samples already sent for review, using placeholders for issue date, ownership and next confirmation.

Why a short record beats a long memory

Once a sample leaves your desk for review, the paperwork that matters is rarely the technical file. It is the small set of facts that lets a colleague answer three questions without calling you: what was sent, who holds it now, and when someone last confirmed its status. A short tracking record is not a second specification. It is a pointer to the documents that already exist, plus the administrative details that keep those documents findable.

This matters in procurement because review cycles cross team boundaries. A sample of semiconductor materials may be logged by purchasing, acknowledged by a receiving desk, and then referenced by a colleague preparing a later comparison. If each of those people keeps a private note, the shared picture drifts. A single short record, kept deliberately minimal, keeps the picture aligned without duplicating confidential technical content.

The discipline is to record only what you can support. If a date came from a supplier message, say so. If an owner was assigned verbally, mark it as provisional until confirmed in writing. The record should never imply that a review has progressed further than the evidence shows. Treat it as an index card, not a verdict.

The six fields that earn their place

A workable short record usually needs no more than six fields. First, a local reference you control, so the record can be found without relying on a supplier's numbering. Second, a short description of what was sent, phrased neutrally and without reproducing specification detail. Third, the date the sample was issued, with a placeholder if the exact date is not yet confirmed. Fourth, the current holder or owning role. Fifth, the next confirmation date, again as a placeholder until agreed. Sixth, a status word drawn from a tiny fixed list, such as sent, acknowledged, awaiting confirmation, or closed.

Everything else is optional and should be added only when a reader genuinely needs it. A free-text notes column tends to fill with speculation, so keep it for factual pointers such as the name of the file where the original message is stored. Avoid pasting technical values into the tracker; those belong in the controlled documents that already govern them.

A useful test before adding a column: would a colleague who has never seen this sample need this field to find the right document or the right person? If the answer is no, leave it out. Short records stay useful because they stay short, and because every field has an obvious owner.

Placeholders for issue date, ownership and next confirmation

Placeholders are not a sign of sloppiness. They are an honest way to show that a fact is expected but not yet established. Write them in a consistent form so they are easy to search, for example a bracketed label followed by the role expected to fill it. That way nobody mistakes a blank for a confirmed value.

For the issue date, distinguish between the date the sample physically left and the date the record was created. They are often different, and conflating them creates confusion later. For ownership, name a role rather than a person where possible, so the record survives staff changes. For the next confirmation, set a placeholder that names who will confirm and by when, and mark it clearly as proposed until the owner agrees.

When a placeholder is filled, keep a brief note of the source, such as a message reference or a file name. This is the same principle that applies when recording which party supplied a date on a materials confirmation: the value is only as good as its provenance. If a placeholder lingers past its proposed date, that is itself useful information. It tells the reader the record needs attention, without anyone having to guess.

Separating the tracker from the technical file

The tracker's job is to point, not to explain. Technical documents, supplier correspondence and any controlled records stay where they are, under whatever access rules already apply. The tracker holds a reference to them and nothing more. This separation protects confidentiality and keeps the tracker readable by people who do not need the underlying detail.

San Techno's public materials note that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential. That is a useful reminder of a general principle: some information simply does not belong in a shared administrative log, however convenient it might seem. Apply the same restraint to your own files. If a colleague needs the technical content, route them to the controlled document rather than copying it into the tracker.

A practical habit is to give each tracker row a single pointer field, such as a file name or a folder reference, and to forbid pasting content into other columns. When a document is revised, update the pointer and note the revision date, rather than duplicating the new content. Readers who want to understand the range of materials a supplier works with can consult public product information, while the tracker stays focused on the administrative question of where a specific sample stands.

A worked example using fictional references

Suppose a procurement team in Singapore sends a sample for internal review and needs a record that survives a handover. They create a row with a local reference such as ST-SAMPLE-014, a neutral description, an issue date placeholder marked as awaiting confirmation from the dispatch desk, an owner role of procurement coordinator, a next confirmation placeholder naming the receiving team, and a status of sent.

Two weeks later, a colleague takes over. They open the tracker, see the placeholder is still unfilled, and know exactly whom to ask. They do not need to reconstruct the history from a mailbox. When the receiving team confirms, the colleague fills the date, notes the message reference, and changes the status to acknowledged. The technical file is untouched throughout.

This example is fictional and exists only to show the shape of the record. The value is in the discipline: one row, six fields, clear placeholders, and a pointer to the real documents. Teams that keep this shape find it easier to answer questions at short notice, because the record answers the administrative question and defers the technical one to the people who own it.

Reviewing the record without over-claiming

A short record needs a light review rhythm, not a heavy audit. A weekly glance at open rows is usually enough to spot placeholders that have aged past their proposed date. The reviewer's task is administrative: confirm the owner is still correct, check that the pointer still resolves, and note any status that has not moved. They are not there to judge the sample itself.

Be careful with language in the review notes. Phrases like approved, accepted or qualified carry weight and should not appear unless a properly authorised person has said so in writing. Prefer neutral wording such as confirmation received or awaiting response. This keeps the tracker honest and avoids implying that a technical judgement has been made when only an administrative step has occurred.

When a row closes, keep it rather than deleting it, and mark it clearly as closed with the closing date. Closed rows are useful for answering later questions about what was sent and when. A one-page worksheet you can copy

Before you close this page, draft the worksheet your team will actually use. Start with the six fields described earlier and add a short header stating the record's purpose and its owner role. Add a line explaining that placeholders must name the role expected to fill them. Add a second line stating that technical content belongs in controlled documents, not in the tracker.

Next, write three example rows using fictional references, covering a newly sent sample, one awaiting confirmation, and one closed. This gives new team members a model to follow and makes the conventions visible. Finally, agree a light review cadence and note who performs it. Keep the whole worksheet to a single page so it can be read at a glance during a handover.

As a closing check, ask whether a colleague unfamiliar with the sample could use the record to find the right document and the right person without asking you. If yes, the record is doing its job. If not, the fix is usually fewer fields and clearer placeholders, not more detail.
