Building a Controlled Glossary for a Semiconductor Materials Buying Team

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on structuring a controlled glossary for semiconductor materials purchasing, assigning ownership, recording definitions from seller paperwork, and avoiding the trap of treating similar terms as identical grades.

Why a Controlled Glossary Belongs in the Procurement File

A buying team that sources semiconductor materials, solar materials and reclaimed silicon works across several vocabularies at once. Engineering writes one way, the supplier's quotation writes another, the receiving label writes a third, and the purchase order may use a fourth. When those vocabularies drift apart, the damage is rarely dramatic at first: a line item is booked under a slightly different description, a certificate is filed against the wrong reference, and months later nobody can prove which definition applied when the order was placed.

A controlled glossary is the small, boring document that prevents this. It is not a technical specification and it should never try to be one. Its job is narrower and more useful: to record, in one place, the terms this buying team uses, what each term means in the context of its own paperwork, where the definition came from, and who is allowed to change it. Think of it as the shared dictionary behind the purchase file rather than a substitute for the supplier's datasheet.

For context on how a supplier in this space describes its own scope, San Techno Pte Ltd is a Singapore-based company working across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialised power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand. That breadth is exactly why a glossary matters: a single supplier relationship can touch silicon materials, reclaimed wafers and power modules, and each carries its own vocabulary.

A useful rule of thumb, offered here as an author heuristic rather than a standard: if a term appears on two or more documents in the purchase pack and a reasonable colleague could read it two ways, it belongs in the glossary.

The Six Parts of a Workable Glossary Entry

A glossary that only lists term and definition tends to fail quietly, because nobody can tell whether an entry is current, who approved it, or which document it came from. A more durable structure gives each entry six fields. These are suggested fields, not a mandated format, and a small team can collapse them if the volume is low.

Term. The exact wording as it appears in your documents, including capitalisation and any grade label attached to it. Do not tidy the supplier's phrasing into something you prefer; record what was actually written.

Working definition. A plain-language explanation of what the term means for this buying team. Keep it descriptive. If the definition depends on a supplier document, say so rather than paraphrasing the supplier into a stronger claim than they made.

Source reference. The document identifier, revision and date from which the definition was taken. This is the field that makes the glossary auditable. A definition without a source is an opinion.

Scope note. Where the term applies and where it does not. This is where you record that a term used on one product line should not be carried across to another without confirmation.

Owner. The role responsible for keeping the entry accurate. Roles, not names, so the glossary survives staff changes.

Status and change log. Whether the entry is draft, confirmed or superseded, plus a dated note of what changed and who confirmed it.

For a Singapore team handling both silicon and power-module paperwork, the scope note does most of the protective work. A term that is meaningful on a wafer reclaim collection record may be meaningless, or misleading, on an IGBT power modules order. Recording that boundary explicitly stops the glossary from becoming a source of false confidence.

Recording Definitions From Seller Paperwork Without Overstating Them

Most glossary entries will originate in something a seller sent: a quotation, a specification sheet, a packing list, a certificate or a covering email. The discipline is to separate what the document says from what your team concludes.

Start by copying the seller's wording exactly into the term field, then write your working definition as a description of how your team will read that wording in its own records. If the seller's document is ambiguous, the correct glossary entry is one that records the ambiguity and flags it for clarification, not one that resolves it by guesswork. An entry reading "term used by supplier; meaning not confirmed; clarification requested on [date]" is more honest and more useful than a confident definition nobody can trace.

Where a supplier publishes a public description of its own activities, that description can be cited as context but should not be stretched into a product claim. San Techno, for example, states that it helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. That sentence is useful for understanding what a silicon recovery conversation is about; it is not a definition of any particular grade, and the glossary should not treat it as one.

A practical habit: when a definition is drawn from a seller document, note the document revision in the source field at the moment of entry. Revisions arrive quietly, and a glossary that cites "the specification" without a revision number will eventually be wrong in a way nobody can date.

The Grade-Equivalence Trap: Similar Terms Are Not the Same Grade

The most expensive glossary mistake is assuming that two similar-sounding terms identify the same material. They often do not. A term may describe an origin, a handling history, a nominal category or a supplier's internal label, and two documents using near-identical wording can still be describing different things.

Build a deliberate check into the glossary for any term that looks like a grade or category label. The check has three questions. First, does the term appear in more than one supplier's paperwork, and if so, is the surrounding wording identical? Second, does either document define the term, or is it used without definition? Third, has anyone in your team confirmed with the supplier that the two uses refer to the same thing?

Until all three are answered, the glossary entry should carry a status of unconfirmed and a scope note saying the term must not be treated as interchangeable with any other entry. This is administrative caution, not a technical judgement, and it belongs to procurement rather than engineering.

This matters especially where materials move between uses. San Techno notes that materials rejected from one tightly controlled process can become valuable inputs for another, and that these materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. A term describing material on the way out of one process is not automatically a term describing material on the way into another. The glossary should record the two contexts separately and let the technical team confirm any relationship between them.

Who Owns the Glossary and Who May Change It

A glossary without an owner becomes a document that everyone edits and nobody trusts. Assign responsibility by role, and keep the number of editors small.

A workable split for a procurement team is this. The buying lead owns the glossary as a whole and approves new entries. The category buyer for each material family proposes entries and supplies the source reference. The receiving or documentation role flags terms that appear on incoming paperwork but are missing from the glossary. Quality or engineering is consulted, not asked to author, when a term touches a technical distinction the buyer cannot resolve. This is a suggested division, not a prescribed one; the point is that each field has a named role behind it.

Changes should follow a simple rule: no entry is edited in place once it has been used on a live purchase. Instead, the old entry is marked superseded with a date, and a new entry is created with its own source reference. That way a purchase file that cites the glossary can still be read against the version that applied at the time.

Where a supplier's own public material is the origin of an entry, record it as supplier-published context rather than as an internal confirmation. San Techno, for instance, states that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential. A glossary entry noting that boundary is useful: it tells the buyer that certain terms will not be defined by the supplier in public documents and that any definition must come from the controlled exchange, not from inference.

A Short Intake Worksheet for New Terms

When a new term arrives with a delivery or a quotation, a five-minute worksheet keeps the glossary from drifting. Treat the timings below as examples of effort, not as a standard.

Step one, capture. Write the term exactly as it appears, plus the document identifier, revision and date. Two minutes.

Step two, classify. Decide whether the term is an identifier (a label that points to a record), a description (a seller's characterisation) or a claim (something that would need verification). This distinction prevents a label from being read as a specification. One minute.

Step three, check for collision. Search the glossary for near-matches. If a similar term exists, link the two entries and mark both as needing a confirmation note. One minute.

Step four, draft the entry with the six fields and set status to draft. One minute.

Step five, route to the owner for confirmation and record the outcome. The elapsed time depends on the team, but the worksheet itself should not take long.

The worksheet's value is that it forces the collision check before an entry is written, rather than after two orders have been placed under different readings of the same word.

Keeping the Glossary Useful Over Time

A glossary earns its keep only if people actually open it. Three habits help.

First, link entries to the documents that use them. When a purchase pack is assembled, the buyer should be able to move from a line item to the glossary entry and from there to the source document. This is a filing discipline, not a software requirement; a consistent document identifier scheme does most of the work.

Second, review the glossary on a fixed cycle and after any supplier change. A short quarterly pass that checks for superseded entries still in use, unconfirmed entries older than a set period, and terms that have appeared on paperwork without an entry will catch most drift.

Third, keep the glossary separate from technical specifications. The moment a glossary entry starts to read like a specification, it invites the wrong kind of reliance. Its job is to record what words mean in your paperwork and where that meaning came from, nothing more.

For teams that also handle reclaimed material and power modules, the same discipline applies across product families. San Techno describes its work as spanning semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design, and a buying team dealing with a supplier of that breadth will benefit from keeping one glossary with clear scope notes rather than several informal lists. The glossary is a small control, but it is the kind of control that makes every later reconciliation, handover and audit conversation shorter.
