Create a supplier reply template that separates available facts from assumptions

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building a supplier reply template that clearly separates confirmed documents, stated constraints and unanswered questions when sourcing semiconductor materials and related products.

Why a supplier reply needs three visible layers

A supplier reply often arrives as a single block of prose. A salesperson confirms one thing, hedges on another, and leaves a third question untouched, all in the same paragraph. When that reply is forwarded internally, the layers blur. A colleague reads a stated constraint as a confirmed fact, or treats an unanswered question as a silent yes.

The fix is structural rather than linguistic. Before you send a request, decide that every reply will be sorted into three layers: confirmed documents, stated constraints, and unanswered questions. Each layer answers a different question. Confirmed documents answer "what can we point to?" Stated constraints answer "what has the supplier told us about limits?" Unanswered questions answer "what still needs a decision or a follow-up?"

This matters most when the material category is broad. A request that touches semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon or wafer reclaim can attract replies from different parts of a supplier organisation, and each part may hold a different slice of the picture. Keeping the three layers separate stops one team's partial answer from being read as the whole company's position.

A useful habit is to treat the template as a receiving form, not a marketing document. You are not asking the supplier to sell; you are asking them to place each statement in the correct box. That single design choice makes the reply easier to file, easier to challenge and easier to hand to whoever owns the next step.

The short fictional request that sets the pattern

Here is a fictional request a Singapore procurement team might send. It is deliberately short, and it names the three layers up front so the supplier knows what shape of answer is wanted.

"We are reviewing a possible purchase of silicon wafers and related semiconductor materials. Please reply using three headings. Under Confirmed documents, list the documents you can provide and the revision or date of each. Under Stated constraints, tell us any limits on quantity, timing, packaging or handling that you want us to record. Under Unanswered questions, list anything you cannot answer now and say who will answer it. Please do not combine these into one paragraph."

Notice what the request does not do. It does not ask the supplier to describe capabilities in general terms. It does not invite a brochure. It asks for a structured reply that a buyer can compare against other replies without rewriting anything.

A fictional reply might look like this. Confirmed documents: a product data sheet dated in a stated month, and a packing list template. Stated constraints: a minimum order quantity the supplier describes as fixed for the current period, and a note that some items are quoted per case rather than per unit. Unanswered questions: whether a certificate can be issued for a specific lot, and which internal team confirms it.

That reply is modest, and that is the point. It contains no performance promises, no claims about how the material will behave, and no suggestion that the supplier has been audited by anyone. It simply places statements where they can be reviewed.

Marking confirmed documents without overstating them

The confirmed-documents layer is where buyers most often overreach. A supplier sends a data sheet, and the buyer records "specification confirmed." But a data sheet confirms that a document exists and what it says. It does not confirm that the material you receive will match it, and it does not confirm that the document is current.

A safer marking convention uses three fields for each document: what the document is, its revision or date, and what it is being used to support. For example: "Data sheet, revision as stated on the file, used to support the grade description in our internal record." That sentence is honest about the document's role without turning it into a guarantee.

When the supplier is a company with a public product page, you can also note the relationship between the reply and the public material. San Techno, for instance, presents silicon wafer reclaim services and semiconductor silicon materials among its public offerings, and its products page is a reasonable place to check how the company describes its own scope. That check helps you phrase a question, but it does not replace the supplier's written reply. A public page tells you what a company says it does; a reply tells you what it will confirm for your specific request.

Keep the confirmed-documents layer free of interpretation. If a document needs engineering review before it means anything to your team, say so in the unanswered-questions layer rather than implying the document settles the matter.

Recording stated constraints as statements, not conclusions

A stated constraint is something the supplier has told you about a limit, and it should be recorded in the supplier's terms, not yours. If a supplier says a lead time is "typically four to six weeks from confirmation," write that down as a stated constraint with the word "typically" intact. Do not convert it into "four weeks" in your own notes.

Constraints commonly appear in three areas: quantity, timing and handling. Quantity constraints include minimum order quantities and pack sizes. Timing constraints include lead times and quote validity. Handling constraints include packaging formats and any conditions the supplier attaches to storage or transport. Each one belongs in the constraints layer with a note on who stated it and when.

A useful discipline is to add a column headed "basis." For each constraint, record whether it came from the supplier's written reply, from a verbal comment you have since confirmed in writing, or from a document. Constraints that rest only on a verbal comment should be flagged for written confirmation before they influence a decision.

This is also where you keep manufacturer claims modest. If a supplier describes a product as suitable for a particular use, record that as the supplier's statement. Do not upgrade it to a finding of your own. The template's job is to preserve the difference between what was said and what was concluded.

Turning unanswered questions into an owned list

The unanswered-questions layer is the most valuable part of the template, and the easiest to neglect. A reply that leaves a question blank is not a failed reply; it is a reply that has told you where the gaps are. The template should capture each gap as a discrete item with an owner and a next step.

A simple structure works. Each question gets an identifier, the exact wording you sent, the supplier's response (including "no response"), the internal owner of the follow-up, and a target date for a further reply. Keep the original wording. If you paraphrase the question later, you lose the ability to show what was actually asked.

Group the questions by the kind of answer needed. Some questions need a document, such as a certificate or a packing list. Some need a commercial decision, such as whether a minimum quantity can be adjusted. Some need an internal engineering view before they can even be phrased properly. Separating these groups stops a commercial question from sitting in a queue waiting for a technical owner who was never asked.

When a supplier's public resources page describes how the company works, use it to sharpen your questions rather than to fill gaps. San Techno's resources section, for example, is a sensible place to look for how the company frames its own material and recovery work. If a public page raises a question your reply did not cover, add it to the unanswered list with a note that it came from a public source, not from the supplier's reply to you.

A one-page worksheet for the receiving desk

The template only works if the person receiving the reply can apply it quickly. A one-page worksheet keeps the three layers visible without requiring a long procedure.

The worksheet has three blocks. Block one, confirmed documents, has columns for document name, revision or date, and what it supports. Block two, stated constraints, has columns for the constraint, the supplier's exact wording, the basis (written reply, confirmed verbal, or document), and the date recorded. Block three, unanswered questions, has columns for the question identifier, the question as sent, the response received, the internal owner, and the next review date.

At the foot of the worksheet, add a short sign-off line: who reviewed the reply, on what date, and which layer still needs attention. This is an author heuristic, not a standard, but it gives a small team a consistent place to record that a reply was read rather than merely received.

A worked fictional example shows the flow. A buyer sends the three-heading request. The reply confirms one data sheet, states a minimum quantity and a lead time, and leaves two questions open. The receiving desk fills the worksheet, marks the data sheet as supporting the grade description only, records the lead time with the word "typically," and assigns the two open questions to a named internal role with a review date. Nothing in the worksheet claims more than the reply did.

Where this template fits alongside your other records

The supplier reply template is one document in a chain, and it works best when it is not asked to do another document's job. It is not a glossary, a revision comparison, or a price comparison. Those tasks need their own records, and mixing them into the reply template is how assumptions creep back in.

Keep the template focused on the three layers and let adjacent records handle their own questions. A glossary settles what a term means. A revision comparison tracks how a specification changed. A price comparison handles quote dates and currency labels. The reply template simply records what the supplier said, what limits they stated, and what remains open.

When the request touches a specialised area, such as a power module design discussion, the template still applies, but the unanswered-questions layer will carry more weight. San Techno describes an AC-to-DC power module design offering and notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential. That is exactly the kind of statement the template should capture as a stated constraint, with the confidential items logged as questions that will not be answered through this channel. Recording the boundary is useful; pressing against it is not.

The same discipline applies to botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand. Where a supplier describes a product in broad terms, the template records the description as a supplier statement and leaves any interpretation to the appropriate internal owner. The goal throughout is modest and practical: a reply that a colleague can read without mistaking an assumption for a fact.
