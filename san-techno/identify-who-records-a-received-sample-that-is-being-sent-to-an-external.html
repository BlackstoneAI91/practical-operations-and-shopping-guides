Who Records a Received Sample Sent to an External Reviewer?

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on assigning the record owner when a received sample leaves the building for an authorised external reviewer, covering handover logs, disclosure approvals and traceability.

Why the record owner question comes up at all

A sample arrives at your receiving desk. It is logged, checked against the purchase reference, and then someone decides it needs an outside opinion. Perhaps a specialist reviewer has been engaged to look at a batch of semiconductor materials, or a partner lab is being asked to comment on a wafer reclaim lot. The sample leaves your premises. At that moment a small but important gap opens: the receiving record says the sample arrived, but nothing yet says who is accountable for the record of it leaving, who approved the release, and who updates the file when the reviewer sends something back.

This guide is about that gap. It is written for Singapore procurement teams who source, receive and document semiconductor and solar materials, and who occasionally need to route a physical sample to an authorised external reviewer. It does not cover how to test or assess a sample, and it does not offer legal advice on disclosure. It covers the administrative question: who owns the record, and how do you make that ownership visible so the file stays traceable.

The short answer is that the record owner should be a named role inside your organisation, not the external reviewer and not whoever happens to be at the receiving desk that day. The rest of this guide explains how to choose that role, what the record should contain, and how to keep the handover clean.

The four roles in a sample handover

Before naming a record owner, separate the roles. In most receiving setups there are four, and confusing them is the usual cause of a missing entry.

The receiving role accepts the physical item and creates the arrival record. This person confirms the sample exists, matches it to a purchase or delivery reference, and notes its condition at intake. They should not be the person who decides whether it can go out.

The holding role keeps the sample while it waits. This may be a store, a locked cabinet, or a labelled shelf. The holder is accountable for the sample being where the record says it is.

The approving role authorises release to the external reviewer. This is the person who confirms that the reviewer is authorised, that the scope of what may be shared is understood, and that any supplier-side confidentiality position has been checked. In a small team this may be the same person as the record owner, but the two decisions should still be recorded separately.

The record owner maintains the handover log. They do not necessarily touch the sample. Their job is to make sure the log reflects reality: who released it, to whom, on what reference, under which approval, and what came back.

A useful rule of thumb, offered here as an author heuristic rather than a standard: if you cannot name the record owner in one sentence, the process is not yet defined.

What the handover record should contain

A handover record does not need to be elaborate, but it should answer a fixed set of questions so that a colleague picking up the file months later can follow it. Consider a template with these fields:

Sample identifier and the internal reference it was received under. This ties the outbound record back to the arrival record. Date and time of release, and the name of the role that released it. The external reviewer, identified by organisation and the engagement reference rather than by personal contact details. The approval reference that authorised the release, and the role that granted it. The scope note: what category of information the reviewer may see, expressed in general terms. The expected return or response date, marked clearly as an expectation rather than a commitment. The return entry, completed when the sample or the reviewer's response comes back, with the date and the receiving role.

Two habits keep this template honest. First, never leave the approval reference blank and fill it in later from memory; an empty approval field is a signal, not a placeholder. Second, keep the scope note general. If the reviewer needs to see something specific, that specificity belongs in the approval, not scattered across the log.

Traceability: keeping the arrival and departure records connected

Traceability here means one thing: given a sample identifier, you can reconstruct the whole journey without asking anyone. Arrival, holding, release, review, return. Each step should point to the next.

The most common failure is a break between the arrival record and the handover record. The receiving desk logs the sample under one reference, the person arranging the external review uses a different reference, and the two never meet. The fix is procedural rather than technical: the handover record must quote the arrival reference, and the arrival record should carry a pointer to the handover once it exists.

A second failure is the silent return. The reviewer sends something back, a colleague signs for it, and the handover log is never updated. The record owner should have a standing instruction to close the loop, and the return entry should name the role that received it, not just the date.

A third, subtler failure is scope drift. The original approval covered a narrow question, the reviewer asks a follow-up, and the answer is given informally without a new record. If the follow-up touches anything the supplier has marked confidential, the informal answer is a disclosure problem. The safe habit is to route follow-up questions back through the approving role rather than answering them at the desk.

Disclosure approvals at a general level

This section stays general on purpose. Whether a particular disclosure is permitted depends on your agreements and your organisation's own rules, and that is a question for the people who own those agreements, not for a procurement guide.

What procurement can do is make the approval visible and bounded. Three practices help.

First, record the approval as a decision with a scope, not as a permission to share freely. "Approved to share the sample and the delivery documentation with reviewer X for question Y" is a usable record. "Approved to share" is not.

Second, separate the confidentiality question from the purchase question. If a supplier has marked something confidential, as San Techno does for the detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes of its power-module work, that marking should be captured in your file at the point of receipt, so the approving role sees it before release rather than after.

Third, keep the reviewer's access to information proportionate to the question asked. An external reviewer engaged to comment on a documentation gap does not need the full commercial file. Narrow access is easier to record and easier to defend.

Where a supplier's own public materials describe testing or certification, treat those descriptions as the supplier's statements rather than as your verification. Your record should note what the supplier said and where it came from, not restate it as your own finding.

A short worksheet for assigning the record owner

Use this as a working checklist when a received sample is about to go to an external reviewer. It is a suggested structure, not a standard, and the scoring is illustrative only.

Step one: name the record owner by role. Write it down before the sample moves. If two people could plausibly claim the role, resolve it now.

Step two: confirm the arrival reference. The handover record cannot be created without it.

Step three: confirm the approving role and capture the approval reference. If the approval is verbal, convert it to a written note with a date and a scope.

Step four: check for any confidentiality marking on the received material and record it in the file.

Step five: record the reviewer by organisation and engagement reference, and note the expected response date as an expectation.

Step six: set the return instruction. Who signs for the return, who updates the log, and by when.

Step seven: at close, check that arrival, release and return all reference the same sample identifier. If any of the three is missing, the record is open, not complete.

A simple illustrative scoring, purely as an example: give one point for each of the seven steps that is fully recorded, and treat anything below seven as an open item to resolve before the next handover. The number is arbitrary; the discipline of checking is the point.

Where this fits in the wider procurement file

The handover record is one page in a larger file, and it works best when it uses the same identifiers as everything around it. If your team maintains a controlled glossary, a revision comparison log, or an attachments list, the sample handover should reference those documents rather than duplicate them.

A final point on tone. The record owner is not a gatekeeper and not a scapegoat. The role exists so that when someone asks who authorised a sample to leave and what came back, there is a clear answer. Naming that role in advance, and giving it a template with fixed fields, does more for traceability than any amount of after-the-fact reconstruction.
