Create a Review Queue for Material Records Whose Attachments Are Unreadable

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building a review queue for material records whose electronic attachments cannot be opened, covering intake triage, record replacement, history retention and the boundary between a bad file and a bad physical label.

Why unreadable attachments need their own queue

A material record can be complete on paper and still be unusable. The purchase reference is there, the lot description is there, the receiving note is there, and then the attachment that was supposed to carry the supporting detail will not open. The file is corrupt, the scan is blank, the format is unsupported, or the document opens to a single grey page. The record looks finished in the system, so nobody flags it, and the gap only surfaces weeks later when someone tries to reconcile a delivery against its evidence.

That is the problem this guide addresses. An unreadable electronic attachment is a documentation defect, not a material defect, and it deserves a separate queue because the fix is different. Someone has to identify the record, request or reconstruct a readable copy, confirm what the replacement actually covers, and keep the original failure on file so the history is honest. Mixing these cases into a general exceptions pile means they get triaged alongside quantity disputes and label mismatches, which need entirely different handling.

This page is written for Singapore procurement teams sourcing, receiving and documenting semiconductor and solar materials, where a single lot file may carry supplier certificates, packing lists, inspection notes and internal receiving sheets. The queue described here is an administrative tool. It does not decide whether material is acceptable, and it does not replace whatever technical review your organisation requires. Its job is to make sure that when a record is cited later, the attachment behind it can actually be read by the person who needs it.

Separate the unreadable file from the unreadable label

The most common mistake in this workflow is treating two different failures as one. An unreadable attachment is an electronic document problem: the file exists in the record but cannot be opened, rendered or interpreted. An unreadable physical container label is a receiving-floor problem: the material is in front of you, but the marking on the box, reel or bag cannot be read with confidence.

These need different owners and different evidence. The electronic case is resolved by obtaining or regenerating a readable document and attaching it to the same record with a note explaining the substitution. The physical case is resolved at the receiving desk, by the people who can see the container, and it should be logged as a receiving exception rather than pushed into the document queue. If you route a damaged label into a file-repair queue, the document team will spend time chasing a replacement PDF while the actual question, what is in this box, goes unanswered.

A simple test at intake: can the reviewer open the attachment and read the content it is supposed to carry? If no, it belongs in this queue. Can the receiver read the identifying marks on the physical item? If no, it belongs in the receiving exception log. A record can appear in both, and that is fine, as long as the two entries are linked rather than merged. Keeping the distinction sharp also stops the queue from becoming a catch-all that nobody trusts.

Intake triage: what to capture when a file will not open

The queue only works if the first entry is detailed enough for someone else to act on. When a reviewer finds an unreadable attachment, capture the following before moving on. Treat this as a minimum set, not a maximum.

Record identifier and the specific attachment name. Not just the lot or order, but which file failed, because a record may carry several.

Failure type, in plain words. Examples: will not open, opens blank, pages missing, unreadable scan quality, format not supported by our viewer. Avoid vague entries like bad file.

Who found it and when. A date and a role are enough. This matters when the same supplier document fails repeatedly and you want to raise it in a review conversation.

What the attachment was supposed to evidence. A certificate, a packing list, an inspection record, an internal sign-off. If nobody knows what the missing content was meant to prove, the replacement cannot be checked.

Whether a readable copy exists elsewhere. Sometimes the same document arrived by a second route, or a colleague holds a working copy. Note where, without pasting personal contact details into the queue.

Interim status. Is the record on hold, or has it been accepted provisionally pending the readable copy? Say which, and say who decided.

A short free-text note is usually enough for the last field. The point is that a colleague picking up the queue the next morning can understand the problem without reopening the failed file, which may itself be slow or unstable to load.

A worked example of the queue in use

The following is fictional and intended only to show the shape of an entry.

A receiving team logs a delivery of solar-grade silicon against a purchase reference. The packing list attachment opens normally, but the supporting material record attached to the same line renders as a blank page in the viewer. The reviewer opens the queue and creates an entry: record reference, attachment name, failure type opens blank, date, reviewer role, expected content described as the supplier material record for the lot, no known readable copy, status on hold pending replacement.

The next day a second reviewer checks the original file in a different viewer and confirms it is genuinely unreadable rather than a viewer quirk. That confirmation is added to the same entry rather than creating a new one. A request goes to the supplier for a reissued copy, and the entry is updated to show the request date and the expected response window. When the replacement arrives, the reviewer opens it, confirms it covers the same lot and the same content, attaches it to the record, and closes the queue entry with a note that the original failed file is retained.

Nothing in that sequence required a judgement about the material itself. The queue tracked a documentation gap from discovery to closure, and the record now carries both the readable replacement and the history of why it was needed.

Replacing the record without erasing the history

The temptation, once a readable copy arrives, is to overwrite the failed attachment and move on. Resist it. The failed file is evidence that a gap existed, and the replacement is evidence that it was closed. Keeping both, with a clear note, gives you a record that can survive an audit question months later.

A workable convention is to keep the original attachment in place, mark it as superseded for readability reasons, and attach the replacement alongside it with its own date and source. The queue entry then becomes the pointer that explains the relationship between the two. If your system cannot hold both, record the failure and the replacement reference in the queue itself and treat the queue as the audit trail.

Be precise about what the replacement covers. A reissued document is not automatically the same document. Check that it refers to the same lot, the same reference and the same content the original was meant to evidence. If the replacement covers a different scope, that is a new question, not a closed one.

This is also where a controlled attachments list earns its keep. If your team already maintains a list of document identifiers, file status and revision controls for a purchase pack, the queue can reference those identifiers instead of inventing new ones. The two tools complement each other: the list says what should exist, the queue says what cannot currently be read.

Review cadence and closure rules

A queue without a review rhythm quietly becomes a list of unresolved items that everyone has learned to ignore. Set a cadence that fits your volume and stick to it. A weekly review is a common starting point for a small team; treat that as an example rather than a recommendation grounded in research.

At each review, work through the open entries and ask four questions. Is the failure still real, or has a readable copy since arrived and not been logged? Has the replacement request been sent, and to whom, in role terms? Is the record still on hold, and does the hold still make sense? Has the entry been open long enough that it needs escalation to whoever owns the supplier relationship?

Closure should require three things: a readable replacement attached to the record, a note confirming it covers the same scope, and a retained reference to the original failure. An entry closed without all three is a gap waiting to reappear.

Finally, keep a short running count of failure types. If the same kind of attachment fails repeatedly from the same source, that pattern is worth raising in a routine review conversation. The queue is not a performance scorecard, and it should not be used to imply anything about a supplier's overall reliability from a handful of entries. Its value is narrower and more useful: it tells you which records cannot currently be read, who is fixing them, and what evidence exists that the fix was real.
