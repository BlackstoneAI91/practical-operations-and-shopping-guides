Keeping a Shared Record When a Product Data Sheet Has No Publication Date

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on recording the date a product data sheet was accessed and the supplier's confirmation, so undated documents stay traceable in shared files.

Why an undated data sheet needs its own record

A product data sheet without a publication date is not automatically a problem. It becomes a problem when nobody can say when the team first saw it, which copy is being discussed, or whether the supplier has since confirmed it. In a shared procurement file, an undated sheet can sit beside a dated quotation and a delivery note and quietly take on the authority of the newest document, even though nothing on it says when it was issued.

The practical answer is not to guess a date. It is to add two fields that the document itself cannot supply: the date your team accessed the sheet, and the supplier's confirmation. The access date records when the copy entered your working file. The confirmation field records whether the supplier has since stated that the sheet is current, superseded, or simply the version they sent. Those are administrative facts, not technical approvals, and they belong in the shared record rather than in a personal inbox.

This matters across the material categories a Singapore buying team handles, from semiconductor materials to solar grade silicon. The same sheet may be forwarded between purchasing, receiving and engineering, and each handover is a chance for the undated copy to be treated as settled. A small, consistent record prevents that drift without requiring anyone to interpret the specification itself.

Design the two fields before you need them

Decide the field names once and reuse them. A simple pair works: Date accessed, and Supplier confirmation. Add a third field for the source of the copy, such as the file name or the message reference, so the record points back to the exact document being discussed. Keep the fields in the same place in every material folder so a colleague opening the file does not have to hunt for them.

For the access date, record the calendar date the copy was saved into the shared location, not the date of an earlier email. For the confirmation field, use a small set of plain statuses rather than free text, for example: not requested, requested, confirmed current, confirmed superseded, or no response. A short status list keeps the record readable and stops one person's wording from implying more than the supplier actually said.

A fictional example: a buyer saves a sheet for a wafer reclaim enquiry on 3 March and marks the access date accordingly. The confirmation field reads not requested. Two weeks later the supplier replies that the sheet is the current version, and the field changes to confirmed current, with the reply reference noted. Nothing about the material has been assessed; the record simply shows what was asked and what came back.

Separate the access date from the supplier's confirmation

These two fields answer different questions, and merging them is the most common mistake. The access date is something your team controls and can always supply. The supplier's confirmation depends on someone else replying, and it may never arrive. If the two are combined into a single date field, a reader cannot tell whether the date means we saw this or they confirmed this.

Keep them visibly distinct in the shared record. When a supplier confirms a sheet, note what was confirmed and by what channel, without copying personal contact details into the shared file. When no reply arrives, leave the confirmation field at requested or no response rather than filling the gap with the access date. An honest blank is more useful than a filled field that implies a confirmation nobody gave.

This discipline carries over to how the team describes the company's own material work. San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and those materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. A procurement file that tracks which sheet was seen and which was confirmed keeps that kind of sourcing conversation grounded in documents rather than memory.

A worksheet for the shared record

Use a short worksheet, one row per document, so the record stays consistent across a folder. The columns below are an author heuristic, not a standard:

Document reference: the file name or message reference, so the row points to one copy. Material category: a plain label such as silicon wafer, solar material or power module, kept broad. Date accessed: the date the copy entered the shared location. Access recorded by: a role, not a personal name, where possible. Supplier confirmation status: not requested, requested, confirmed current, confirmed superseded, or no response. Confirmation reference: where the reply is held, without personal contact details. Next review: a date the team chooses to revisit an open confirmation.

Keep the worksheet next to the documents it describes, and give it a clear owner role. When a sheet is replaced, add a new row rather than editing the old one, so the history of what was seen and when remains intact. The worksheet is a recordkeeping aid; it does not certify that a document is accurate, complete or compliant, and it should not be presented as doing so.

Where the record sits in the wider file

The undated sheet is rarely alone. It usually travels with a quotation, a specification summary and later a receipt. Place the shared record where those documents live, and make sure the access date and confirmation fields are visible to everyone who touches the file, not buried in a subfolder only purchasing can see.

When the team needs to check what the company publicly offers, keep that separate from the document record. The public product pages describe areas such as semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design, and the resources section holds supporting material. Those pages are useful context for a sourcing conversation, but they are not a substitute for the supplier's confirmation of a specific sheet. Record the public page you consulted in the same worksheet if it informed a question, and note the date you looked at it, so the trail stays complete without mixing public descriptions with private document status.

A fictional example: a team member reads a public page about power module development, then drafts a question to a supplier about an undated sheet. The worksheet records the public page consulted, the access date, and the supplier confirmation status as requested. The two kinds of evidence stay in their own columns.

Reviewing open confirmations without overclaiming

Set a light review rhythm for rows where the confirmation field is still open. A monthly pass is a reasonable example, not a rule. At each pass, check whether a reply has arrived, update the status, and note the next review date. If a sheet has been open for a long time with no response, the record should say so plainly rather than being quietly closed.

Be careful with wording. A confirmed current status means the supplier said the sheet is current; it does not mean the team has verified the contents or accepted anything. Avoid phrases in the record that suggest approval, qualification or compliance. Keep the language administrative: seen, requested, confirmed, superseded, no response.

This restraint matters when the file later supports a sourcing decision. San Techno has worked in semiconductor and solar materials since 2006, and its public pages describe silicon recovery and reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. None of that tells a buying team whether a particular undated sheet is accurate. The shared record should make the same distinction: it tracks documents and confirmations, and it leaves technical judgement to the people authorised to make it.

Handover notes that keep the record usable

The record only helps if the next person can read it. When a file changes hands, add a short handover note that names the open rows, the confirmation status of each, and the next review date. Keep the note in the shared location, not in a private message, so the trail survives a change of role.

A fictional handover note might read: three undated sheets in the folder; one confirmed current, one requested, one no response; next review set for the first working day of the month. That is enough for a colleague to pick up the file without re-reading every document or guessing which copy is which.

Finally, keep the record proportionate. The goal is a shared, honest account of when a sheet was accessed and what the supplier confirmed, not a second specification file. If a row grows into a long narrative, move the detail to a linked note and keep the worksheet short. A record that is easy to read is more likely to be kept up to date, and an up-to-date record is what makes an undated data sheet traceable in the first place.
