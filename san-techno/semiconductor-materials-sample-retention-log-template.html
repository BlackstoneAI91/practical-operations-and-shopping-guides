Sample Retention Log for Semiconductor Materials Evaluations

Published by San Techno.

A practical template and guide for Singapore procurement teams to log retained evaluation samples of semiconductor materials, track storage locations, and set review and disposal dates so a supplier trial closes out cleanly.

Why a retention log belongs in the trial file

An evaluation trial for semiconductor materials rarely ends when the last test result is written down. A portion of the delivered sample usually stays behind: a sealed reference pack, a partly used container, a labelled bag kept for a second look. Without a record, those retained items drift. They sit in a store room, lose their label, and become impossible to tie back to the purchase order or the trial report that justified them.

A sample retention log fixes that by treating retained material as a record, not as leftover stock. The log answers four questions for every retained item: what it is, where it is, why it is being kept, and when the retention period ends. That last question is the one most trial files miss, and it is the one that lets procurement close a trial cleanly rather than leaving it open indefinitely.

This guide is written for Singapore procurement teams who source, receive and document semiconductor and solar materials. It offers a log structure, a worked fictional example, and a short review routine. It does not describe how to test, process or dispose of any material; those steps belong to the technical and facilities teams and to local requirements. The log is an administrative tool that keeps the paperwork honest.

That breadth is useful context here: a single procurement team may hold retained samples from very different material families, and one log format should be able to hold them all without confusion.

What the log should capture before the first sample is stored

Design the log fields before any sample arrives, because retrofitting fields onto a trial already in progress is where records break. A workable log has three field groups.

Identity fields tie the retained item to the trial. Record the internal trial reference, the supplier name, the material description as written on the supplier paperwork, the lot or batch reference if one is given, and the date the sample was received. Keep the supplier's own wording for the material description rather than paraphrasing it, so the log and the delivery documents can be matched later.

Storage fields record where the item physically sits. Use a location code your team already understands, such as a store room identifier plus a shelf or bin reference, and add the container type and the quantity retained in the unit the supplier used. If the sample arrived in a sealed pack, note that it is sealed; if it was opened for evaluation, note that too. This is a description of condition, not an instruction to open or reseal anything.

Retention fields carry the decision. Record the reason for keeping the item, the retention end date, the role responsible for the review, and the outcome once the review happens. The reason matters more than it looks: a sample kept to answer a later specification query is a different record from one kept as a sealed reference against future deliveries, and the two may deserve different retention periods.

A note on scope. Where a trial involves power modules rather than raw materials, the log should stay at the level of what procurement actually holds. San Techno's power-module programme addresses heat movement, package stability and reliable operation under repeated electrical and thermal loading, and detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential. A retention log should never become a place where confidential technical detail is copied. Log the item, the reference and the location; leave the engineering content in the engineering file.

A worked fictional example

The following example is invented to show how the fields fit together. It does not describe a real supplier, material or trial.

A procurement team runs a trial referenced TR-2401 for a solar-related material. Two packs are retained. The first is a sealed reference pack, logged with the supplier's material description, the lot reference from the delivery note, a store location of SR-2 shelf B, a quantity of one pack, a condition note of sealed, a reason of sealed reference against future deliveries, a retention end date twelve months after receipt, and a review owner in procurement. The second is a partly used container from the same delivery, logged with the same trial reference, a location of SR-2 shelf C, a condition note of opened, a reason of pending specification query, a retention end date three months after receipt, and the same review owner.

At the three-month review, the specification query has been answered. The reviewer records the outcome, notes that the opened container is no longer needed for the trial, and passes the item to the facilities team under the site's own arrangements. The log entry is not deleted; it is closed with a date and an outcome. The sealed pack stays until its twelve-month date, when it is reviewed again.

The example shows the point of separate retention dates. A single end date for the whole trial would either hold the opened container far longer than needed or force the sealed reference pack out too early. Two reasons, two dates, one trial reference.

For teams whose trials involve reclaimed material rather than new stock, the same log structure applies, and the intake paperwork that supports it is set out in the wafer reclaim intake document checklist for a procurement audit. The retention log sits alongside that pack rather than replacing it.

The log template

The template below is a field list, not a form to copy verbatim. Adapt the column names to your own records system, but keep every field.

Trial reference. Internal identifier for the evaluation.

Supplier. The name as it appears on the delivery documents.

Material description. The supplier's wording, unchanged.

Lot or batch reference. As given, or blank if none was provided.

Receipt date. The date the sample was received and booked in.

Container type and quantity. What was retained, in the supplier's unit.

Condition at retention. Sealed, opened, or other plain description.

Storage location. A code your team already uses.

Reason for retention. One short sentence.

Retention end date. A specific date, not a duration.

Review owner. A role, not a person's name, so the log survives staff changes.

Review outcome and date. Completed when the review happens.

Handover reference. Where the item went at close-out, recorded as a reference to the receiving team's own record.

Two rules keep the template usable. First, every retained item gets its own row, even when several items come from the same delivery. Second, no row is deleted; a closed row keeps its history. A log that only shows current holdings cannot answer the question a later audit will ask, which is what happened to the sample that is no longer on the shelf.

Setting retention periods without guessing

Retention periods are a judgement call, and the log should show that judgement rather than hide it. Three inputs usually shape the decision.

The first is the purpose. A sealed reference kept against future deliveries needs to outlast the period in which those deliveries are expected. A sample kept only to answer an open query needs to last until the query closes, plus a short margin.

The second is the trial's own timeline. If the evaluation report is due in a set number of weeks, the retained sample should stay at least until the report is signed off, in case a reviewer asks to see it.

The third is the site's own records policy. Where a policy already sets a retention period for evaluation records, the sample log should follow it rather than invent a parallel rule.

A simple author heuristic, offered as an example rather than a standard, is to set the retention end date as the later of the report sign-off date plus one review cycle, or the date the last related delivery is expected. Teams can adjust this to their own practice. The important discipline is that the date is written down at the point of retention, not decided later when the shelf is full.

Where a trial involves material that can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss, the retention decision deserves a moment's thought. Keeping a sample indefinitely is not automatically the careful choice; a clear end date and a recorded handover is often the tidier one.

The review routine and clean close-out

A log only works if someone reads it on a schedule. A monthly review of rows approaching their retention end date is usually enough for a procurement team, and it takes little time if the log is kept current.

At each review, the owner checks three things. Is the item still where the log says it is? Is the reason for retention still valid? Is the end date still right? If the answer to the second question is no, the row moves to close-out early. If the answer to the first is no, the location is corrected and the discrepancy noted, because a sample that has moved without a record is exactly the situation the log exists to prevent.

Close-out has its own small discipline. Record the outcome, the date, and a handover reference to whichever team takes the item next. Do not record disposal instructions in the log; the log records that the item left the trial's custody and who acknowledged it. The receiving team's own records carry the rest.

When every row for a trial is closed, the trial file is complete: the evaluation report, the delivery documents, the retention log with all rows closed, and the handover references. That is what a clean close-out looks like, and it is what makes the next audit straightforward.

For teams that also handle reclaimed wafers, the same close-out logic applies to collection records, and the silicon collection records handover between shifts sheet is a useful companion when items change hands across shifts.

Where this fits in the wider trial file

The retention log is one document among several that a procurement team assembles around an evaluation. It sits beside the specification comparison made before the trial, the receiving records made at delivery, and the evaluation report made at the end. Each answers a different question, and none substitutes for another.

A final word on restraint. The log should contain only what procurement needs to track an item: identity, location, reason, date and handover. Technical detail, commercial terms and anything marked confidential stay in their own files. A log kept to that boundary stays useful for years, and it closes cleanly every time.
