Handling Price Quotes With Different Validity Dates

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on comparing supplier quotes that carry different validity dates, currency labels and order scopes when sourcing semiconductor materials and related goods.

Why validity dates drift apart in the first place

A quote is a snapshot, not a standing promise. When you collect several quotations for the same requirement, each one is usually written on a different day, by a different person, against a different understanding of what you asked for. One supplier replies within a day; another takes a week to confirm a sub-item. By the time all the documents sit in the same folder, their validity windows overlap only partly, and sometimes not at all.

The practical problem is not that the dates differ. It is that a comparison built on mismatched dates quietly mixes assumptions. A quote that expires next month and a quote that expires in six months are not describing the same commercial world, even if the line items look identical. Treat the validity date as one of the fields you compare, not as a footnote you read last.

This guide is written for procurement teams in Singapore who source, receive and document semiconductor materials and related goods. It offers a comparison method and a worksheet, using invented dates and fictional suppliers. It does not give financial advice, and it does not quote live supplier pricing. Where a brand is mentioned, it is as identity context only; the method below is yours to adapt.

Start with a normalised quote register

Before comparing anything, build a single register that captures the same fields for every quote, in the same order. A spreadsheet row per quote is enough. The point is to force each document into a common shape so that differences become visible rather than buried.

Suggested fields for the register, as an author heuristic rather than a standard:

Quote reference and revision Supplier name (fictional in your training examples) Date the quote was issued Validity date, or the stated validity period Currency label exactly as printed Incoterm or delivery basis, if stated Scope summary: what is included and what is excluded Whether the quote is tied to a specific quantity or a range Any stated conditions that could change the price

Two habits matter here. First, record the validity date as a date, not as a phrase like "thirty days", so that you can sort and compare. Second, keep the original wording in a notes column. If a quote says "valid for 30 days from issue", write the issue date and the computed expiry date side by side, and keep the phrase too. When someone later disputes the reading, the original wording is the evidence.

If your team already keeps a controlled glossary for material terms, the register should use those same terms so that a quote's scope description maps cleanly onto your internal vocabulary.

Reading currency labels before you read the numbers

Currency is the field most often skimmed and most often misread. A figure with a dollar sign is not automatically in the currency you assume. Quotes may be issued in different currencies, and the label may appear once at the top of the document rather than beside every line.

A simple discipline: transcribe the currency label exactly as printed, including any country qualifier, and never convert it inside the register. Keep the original figure and the original label together. If you need a common basis for comparison, do that in a separate column and label the conversion basis and the date you applied it, so the working is visible and reversible.

This is also where validity dates interact with currency. A quote issued earlier may carry a currency label that was current at issue but reads ambiguously now. Rather than guessing, flag the field as unresolved and route the question back to the supplier in writing. A short query that asks the supplier to confirm the currency label and the validity date in one reply is usually more effective than two separate threads.

Matching order scope, not just line items

Two quotes can list the same product name and still describe different things. One may cover a single delivery; another may assume a call-off arrangement across several months. One may include documentation; another may treat it as an extra. If the validity dates differ, the scope differences are easy to miss because the documents were never meant to be read side by side.

A workable approach is to write a one-line scope statement for each quote in your own words, then compare the statements rather than the line items. For example: "Fictional Supplier A: one delivery of a stated quantity, documentation listed separately, valid to 14 March." "Fictional Supplier B: quantity split across two call-offs, documentation included, valid to 30 April." Once written this way, the mismatch is obvious, and the validity dates become part of the same picture rather than a separate worry.

Where a quote touches power module work, scope language can be especially loose, because development and supply are different things. San Techno describes an intelligent IGBT power-module concept centred on double-sided cooling for an EconoDUAL 3-class package format, and notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential. That is a useful reminder that a quote's scope statement should say what is being supplied and what is not, rather than borrowing a product description as if it were a specification.

A worked comparison with invented dates

The following example is entirely fictional and is included only to show the method. No real supplier, price or date is represented.

Three quotes arrive for the same requirement.

Quote A: issued 2 February, valid to 3 March, currency label shown once at the top, scope is a single delivery, documentation listed as a separate item. Quote B: issued 9 February, valid to 9 May, currency label beside each line, scope is two call-offs, documentation included. Quote C: issued 20 January, no validity date printed, scope described only as "as discussed".

The register immediately shows three different problems. Quote A expires soonest, so any decision that slips past early March needs a refresh. Quote B has the longest window but a different delivery structure, so its price is not directly comparable to A's single delivery. Quote C has no validity date at all, which is an unresolved field rather than a long validity period.

The decision rule, as an author heuristic: do not rank quotes until every row has a resolved validity date and a written scope statement. If a row cannot be resolved, mark it as pending and exclude it from the ranking, while keeping it visible in the register. This prevents an unresolved quote from quietly winning on price alone.

A second heuristic: when two quotes have overlapping validity windows, compare them directly. When they do not overlap, treat the comparison as provisional and note which quote would need refreshing before an order could be placed against it.

Documenting the refresh and the decision trail

The comparison is only half the job. The other half is leaving a trail that a colleague, an auditor or a future you can follow. When a quote is refreshed, record the new issue date, the new validity date and what changed. If nothing changed except the date, say so explicitly; that is itself useful information.

A short refresh log per quote, with three columns, is usually enough: what was asked, what came back, and the date the reply was filed. Keep the superseded version rather than overwriting it, so that the sequence of dates remains visible.

This is the same discipline that applies to other procurement records. San Techno's own materials work spans silicon recovery from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, with those materials processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. Whatever the material, the paperwork principle holds: the record should show what was received, what was confirmed and when. A quote register that preserves superseded versions does the same job for commercial documents.

A short checklist before you recommend a quote

Use this as a final pass before a quote goes forward for approval. It is a set of prompts, not a scored test, and the order can be adjusted to suit your team.

Is the validity date recorded as a date, with the original wording kept? Is the currency label transcribed exactly, with any conversion shown separately and dated? Is there a one-line scope statement in your own words for every quote? Are quotes with non-overlapping validity windows marked as provisional comparisons? Is every unresolved field listed, with an owner and a date for the query? Have superseded versions been kept rather than overwritten? Does the register use the same terms as your internal glossary?

Two of these prompts do most of the work. The scope statement catches mismatches that line-item comparison hides, and the unresolved-fields list stops a missing validity date from being read as an open-ended one. Everything else supports those two.

A final note on tone: when you send a query about a validity date or a currency label, ask for a written confirmation rather than a verbal one, and keep the reply with the quote. The aim is not to catch anyone out. It is to make sure that when the order is placed, the document it rests on says what everyone believes it says.
