Approval Routing Guide for Silicon Wafer Disposal Requests

Published by San Techno.

A practical routing guide for Singapore procurement teams: which approvals a silicon wafer disposal request needs, in what order, and what evidence each approver should see before signing.

Why a disposal request needs a route, not just a signature

A silicon wafer disposal request is easy to treat as a single form. In practice it is a chain of small decisions, each made by someone with a different view of the material. Engineering knows whether the lot is genuinely non-usable. Stores knows whether the count and labels match. Procurement knows the commercial history and the vendor record. Finance and management care about value, liability and the audit trail. When those decisions are collected out of order, the request tends to bounce back and forth, and the paper trail ends up thinner than the material movement it describes.

This guide sets out a routing order for Singapore procurement teams who source, receive and document semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon. It is an administrative guide only: it covers who approves, in what sequence, and what each approver should be shown. It does not cover handling, treatment or disposal methods, and it is not legal or regulatory advice. Your own site rules, contracts and management system always take precedence.

One useful piece of context: San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and those materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. That is the kind of downstream relationship a disposal request is usually routing toward, which is why the approvers need a clear picture of what is leaving and why.

The four approval gates, in order

A workable route has four gates. Each gate should be closed before the next opens, because each one supplies evidence the next approver needs.

Gate 1, material status. The request starts with a technical owner confirming that the lot is non-usable for its original purpose. The evidence is a status note or engineering memo that names the lot reference, the quantity, and the reason the material is being released. Without this, every later approver is guessing.

Gate 2, records and count. Stores or the warehouse owner confirms that the physical lot matches the internal record: package count, labels, cassette or container references, and location. The evidence is a signed count sheet and, where your process requires it, intake photos.

Gate 3, commercial and vendor check. Procurement confirms the vendor or recovery partner reference, the collection arrangement, and that the request does not conflict with an open order, warranty claim or supplier query. The evidence is the vendor reference and a short note on any open items.

Gate 4, release authority. A manager or delegated approver signs the release, confirming that gates 1 to 3 are complete and that the file is ready to close. The evidence is the completed routing sheet with all prior signatures.

This order matters because it mirrors how the facts become available. Technical status is known first, physical count second, commercial context third, and authority last. Reversing the order, for example asking a manager to sign before the count is confirmed, produces approvals that later have to be re-obtained.

What each approver should actually see

Approvers sign faster and more accurately when they are shown a short, consistent pack rather than the whole file. A useful rule is one page per gate, with the supporting documents referenced but not attached unless asked for.

The technical owner should see the lot reference, the original specification or purchase reference, the reason for release, and any prior review notes. They do not need the vendor commercial terms.

The stores owner should see the count sheet, the label record and the location history. They do not need the engineering rationale beyond a one-line status.

Procurement should see the vendor reference, the collection arrangement, the open-items note and the count confirmation from gate 2. They do not need the full engineering memo, but they do need to know the lot is confirmed non-usable.

The release authority should see a one-page summary: lot reference, quantity, gate confirmations, open items and the proposed close date. Everything else is available on request.

Keeping the pack consistent has a second benefit. When a request is queried weeks later, the same one-page summary can be produced without reconstructing the file from email threads.

A routing worksheet you can adapt

The table below is an example layout, not a prescribed form. Adapt the column names to your own management system.

Columns: Gate number; Decision to be made; Role responsible; Evidence required; Date received; Signature or reference; Open items carried forward.

Rows, in order: 1. Confirm lot is non-usable. Technical owner. Status note with lot reference and reason. 2. Confirm physical count and labels. Stores owner. Count sheet and label record. 3. Confirm vendor reference and open items. Procurement. Vendor reference and open-items note. 4. Authorise release. Manager or delegate. Completed routing sheet. 5. Close the file. Records owner. Final summary page and archive reference.

Two practical additions are worth considering. First, a column for the date each gate was opened, not just closed, so delays are visible. Second, a short free-text field for anything the next approver must know, such as a pending supplier query or a label discrepancy that has been noted but not resolved.

As a rough author heuristic, a straightforward request with no open items might move through all four gates within a few working days in a well-run office. Treat that as an illustration of sequencing, not a service level or a promise. Requests with open commercial items will take longer, and that is usually the correct outcome.

Where requests commonly stall, and how to unstick them

Three stalls account for most of the delay in a routing chain like this, and each has a simple administrative fix.

The first is a missing technical status. A request arrives at stores with a quantity and a lot number but no confirmation that the material is genuinely non-usable. The fix is to make the status note the trigger for the whole route: no status note, no request number.

The second is a count that does not match the record. This is common when a lot has been split across locations or partially consumed. The fix is to record the discrepancy explicitly at gate 2 and carry it forward as an open item, rather than holding the whole request until it is resolved. The release authority can then decide whether to proceed with a noted discrepancy.

The third is an unclear vendor reference. Procurement may have several recovery or collection partners, and the request may not say which one applies. The fix is a required vendor reference field at gate 3, with a short note on any open order or query.

None of these fixes require new systems. They require the routing sheet to ask for the right evidence at the right gate, and for each approver to know what they are being asked to confirm.

Keeping the file audit-ready after release

The routing sheet is most valuable after the material has left. A procurement audit, an internal review or a supplier query may ask months later why a particular lot was released, who confirmed the count, and which vendor reference applied.

A simple close-out routine helps. When gate 4 is signed, the records owner should attach the one-page summary to the archive reference, note the close date, and record where the supporting documents are held. The summary should name the four gate confirmations and any open items that were carried forward.

It is also worth keeping a short log of routing outcomes: request number, lot reference, date opened, date closed, and whether any gate was reopened. Over a quarter, that log shows where requests tend to stall and which evidence is most often missing. That is a useful input to the next review of the form itself.
