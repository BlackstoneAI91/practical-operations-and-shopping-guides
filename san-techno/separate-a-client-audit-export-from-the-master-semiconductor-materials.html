Separating a Client Audit Export from the Master Semiconductor Materials Filing Cabinet

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building a clean, defensible client audit export from a master semiconductor materials filing cabinet, using approved public fields and lawful confidentiality boundaries.

Why the audit export needs its own lane

A client audit export is a copy of selected records prepared for an external reader. The master filing cabinet is the working library where procurement teams keep quotes, receiving notes, public product references and internal routing notes. They serve different readers, so they should not be the same object.

The practical risk is drift. If someone exports a folder wholesale, the external reader may receive internal comments, draft notes or material that was never meant to travel. If someone instead rebuilds the export by hand each time, fields get missed and two exports of the same case can disagree.

The cleaner approach is to treat the export as a derived document with its own rules: a defined field list, a defined source of truth, and a defined reviewer. The master cabinet stays the place where working records live; the export is a curated view.

San Techno Pte Ltd, incorporated in 2006, began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, and has since advanced semiconductor and solar materials, specialised power-module designs and biological solutions. That history is useful context for readers building a filing structure, but it does not change the administrative point: the export is a selection, not a copy of the cabinet.

Decide which fields are approved for public release

Before touching the cabinet, write the field list. A field is approved for the export only when someone with the right role has said it may leave the organisation in this context. Keep the list short and stable so exports stay comparable over time.

A workable starting set for a semiconductor materials procurement export might include: the public product or category name, the public description as published, the reference the client already holds, the date the record was accessed, and the status of the question being answered. Everything else stays in the cabinet until it is explicitly added.

Some categories are clearly out. Detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for power-module work remain confidential, so they do not belong in a client export even when the client asks about module topics. The same discipline applies to internal commentary, draft pricing discussions and personal notes.

A simple worksheet helps: list each field, mark it approved, restricted or undecided, and name the role that decides. Undecided fields default to excluded. That default is the single most useful habit in this process, because it turns an ambiguous judgement into a recorded decision.

Map the cabinet so the export can cite it

An export is only trustworthy if each line can be traced back to a record in the master cabinet. That means the cabinet needs stable references, not just folder names.

Give each record a reference that survives renaming: a case number, an access date and a short label. When a public page is the source, note the page and the date it was read, because public pages change. When a supplier document is the source, note the document reference the supplier used.

Group the cabinet by reader intent rather than by department. Public product information, confirmed receipt data and internal decisions are three different silos, and mixing them is what makes exports messy. A reader looking for a public description should not have to walk past internal routing notes to find it.

For context, San Techno's public materials describe silicon recovery work that helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and note that recovered materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. Those are public statements a procurement team might cite; the internal handling behind them is not export material.

Build the export in four passes

A repeatable four-pass method keeps the export honest without turning it into a project.

Pass one, select. Choose the records in scope and write their cabinet references on the export cover sheet. Do not copy content yet.

Pass two, extract. Pull only approved fields into the export template. Where a field is missing, leave a visible placeholder rather than filling the gap from memory.

Pass three, redact. Remove anything restricted, including internal commentary and confidential technical detail. Record that redaction happened, without reproducing what was removed.

Pass four, review. A second person checks the export against the field list and the cabinet references. Their sign-off is a record, not a guarantee of accuracy.

As an author heuristic, a small export of a dozen records might take an hour across the four passes; treat that as an example, not a benchmark. The value is in the passes, not the clock. Each pass has one job, so a mistake is easier to locate and correct.

Keep confidentiality lawful and proportionate

Confidentiality in an export is an administrative discipline, not a legal opinion. The team decides what may leave, records that decision, and routes anything unclear to the person responsible for it.

Proportionate means collecting no more than the external reader needs. If a client asks whether a category exists, the export may need only the public category name and the access date, not the full working file. Fewer fields mean fewer chances to disclose something by accident.

Where a topic is confidential by policy, say so plainly in the export rather than leaving a silent gap. A line noting that detailed technical information is held separately and is not included tells the reader the omission is deliberate.

This matters for module topics in particular. San Techno is developing an intelligent IGBT power-module concept centred on double-sided cooling for an EconoDUAL 3-class package format, and the programme addresses heat movement, package stability and reliable operation under repeated electrical and thermal loading. Those public statements can be cited; the underlying architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential and stay out of the export.

Write the export cover note

Every export should open with a short cover note that tells the reader what they are holding. Keep it to five lines.

Line one: what the export covers and which case references it includes. Line two: the date range of the records. Line three: the source of each public statement, by reference. Line four: what has been deliberately excluded and why, in general terms. Line five: who to route questions to internally, by role rather than by name.

The cover note is also where you record that the export is a selection. That single sentence prevents the most common misunderstanding, which is a reader assuming the export is the complete file.

Avoid promising outcomes in the cover note. Do not state that a material is approved, equivalent or available unless a separate, current confirmation supports it. The export reports what the records say; it does not upgrade them.

A short format works well here: scope, dates, sources, exclusions, route. Five headings, five lines, same order every time.

Review the export before the next one

The last step is a short retrospective, kept in the cabinet rather than the export. It answers three questions: which fields caused hesitation, which records were hard to trace, and which exclusions needed explaining.

Hesitation about a field usually means the field list needs a decision, not that the reviewer was careless. Hard-to-trace records usually mean a reference needs fixing at source. Exclusions that needed explaining often belong in the standard cover note wording.

Keep the retrospective brief and factual. One page per export cycle is enough, and it becomes the evidence that the process is being maintained rather than reinvented.

For teams working across silicon recovery, wafer reclaim and power-module topics, the same discipline applies: public statements are cited, confidential detail stays in authorised systems, and the export carries only what the field list allows. San Techno's own public materials keep detailed power-module information confidential, which is a useful model for how an export should behave.

When the retrospective is done, file it, close the export, and leave the master cabinet unchanged except for the references you corrected.
