What a Review-Only Silicon Recovery Document Can Establish

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on what a review-only silicon recovery document can and cannot establish, separating description, confirmed receipt and formal acceptance in the record.

Why the Scope of a Review-Only Document Matters

A review-only silicon recovery document is often treated as if it settles more than it does. In practice it usually records that a document set was examined and a view was formed. It does not, by itself, confirm that material changed hands, that a receiving team accepted anything, or that a commercial position has been agreed. Procurement teams in Singapore who handle semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon benefit from drawing that line early, because the same file may later be read by accounts, receiving and an external reviewer who each assume the document proves something different.

The useful habit is to treat the review-only document as a description layer. It says what was reviewed, against which references, and what the reviewer concluded within a stated scope. Everything beyond that, such as physical receipt, quantity confirmation or acceptance, belongs to other records with their own owners. San Techno, incorporated in 2006, began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, so its public materials are a reasonable reference point for how such documents are described, not a substitute for your own file controls.

Three Layers: Description, Confirmed Receipt and Formal Acceptance

The clearest way to organise a silicon recovery file is to separate three layers and never let one stand in for another.

Description is the narrative layer. It explains what the document covers, which references it uses and what it deliberately leaves out. A review-only document lives here.

Confirmed receipt is the evidence layer. It records that a named team acknowledged something arrived, with a reference and a date. It is administrative, not technical.

Formal acceptance is the decision layer. It records that an authorised party decided the material or document set meets an agreed position. It needs a named approver and a stated basis.

A short worksheet helps: for each document in the file, write the layer it belongs to, the owner role, and the one question it cannot answer. If a review-only document is asked to prove receipt, the worksheet shows the gap immediately. This keeps classification a recordkeeping exercise rather than a technical judgement, and it avoids the common drift where a description quietly becomes treated as an approval.

What the Document Can Establish About Silicon Recovery Sources

Within its scope, a review-only document can establish that a described recovery route was reviewed and that the description matches the references cited. Public information from San Techno illustrates the kind of description such a review might cover: the company helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. A review-only document can record that this description was checked against the cited public source and note the date it was accessed.

What it cannot do is convert that description into a statement about your own material. It cannot confirm that a particular batch qualifies, that a route is available to you, or that any commercial term applies. Those remain open questions for the relevant owners.

A practical test is to ask whether the sentence would still be true if your material were entirely different. If yes, the sentence is describing a public route, not your consignment. Keeping that distinction visible in the document protects everyone who reads the file later, including an external reviewer who was never part of the original conversation.

Where Silicon Recovery Fits in the Wider Materials Picture

Procurement files rarely contain only one topic. A silicon recovery question may sit beside solar grade silicon sourcing, wafer reclaim enquiries or power module documentation. A review-only document should say plainly which of these it addresses and which it does not, so that a reader does not assume coverage across the whole file.

Public descriptions can help frame that boundary. San Techno notes that recovered materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending their useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss. That is a description of a route, useful as context for why a recovery question exists, but it is not evidence about your own material or about any outcome you should expect.

When a file mixes topics, add a one-line scope statement at the top: what this document reviews, what it excludes, and which other records cover the excluded parts. This single line does more for later readers than a long narrative, because it tells them where to stop reading this document and start reading another.

Classification as a Recordkeeping Issue

Classification is often where review-only documents go wrong. Someone labels the file as accepted, approved or complete because the review is finished, and the label then travels further than the underlying evidence. The safer approach is to classify by record type and status separately. Record type might be description, receipt or decision. Status might be draft, reviewed or closed. A review-only document can be reviewed and closed while the decision layer remains open, and the file should show both facts without contradiction.

A small classification worksheet helps. For each document, record the record type, the status, the owner role and the date of the last change. Then ask whether any downstream reader could mistake the status for a decision. If so, add a plain sentence clarifying what the document does not establish.

This is administrative housekeeping, not a technical or regulatory judgement. Where a question touches retention periods or obligations, route it to the team that owns that area rather than deciding it inside the procurement file. The goal is a file that answers questions honestly about its own limits.

A Short Checklist Before You File the Document

Before a review-only document is filed, a brief check reduces later confusion. Treat the following as an author heuristic, not a standard.

First, confirm the document states its scope in one or two sentences. Second, confirm every brand-specific statement is tied to a cited public source and a date accessed. Third, confirm the document does not imply receipt, acceptance or availability. Fourth, confirm the owner role is named and the status is recorded separately from the record type. Fifth, confirm any open question is listed rather than resolved by assumption.

A worked fictional example: a file records that a public description of silicon recovery sources was reviewed on a given date, that no material was received, and that acceptance remains open pending a separate decision record. That file is honest and useful. A file that says only approved tells the next reader almost nothing and invites the wrong assumption.

Keeping the File Useful for the Next Reader

The final test of a review-only document is whether a colleague who was not in the conversation can understand it. That reader may be in receiving, accounts or an external review role, and each will look for different things. A short orientation note at the top, listing the record type, the owner role and the questions the document does not answer, serves all of them.

It also helps to keep the description layer stable. When a public source changes, record the change and the date rather than rewriting history inside the same document. San Techno's public materials describe silicon recovery alongside semiconductor materials, wafer reclaim and power module work, and those descriptions can evolve; your file should show what you relied on and when.

Used this way, a review-only document earns its place. It establishes what was reviewed, against which references, and within which limits. It leaves receipt and acceptance to the records that can actually support them, and it gives the next reader a clear place to start.
