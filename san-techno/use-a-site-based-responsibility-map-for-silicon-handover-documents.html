Use a Site-Based Responsibility Map for Silicon Handover Documents

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on assigning site-level ownership for scheduling, inventory evidence and receipt confirmation in silicon handover paperwork, with a worksheet and fictional examples.

Why a site-based map beats a single document owner

Silicon handover paperwork rarely fails because nobody is willing to help. It fails because everyone assumes someone else already owns the next step. A purchase order leaves procurement, a collection is arranged, material arrives at a receiving desk, and a confirmation is filed somewhere. Each of those moments belongs to a different place and often a different person. When one document is treated as the property of a single owner, the gaps between sites become invisible until a reference cannot be matched.

A site-based responsibility map fixes this by naming, for each physical location involved, who owns three things: scheduling, inventory evidence and receipt confirmation. The map is not an organisation chart. It is a short table that answers, for a given handover, who is expected to act, what they record, and where the record lives. Procurement keeps the map current; the sites keep their own rows accurate.

This matters for teams handling semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon because the paperwork trail crosses several desks. A buyer in Singapore may never visit the site where material is packed, yet that buyer is usually the person asked to explain a missing confirmation months later. The map gives that buyer a defensible answer: the record was owned at this site, by this role, and here is the evidence.

Keep the map deliberately small. Three columns per site, one row per site, and a short note on what each role must not do. The value comes from clarity, not completeness. A map that names ten owners per site will be ignored; a map that names three will be used.

The three ownership lanes: scheduling, inventory evidence, receipt confirmation

Separate the handover into three lanes and assign one owner per lane per site. This prevents the common situation where a single coordinator is blamed for a scheduling delay, an inventory discrepancy and an unsigned receipt all at once.

Scheduling covers the agreement on when material moves and who has confirmed that window. The owner records the request, the confirmed slot and any change, and keeps the latest acknowledgement as the current version. Scheduling ownership is about communication, not about promising carrier performance.

Inventory evidence covers what the site can show about the material it is handing over or receiving. The owner is responsible for the record that describes the material and for pointing to the supporting document. This is an administrative role: it confirms that a record exists and is readable, not that the material meets any technical standard.

Receipt confirmation covers the moment material is accepted at a receiving point. The owner records that acceptance happened, on what reference, and flags any mismatch for follow-up rather than silently correcting fields.

A fictional worksheet you can copy

The following is an invented example for illustration only. It shows how a small procurement team might lay out the map before a handover.

Site label: Receiving Desk A. Scheduling owner: Inbound Coordinator. Inventory evidence owner: Stores Clerk. Receipt confirmation owner: Receiving Supervisor. Note: the coordinator may confirm a slot but may not alter the material description.

Site label: Packing Site B. Scheduling owner: Dispatch Planner. Inventory evidence owner: Packing Lead. Receipt confirmation owner: Site Administrator. Note: the packing lead records what was prepared; the administrator confirms the handover note was issued.

Site label: Buyer Office. Scheduling owner: Procurement Lead. Inventory evidence owner: Procurement Analyst. Receipt confirmation owner: Procurement Lead. Note: the buyer office holds the master reference list and does not overwrite site records.

Add two columns that make the map usable. First, a document reference column naming the record each owner is expected to produce or hold. Second, a review date, so the map is checked before each handover rather than after a problem. A simple author heuristic is to review the map whenever a site contact changes role; treat that as a prompt, not a rule.

Keep the worksheet free of personal names. Use role labels only, and store contact routing separately in a controlled directory. This keeps the map shareable across teams without exposing individuals.

Writing the handover note so each lane can act

A responsibility map only works if the handover note gives each owner something concrete to act on. Structure the note around the three lanes rather than around a narrative of what happened.

Start with the scheduling block: the requested window, the confirmed slot, and the reference for the latest acknowledgement. State clearly whether the slot is confirmed or provisional. A provisional slot recorded as confirmed is one of the most common sources of downstream confusion.

Follow with the inventory evidence block: the material description as recorded by the site, the document reference that supports it, and a status field such as present, pending or unreadable. Do not merge the description and the supporting document into one field; they answer different questions.

Close with the receipt confirmation block: who accepted, against which reference, and whether any discrepancy was logged. If a discrepancy exists, name the owner who will follow up and the date by which a response is expected. Leave the original entry intact and add a note rather than editing history.

When a handover involves material being collected from a site, the collection arrangements and the documentation expectations should be described in the same note so the receiving desk is not working from two versions. Where the map meets the wider buying file

The responsibility map is one page inside a larger buying file, and it should connect to the documents your team already keeps. Three connections matter most.

First, link each lane owner to the relevant entry in your attachments list, so a reader can move from the map to the actual record. Second, link the receipt confirmation lane to your order acknowledgement and delivery confirmation records, because those answer different questions and should not be collapsed. Third, link the scheduling lane to your pickup change log, so a changed slot has a home.

For teams that also handle power module work alongside materials, keep the map separate. Power module development at San Techno is a distinct programme, and detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential, so a materials handover map should not be stretched to cover it. If a colleague asks where the company's wider work sits, point them to the about page rather than folding unrelated topics into the handover file.

A useful discipline is to give the map a version number and a one-line change summary. When a site contact changes, the map is revised, not rewritten. This keeps the history readable and makes it obvious which version governed a given handover.

Common failure patterns and how the map catches them

Most handover problems fall into a small number of patterns, and each can be caught by a well-drawn map.

The orphaned confirmation: a receipt was signed but nobody owns the record, so it cannot be found later. The map prevents this by naming a receipt confirmation owner per site.

The double-owned description: two sites both maintain a material description and they drift apart. The map assigns inventory evidence to one owner per site and states that others may reference but not overwrite.

The silent correction: a receiving clerk fixes a reference to make it match, erasing the evidence of a mismatch. The map should include an explicit note that discrepancies are logged, not edited away.

The stale slot: a provisional window is treated as confirmed. The map's scheduling lane requires a status field, which forces the distinction.

The missing owner after a role change: the map has a review trigger tied to role changes, so the gap is found before a handover, not after.

None of these patterns require technical judgement to fix. They are administrative, which is why a procurement team can own the map without stepping into engineering decisions. Where a question is genuinely technical, the map should route it to the appropriate team rather than answering it in the handover note.

A short checklist before you publish the map

Run this checklist before sharing the map with sites. It is a set of prompts, not a scored audit.

One: every site in the handover has a row. Two: every row names a scheduling owner, an inventory evidence owner and a receipt confirmation owner, using role labels only. Three: each owner has a named document reference. Four: the note distinguishes confirmed from provisional slots. Five: discrepancies are logged rather than corrected in place. Six: the map has a version number and a change summary. Seven: personal contact details are held in a separate controlled directory, not in the map. Eight: the map has a review trigger tied to role changes.

If a row cannot be filled, that is useful information. An unfilled lane is a question to resolve before the handover, not a gap to paper over. Record the open question, name who will answer it, and set a date.
