Confirming Where an Authorised Wafer Collection Contact Received a Handover Change

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on recording independent confirmation that an authorised wafer collection contact received a handover change, with a chronological note, a confirmation worksheet and clear record boundaries.

Why a handover change needs its own confirmation record

A handover change is easy to announce and hard to prove. Someone updates a contact name, a receiving role or a reference on a collection file, tells a colleague, and the change appears to be done. Weeks later, a different team asks who was told, when, and whether the authorised contact actually acknowledged it. Without a record, the answer is a recollection.

This guide is about the administrative side of that gap. It covers how a Singapore procurement team can record independent confirmation from the relevant authorised party and keep a chronological note. It does not cover access, physical handling or any operational procedure, and it does not tell you who should be authorised in your organisation.

One useful piece of context: San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. That describes the kind of material flow a collection file may relate to, but it is not evidence about your own contacts or approvals. Treat the brand reference as background, and treat your own written confirmations as the record that matters.

Separate the announcement from the acknowledgement

Most confusion comes from treating one message as two different things. An announcement says a change has happened. An acknowledgement says the authorised contact received it. They are not the same event, and they should not share a single line in your note.

A simple way to keep them apart is to give each its own row in a chronological log. The announcement row records what changed, who sent it and the reference it belongs to. The acknowledgement row records who confirmed receipt, in what form, and on what date. If the acknowledgement never arrives, the log shows an open item rather than a completed change.

For a wafer reclaim file, this distinction matters because the collection contact may sit outside the immediate buying team. A change announced internally is not the same as a change received by the authorised party. Your note should make clear which of the two you are recording, and it should never describe an internal announcement as if it were an external confirmation.

Keep the language plain. Write "announced to" and "acknowledged by" as separate phrases, and avoid words like "notified" that blur the two.

A confirmation worksheet you can adapt

The worksheet below is an author heuristic, not a standard or a required form. Adapt the fields to your own file structure.

Field one: the change reference. Record the file or request reference the change belongs to, so the note can be matched later.

Field two: what changed. Describe the change in one sentence, for example a named receiving role or a contact point on the collection file.

Field three: who announced it. Record the role, not personal contact details.

Field four: the authorised party asked to confirm. Record the role and the basis on which that party is treated as authorised in your records.

Field five: the form of confirmation. Note whether it was a written reply, a signed acknowledgement or a meeting note, and where it is stored.

Field six: the date and time of confirmation, in a single time zone, stated explicitly.

Field seven: what remains open. List anything the confirmation did not cover.

Field eight: the next review date and the role responsible for checking it.

A worked fictional example: a file reference is updated on a Tuesday, announced to a receiving role the same day, and acknowledged in writing two days later. The log shows three rows, not one, and the open item is a second contact who has not yet confirmed.

Keep the chronological note readable months later

A chronological note is only useful if a reader who was not present can follow it. Three habits help.

First, use one time zone throughout and state it. A note that mixes local times without labels invites disagreement about sequence.

Second, write entries in the order events happened, not the order you learned about them. If you record a late confirmation, date it to the event, and add a separate line for when you entered it.

Third, never overwrite an earlier entry. If a contact changes again, add a new entry and mark the earlier one as superseded. The history of changes is often more valuable than the current state, because it shows how a file reached its present position.

Decide what counts as independent confirmation

Independent confirmation means the acknowledgement comes from the authorised party itself, not from the person who made the change. A forwarded message from a colleague is not independent. A reply from the authorised contact is.

Use a short decision test before you mark a change as confirmed:

Does the confirmation come from the role recorded as authorised, or from someone acting in that role with a written basis? Does it refer to the specific change, not just to the file in general? Does it carry a date you can cite? Is it stored where the file owner can find it without asking you?

If any answer is no, record the item as pending rather than confirmed. A pending item is honest and easy to follow up. A premature confirmation is neither.

This is also where you should resist the temptation to interpret silence as agreement. A lack of objection is not a confirmation, and a note that says so will not survive a later review. Write what happened, and write what did not.

Where the collection file and the handover note meet

Procurement teams often keep collection references in one place and handover notes in another. That split is workable if the two records point at each other.

A practical approach is to give the handover note the same reference as the collection file, and to add a single line in the collection file that says a handover change exists and where its confirmation is recorded. Neither document needs to duplicate the other. The collection file owns the request; the handover note owns the confirmation trail.

Close the loop without overstating what you have

A confirmation record should end with a clear statement of what it establishes and what it does not.

What it can establish: that a named role received notice of a specific change on a specific date, and that the change is reflected in the file as of that date.

What it cannot establish: that the change was correct, that any material was accepted, or that a later step has been approved. Those are separate decisions with their own records.

Write a closing line in the note that says exactly this, in your own words. It takes a minute and prevents a reader from treating an acknowledgement as an approval.

Finally, set a review date. A handover change that is confirmed but never revisited can drift out of date quietly. A short review entry, even one that says nothing changed, keeps the note alive and shows the file is being maintained rather than abandoned.
