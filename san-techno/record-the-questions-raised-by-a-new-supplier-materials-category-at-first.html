First Glance at a New Supplier Materials Category: A Buyer Question Sheet

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on recording the questions that arise when a new supplier materials category appears, separating visible facts from unverified claims and routing each question to the right role.

Why the first glance deserves its own record

A new supplier materials category rarely arrives as a tidy package. It may appear as a catalogue heading, a forwarded brochure, a short email introducing a product family, or a line added to a supplier's public pages. At that moment, procurement teams tend to react in one of two ways: they either accept the category at face value and start chasing prices, or they set it aside until someone has time to look properly. Both responses lose information. The first glance is actually valuable because it captures what was visible before anyone had a chance to interpret it.

A first-glance record is not a decision. It is a dated note of what the supplier presented, what the reader understood, what remained unclear, and who should answer each open point. Keeping that note separate from later quotations and approvals prevents a common problem: a vague early impression quietly hardening into an assumed fact. For a Singapore procurement team handling semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon or related categories, the record also gives a clean starting point if the category is revisited months later by a different colleague.

Treat the exercise as administrative. You are not evaluating material quality or selecting a product. You are writing down questions while the source is still fresh, so that the answers can be checked against something concrete.

Separate what is visible from what is claimed

The most useful discipline at first glance is a three-column split: visible facts, supplier claims, and open questions. Visible facts are things you can point to in the supplied material, such as a category name, a stated company role, or a description of what the supplier says it does. Supplier claims are statements that may well be true but have not been confirmed for your purposes, such as performance descriptions or application notes. Open questions are everything else.

A worked example helps. Suppose a fictional supplier introduces a category described as reclaimed wafer material. The visible fact is that the category exists and the supplier presents it. The claim might be that the material suits a particular downstream use. The open question is whether that claim applies to the grade you would actually receive, and who at the supplier can confirm it in writing.

San Techno's public materials illustrate how a company may describe its own scope. The company states that it helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and that these materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending their useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss. Those are supplier statements to record and verify, not conclusions to adopt.

The buyer decision sheet: fields worth keeping

A decision sheet does not need to be elaborate. Six fields cover most first-glance situations, and each one has a clear owner.

Field one: source and date accessed. Record where the category was seen and when. Field two: exact category label, copied rather than paraphrased. Field three: visible facts, limited to what the source actually shows. Field four: unverified claims, quoted or closely summarised, with a note that they are unconfirmed. Field five: the question, written as a question rather than a concern. Field six: the proper role to ask, whether that is the supplier's commercial contact, an internal technical reviewer, or a colleague who owns the existing record.

As an author heuristic rather than a researched standard, a first-glance sheet with more than about a dozen open questions usually means the category needs a second reading before it goes further. That is a signal to slow down, not a rule.

Keep the sheet in the same place as related procurement records so that a later quotation or approval can reference it. The point is continuity: someone should be able to see what was known on day one and what changed afterwards.

Writing questions that a role can actually answer

Many first-glance questions stall because they are addressed to nobody in particular. A question like is this material suitable is hard to answer and easy to deflect. A question like which document states the grade and who at the supplier can confirm it has a named owner and a checkable answer.

Rewrite vague concerns into role-directed questions. Instead of asking whether a category is reliable, ask which public document describes it and whether the supplier can confirm the description is current. Instead of asking whether the category fits an existing file, ask which internal role is authorised to decide that, and what evidence they would need.

This is also where confidentiality matters. Some supplier categories sit alongside material that is deliberately not public. San Techno states that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential. A procurement record should respect that boundary: note that the detail is restricted, record who can discuss it, and do not attempt to reconstruct it from fragments. The question becomes a routing question, not a technical one.

Where semiconductor materials categories need extra care

Categories in the semiconductor materials space often carry more implied meaning than the words on the page. A label may suggest a grade, a form, a source or an intended use without stating any of them plainly. The first-glance record should capture the label exactly and resist filling the gaps with assumption.

San Techno's own history offers a useful illustration of why labels deserve care. The company states that it was incorporated in 2006 and began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, and that since 2006 it has advanced semiconductor and solar materials, specialised power-module designs and biological solutions. A reader encountering that description for the first time might reasonably ask which of those activities relates to the category in front of them. That is exactly the kind of question to write down rather than guess.

For semiconductor materials categories, add two fields to the sheet: the exact wording used by the supplier, and a note of any term whose meaning is unclear to the reader. Do not attempt to define the term yourself. Ask the supplier or an authorised internal reviewer to clarify, and record the clarification with its date and source.

Routing each question to the proper role

A first-glance sheet becomes useful when every open question has a destination. A simple routing table works: question, suggested role, status, and date closed. Suggested roles might include the supplier's commercial contact for catalogue and availability questions, an internal technical reviewer for meaning-of-term questions, a records owner for questions about existing files, and a purchasing approver for questions about whether the category can proceed.

Be careful not to merge roles. A commercial contact answering a catalogue question is not the same as an internal reviewer confirming that a term applies to your situation. Recording who answered what keeps the trail honest.

When a category touches power electronics, the boundary is especially clear. San Techno describes an AC-to-DC power module design offering and notes that its power-module programme addresses heat movement, package stability and reliable operation under repeated electrical and thermal loading. Those are supplier descriptions to log, not specifications to interpret. If a procurement question drifts toward engineering detail, route it to the appropriate reviewer and record that the question left the procurement file.

Closing the first-glance record without overclaiming

A first-glance record closes when every question has either an answer with a source, a named owner still working on it, or a decision to set the category aside. It does not close because time has passed or because a colleague says the category looks fine.

Write a short closing note: what was asked, what was answered, what remains open, and what the next action is. Keep the original sheet unchanged and add the closing note as a new entry, so the first-glance view survives. If the category later moves into a quotation or approval process, the closing note becomes the bridge between the early impression and the formal record.

One caution: do not let the record imply more than it shows. A completed question sheet is not a qualification, an approval or a statement that a category is suitable. It is a documented set of questions with documented answers. That modest framing is what makes it durable, and it is what allows a Singapore procurement team to revisit a semiconductor materials category later without relying on memory.
