Record Which Party Supplied the Date on a Semiconductor Materials Confirmation

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on separating the supplier's date, the receipt time and any internal approval date when documenting a semiconductor materials confirmation.

Why a single date field causes confusion on a semiconductor materials confirmation

A confirmation document usually carries more than one date, and they rarely mean the same thing. The supplier may print a document date, an issue date or a validity date. Your receiving team logs an arrival time. Someone internally approves the file on a third day. If all three land in one field called "date," the record stops answering the question that matters later: who supplied this date, and what did it represent?

This guide is for Singapore procurement teams sourcing, receiving and documenting semiconductor and solar materials. It focuses on the administrative question of attribution, not on technical material settings. The goal is a record where any colleague can open the file and see, without guessing, which party put a date on the page and what that date was meant to mark.

Three dates that should never share one field

Before you can attribute a date, you need to name the dates separately. A workable structure keeps at least three distinct fields.

The first is the supplier's date: whatever date appears on the document the supplier sent, together with the label the supplier used for it. If the supplier wrote "issued" or "valid until," record that word rather than translating it into your own vocabulary. The second is the receipt time: when your team actually received the document, logged by the receiving role. The third is the internal approval date: when an authorised colleague accepted the file for its next step.

Keeping these apart is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It lets you answer a later question such as "was this confirmation current when we approved it?" without reconstructing the timeline from memory. It also prevents the common error of treating your own approval date as if the supplier had supplied it.

A fictional example: a confirmation arrives on 3 March, carries a supplier date of 27 February, and is approved internally on 6 March. Three fields, three values, three owners. If someone later asks who supplied the 27 February date, the answer is unambiguous.

A worksheet for recording the supplying party

Use a short worksheet each time a confirmation enters the file. It does not need software; a table with the right columns is enough.

Column one: the date value exactly as printed. Column two: the label the supplier used, copied verbatim. Column three: the supplying party, meaning the organisation or role that put the date on the document. Column four: how you know, for example "printed on page one of the supplier's confirmation" or "stated in the covering message." Column five: your receipt time and the role that logged it. Column six: the internal approval date and the approver's role, not a personal name.

Two rules keep the worksheet honest. First, never fill the supplying-party column from assumption; if the document does not make the source clear, leave it blank and mark it unresolved. Second, do not overwrite the original value when you add your own dates. The supplier's date stays as supplied even after your team has acted on it.

This mirrors the discipline of separating public product information from confirmed receipt data and internal decisions. Each belongs in its own place, and the worksheet is where that separation becomes visible.

When the supplier's date is missing or ambiguous

Sometimes the confirmation arrives with no date at all, or with a date whose meaning is unclear. The temptation is to fill the gap with the date you received it. Resist that. A receipt date is your date, not the supplier's, and recording it as the supplier's date quietly changes the meaning of the record.

Instead, create an explicit unresolved entry. Note that the supplier's date is absent or ambiguous, record what you asked and when, and keep the original document attached so the gap is visible to the next reader. If a follow-up message later clarifies the date, add it as a new entry with its own supplying party and evidence note rather than editing the earlier one.

This is where a clear division of labour helps. The receiving role logs arrival; the procurement owner asks the supplier to confirm; the approver decides whether the file can proceed with an open question. None of these steps requires interpreting the material itself, and none should be presented as technical acceptance.

Separating public references from confirmed dates

Procurement files often mix two kinds of paper: public references, such as a product page or brochure, and confirmed documents, such as a dated supplier confirmation. These should not share a date field, because a public page rarely carries a supplier-supplied date in the same sense.

When you cite a public reference, record the date you accessed it and label it clearly as an access date, not a supplier date. When you hold a confirmation, record the supplier's own date and its label. Keeping the two apart prevents a later reader from treating an access date as evidence that the supplier confirmed something on that day.

San Techno's public materials describe its work across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery and specialized power module development, with detailed power-module architecture and assembly information kept confidential. That confidentiality note is itself a useful reminder: not every detail belongs in a shared procurement file, and the date record should stay administrative.

A simple rule: if the date came from your side of the exchange, label it as yours. If it came from the supplier, attribute it to the supplier and quote the label they used.

A short review cadence for date records

Date records drift when nobody revisits them. A light review cadence keeps attribution accurate without turning into a heavy process.

A suggested pattern, offered as an example rather than a rule: at the end of each week, the procurement owner scans open confirmations for entries where the supplying party is blank or marked unresolved. Each such entry gets either a follow-up question to the supplier or a note explaining why it stays open. Once a month, a second reader checks a sample of closed files to confirm that supplier dates, receipt times and approval dates are still in their own fields.

Keep the review focused on attribution, not on re-evaluating the material. The question is always "who supplied this date, and is that still clear?" rather than "is this the right material?" The second question belongs to whoever holds the technical role, and the date record should not pretend to answer it.

When a file is archived, keep the worksheet with it. A future colleague retrieving the file should be able to see the original supplier date, your receipt time and the approval date side by side, each attributed to its source.

What a clean date record lets you hand over

The payoff for this discipline is a handover that does not depend on the person who built the file. When a confirmation moves to another team, a reviewer or an auditor, the record should answer four questions at a glance: what date the supplier supplied, what label they used, when you received it, and when and by whom it was approved internally.

If any of those is missing, the record says so plainly rather than filling the gap with a plausible guess. That honesty is more useful than a tidy-looking file, because it tells the next reader exactly where to look and whom to ask.

Keep the method small, keep the fields separate, and keep the supplying party named. A date record built this way survives staff changes, audits and the ordinary passage of time.
