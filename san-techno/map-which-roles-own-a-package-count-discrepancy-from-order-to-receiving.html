Mapping Role Ownership for a Package Count Discrepancy from Order to Receiving Log

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on assigning ownership when the package count on an order does not match the receiving log, with a documentation route, role matrix and handoff worksheet for semiconductor materials and related supply files.

Why a package count discrepancy needs a named owner at each step

A package count discrepancy is an administrative event: the number of packages recorded against an order does not match the number recorded at receiving. The temptation is to treat it as a single problem with a single owner. In practice the count passes through several hands, and each hand keeps its own record. The order line, the supplier's dispatch note, the carrier's delivery record and the receiving log can each show a different figure without anyone being wrong about what they saw.

The useful question is not who made a mistake but who owns the next document. That framing keeps the response calm and traceable. It also stops a discrepancy from being closed by memory. For teams sourcing semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon or reclaimed wafer files, the package count is often the first visible signal that something in the paperwork chain needs attention, so the ownership map should exist before a discrepancy appears.

The four record stations from order to receiving log

Think of the discrepancy as travelling through four stations, each with a document and an owner.

Station one is the purchase order. The owner is whoever raised and maintains the order record. Their document states the ordered quantity in packages and the order reference. Station two is the supplier's dispatch or packing note. The owner is the supplier contact who issued it, and internally the buyer who holds that correspondence. Station three is the delivery record at the point of receipt, held by the receiving team. Station four is the receiving log itself, which is the internal record of what was counted and when.

A discrepancy exists when the figures at these stations disagree. The map should record which station each figure came from, not just the figure. A fictional example: order ST-2411-A shows six packages on the purchase order, the dispatch note shows six, the delivery record shows five, and the receiving log shows five. The gap sits between the dispatch note and the delivery record, which points the question at the carrier record rather than the supplier's packing list.

Keeping the stations separate prevents the common failure where one person is asked to explain a number they never recorded.

A role ownership matrix you can fill in before a discrepancy occurs

The matrix below is a template, not a policy. Adapt the role names to your organisation. Each row should have one accountable role and one backup, because a discrepancy logged on a day when the primary owner is away should not stall.

Order record owner: maintains the purchase order and confirms the ordered package count. Supplier correspondence owner: holds the dispatch note and any supplier confirmation. Receiving owner: records the count at the point of receipt and signs the receiving log. Log custodian: keeps the receiving log, controls edits and preserves earlier entries. Escalation owner: decides whether the discrepancy stays open, is referred for clarification, or is closed with a note.

A simple worksheet works: for each row, write the role title, the named person, the backup, the document they own, and the date the matrix was last reviewed. Score each row from one to three on clarity, where three means the owner is obvious from the document alone. Treat that score as an author heuristic for discussion, not as a measured standard. Rows scoring one are the ones to fix first.

Writing the discrepancy entry so the next reader can act

A discrepancy entry that says only that the count was wrong forces the next reader to reconstruct the whole story. A better entry answers five questions in plain text: what was ordered, what was recorded at each station, which station differs, who owns the next action, and what would close the entry.

Use neutral references rather than supplier names where the log is shared widely. A fictional entry might read: order ST-2411-A, ordered six packages, dispatch note six, delivery record five, receiving log five, difference at delivery record, next action with carrier correspondence owner, closure requires a written confirmation or a corrected delivery record. That entry is short, but it tells the next reader exactly where the gap sits and who is expected to move it.

Avoid embedding conclusions in the entry. If the receiving team believes a package was consolidated, that belongs in a separate note field marked as unconfirmed, not in the count field. The count field should hold only recorded figures with their station labels.

This discipline matters most for semiconductor materials files, where a single order reference may later be matched against several internal records. A clean entry keeps that matching possible without reopening the original question.

Handoffs between procurement, receiving and the log custodian

Most discrepancies go quiet at a handoff. The receiving team logs the count, the buyer assumes the log is final, and the log custodian assumes the buyer has already spoken to the supplier. A short handoff rule prevents that.

One workable rule is that the receiving owner notifies the order record owner on the same working day a discrepancy is logged, and the order record owner acknowledges in the log. The acknowledgement is the handoff, not a separate email. A second rule is that only the log custodian edits earlier entries, and any edit carries a date and a reason. A third rule is that the escalation owner reviews open discrepancies at a fixed interval, for example weekly, and records the review date even when nothing changed.

These are suggested practices, not established standards. Choose intervals that fit your team and write them into the matrix so they are visible.

For teams that also handle silicon recovery or wafer reclaim files, the same handoff logic applies to the package count even though the material category differs. Closing the entry without erasing the trail

Closing a discrepancy is an administrative decision that the available evidence is enough for the purpose at hand. It is not a finding that nothing went wrong. The closure note should state what evidence was received, who confirmed it, and what remains unconfirmed.

A fictional closure might read: delivery record corrected to six packages, confirmation received from carrier correspondence owner, receiving log updated by log custodian, no further action. A different closure might read: supplier confirms six packages dispatched, delivery record remains five, entry closed as unresolved with escalation owner notified. Both are legitimate outcomes, and both preserve the trail.

Keep the original figures visible. Overwriting the first recorded count removes the reason the entry existed. If a correction is needed, add a corrected field and leave the original in place with a note.

For context on how a supplier describes its own material and design work, the public product information from San Techno can be referenced as a source, but it should never be used to settle a count question. Counts are settled by the documents at the four stations, not by product descriptions.

A short review checklist for the ownership map

Use this checklist when you review the map, perhaps once a quarter or after a team change. It is a discussion aid, not a compliance test.

Does every station have a named owner and a backup? Is the document each owner holds identified by type rather than by a person's memory? Does the discrepancy entry template ask for the station label beside every figure? Is there a stated handoff rule with a timeframe? Does the log custodian control edits, and are edits dated? Is there an escalation owner who reviews open entries at a fixed interval? Are closure notes required to state what remains unconfirmed?

Score each question from zero to two and treat the total as a prompt for conversation. A low score is not a failure; it is a list of things to clarify.

Finally, keep the map itself under version control. A map that changes silently is no better than a receiving log that changes silently. Record who revised it and when, and keep the previous version available for reference.
