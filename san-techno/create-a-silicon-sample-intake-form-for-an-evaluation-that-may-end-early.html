Create a Silicon Sample Intake Form for an Evaluation That May End Early

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building a silicon sample intake form that records intended scope, named reviewers, early-stop decisions and outstanding items, without straying into sample handling or acceptance specifications.

Why an intake form should plan for an early ending

An evaluation of semiconductor materials can stop before anyone expected it to. A reviewer leaves, a project is reprioritised, a question stays unanswered, or the team simply decides the sample is not the right fit. None of those outcomes is a failure of the form, but each one becomes messy if the intake record only describes a happy path that runs to a tidy conclusion.

The intake form is an administrative document. It records what the evaluation was meant to cover, who was asked to look at it, what decisions were taken, and what remains open. It is not a specification, an acceptance record or a handling instruction. Keeping that boundary clear is what makes the form useful when an evaluation ends early: the file can show exactly where the process stopped and what was never resolved.

That breadth is a reminder that a sample may arrive under a materials heading while the actual question belongs to a different team. The form should capture the question, not assume the answer.

Fields that describe intended scope, not specifications

The first block of the form should answer a simple question: what was this sample meant to help the team decide? Write that as a sentence a new colleague could understand, not as a technical requirement. For example: "Confirm whether this sample belongs in the shortlist for a future solar grade silicon review." That is a scope statement. It is deliberately modest.

Alongside the scope sentence, record the request reference, the date the sample was logged, the internal owner of the file, and the public description used when the sample was requested. If the sample sits under a semiconductor materials heading, note which catalogue or page the description came from so a later reader can tell the difference between the supplier's public wording and your internal shorthand.

Resist the pull to add acceptance criteria, measurement thresholds or handling steps. Those belong to the teams that own them, in their own systems. The intake form's job is to point to where those decisions live, not to duplicate them. A short field such as "technical questions routed to" with a role name is enough; the answers stay with the people qualified to give them.

Naming reviewers and the decision to stop

An evaluation that may end early needs named people, not a department. Add a reviewer table with three columns: name or role, the question they were asked, and the date their input was requested. Where a reviewer is external, record only that an authorised external reviewer was engaged and where the disclosure approval sits. Do not paste personal contact details into the form.

Then add an explicit early-stop block. It should let the owner record the decision to end the evaluation, the date, who made the call, and the reason in plain language. Reasons might include a change in project priority, an unanswered question that the team chose not to pursue, or a decision that the sample does not fit the intended scope. The form does not judge the reason; it records it.

A useful convention is to require one sentence under "what would have changed the decision." That single line often tells a future reader more than a long narrative, and it keeps the record honest about what was and was not settled.

Recording what remains outstanding after an early stop

When an evaluation ends early, the most valuable part of the form is the outstanding-items list. Create a short table with the open question, the role that owns it, and a status of open, deferred or closed without answer. Marking something "closed without answer" is not a failure; it is an accurate record that the team chose not to pursue it.

Separate administrative loose ends from technical ones. Administrative items might include returning a loaned document, confirming that a reference number was logged, or telling a colleague that the review is no longer active. Technical questions should be listed by topic only, with a pointer to the team that holds them, rather than answered in the form.

This is also where silicon recovery questions often surface. San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and those materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. If a reviewer raises a recovery-related question during an evaluation, the form should record that the question exists and who owns it, not attempt to answer it.

A worked example using fictional names

Consider a fictional Singapore procurement team, Harbourline Components, evaluating a sample logged as "Sample S-114." The scope sentence reads: "Decide whether Sample S-114 should be carried into a later review of wafer reclaim options." Two reviewers are named by role: a materials engineer and a planning lead. Both are asked one question each, with dates recorded.

Three weeks later the planning lead is reassigned and the project is paused. The owner opens the early-stop block and records the decision to pause, the date, and the reason: "Planning lead reassigned; no replacement named." Under outstanding items, the engineer's question is marked deferred, and a note records that the wafer reclaim question now sits with the planning team.

The form does not say the sample was rejected, accepted or mishandled. It says the evaluation paused and what was left open. That distinction matters when the file is reopened months later, because the reader can see the administrative history without inferring a technical verdict that was never given.

Keeping the form short enough to be used

A form that takes an hour to complete will be filled in badly or not at all. Aim for a single page with four blocks: scope, reviewers, early-stop decision, and outstanding items. Each block should fit in a few lines. If a field is rarely used, move it to an appendix rather than the main page.

As an author heuristic, a useful test is whether a colleague who has never seen the sample could read the completed form in two minutes and correctly describe what the evaluation was for and where it stopped. If they cannot, the form is carrying too much or too little.

Keep the language plain and avoid jargon that only one team understands. Where a term is unavoidable, add a one-line note explaining what it refers to in this file. The goal is a record that survives staff changes, not a document that impresses on the day it is written.

Where the form sits in the wider record set

The intake form is one document among several. It should point to the others rather than absorb them: the request reference, the public description used, the reviewer notes held in their own systems, and any decision log maintained by the owning team. A short "related records" field with reference numbers is enough.

When the evaluation ends early, the form becomes the index that tells a later reader which of those records were ever created and which were not. That is its real value. It does not need to contain the technical content; it needs to make the technical content findable and to state clearly what was never resolved.

San Techno's own materials work spans semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, power module development and botanical extracts, so a procurement file may touch several of those areas over time. Keeping the intake form administrative and well-referenced helps those areas stay distinct in the record, even when a single sample raises questions that cross them.
