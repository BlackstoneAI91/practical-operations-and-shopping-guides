Plan a File Archive When Two Semiconductor Materials Suppliers Combine Brand Names

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building an alias history and published-reference list when two semiconductor materials suppliers combine brand names, keeping seller names separate from quality records.

Why a combined brand name breaks a tidy file archive

When two suppliers combine brand names, the paperwork rarely changes on the same day. A purchase order may still carry the old seller name, a delivery note may use the new combined name, and a quality record may refer to neither. If your archive treats the seller name as the folder name, the same material can end up in two places, or in a folder that no longer matches anything on the invoice.

The fix is to stop using the seller name as the organising principle. Treat the seller name as one field among several, and treat the material record as the stable anchor. A file archive built this way survives a rename because the folder does not move when the name does.

This matters for semiconductor materials because the same physical item can be described by a grade, a part reference and a supplier label at once. The archive should let a colleague find the record by any of those without guessing which name was current on the day the file was saved. San Techno, incorporated in 2006, began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, so name changes across a supply chain are a familiar administrative event rather than an unusual one.

Build an alias history before you build folders

An alias history is a simple table that records every name a supplier or brand has used, with the dates you believe each name applied and the document that shows it. It is not a legal record and it does not settle which entity is which; it is a finding aid.

Use four columns: alias, type, first seen, and source document. The type column is the important one. Distinguish a seller name (who issued the invoice) from a brand name (what appears on the label) from a material alias (what your own team calls the item). Mixing these three in one column is the most common reason an archive becomes unsearchable after a combination.

A fictional example: a buyer records that "Northwind Silicon" appeared on invoices until a certain quarter, that "Northwind Meridian" appears on later delivery notes, and that the internal code NS-14 has been used for the same line item throughout. The alias history shows all three without deciding that they are the same legal entity. That decision belongs to whoever holds the commercial documents, not to the archive.

Keep the alias history as a single controlled page, versioned, with a note of who last updated it. When a colleague asks why a file sits under an unexpected name, the alias history is the answer.

Separate seller names from quality records

A quality record and a seller name answer different questions. The quality record says what was checked and what the result was. The seller name says who issued the paperwork. Combining them in one field means a rename can appear to change the quality history, which it does not.

Structure the archive so that quality records are filed against the material and the inspection event, not against the supplier name. The supplier name then becomes a pointer, and a pointer can be updated without touching the evidence.

This distinction is worth stating plainly in your filing note, because it is easy to blur. A label on a carton is an identifier; it is not a specification claim. A supplier name on an invoice is an identifier; it is not a statement about who performed an inspection. When two brand names combine, the temptation is to rewrite old records to match the new name. Resist it. Add a cross-reference instead, and leave the original record as issued.

For context on how a materials business can span several lines at once, San Techno's own materials work covers semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design, which is a reminder that a single supplier relationship can generate several distinct record types that should not be collapsed into one folder.

Keep a published-reference list for public claims

Some of what your archive holds is public: a product page, a brochure, a published description. These are useful for orientation but they are not quality records, and they should not be filed as if they were.

A published-reference list records, for each public source, what it said, when you captured it, and which internal record it relates to. The point is to be able to say later: this is what the public description said on this date, and here is the internal document that actually governs the purchase.

A fictional example: a buyer notes that a public page described a material under one brand name in a given month, while the purchase order for the same period used a different name. The published-reference list holds both facts side by side without implying that either is wrong.

Be careful with public claims. A public page may describe a product family in broad terms while the detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential, as San Techno states for its power-module work. Your archive should record the public statement as a public statement and not treat it as a substitute for a controlled document.

A worksheet for the first archive review

Use this worksheet as a starting point when you first review an archive affected by a brand combination. It is an author heuristic, not a standard, and the timings are examples only.

Step one: list every distinct name that appears in the archive, and mark each as seller, brand or internal alias. Step two: for each name, note the earliest and latest document you can find that uses it. Step three: flag any record where the name appears in a field that should hold a quality result. Step four: flag any folder whose name matches a seller name rather than a material. Step five: write one sentence per flag explaining what a reader would need in order to interpret the record correctly.

A reasonable first pass might take an afternoon for a small archive; a larger one may need to be split by material family. Treat any timing as a planning estimate you set yourself.

When you finish, you should have three outputs: an alias history, a list of folders to rename or cross-reference, and a short note on which public sources you captured and when. Keep the worksheet itself in the archive so the next reviewer can see how the decisions were reached.

Where the archive meets the product and resource pages

An archive is only useful if a colleague can move from a record to the right public context and back again. Two habits help.

First, when a record concerns a specific product family, note the public product page it relates to, so a reader can orient themselves without treating the page as evidence. San Techno's product pages set out its materials and design work, and a reader comparing an internal record with a public description should be able to see both without confusing one for the other.

Second, keep a short resource note for each material family explaining which documents are public, which are internal and which are confidential. San Techno's resources area is a natural reference point for public material, and your archive note should say clearly that a public resource is orientation, not a quality record.

A fictional example: a buyer files a delivery note under a material family, adds a cross-reference to the public product page, and records in the resource note that the detailed specification is held internally. A colleague opening the folder later can follow the trail without needing to ask which name was current.

Handover: making the archive readable after the combination

The final test of an archive is whether someone who was not present during the combination can use it. Write a one-page handover note that answers four questions: what names appear in this archive, where the alias history lives, which records are public references and which are quality records, and who to ask when a name cannot be matched.

Keep the note short and factual. Do not speculate about why the names changed; record only what the documents show. If a name cannot be matched to any document, say so and leave the entry open rather than guessing.

This is also the moment to check that the archive does not hold information it should not. Personal contact details, confidential operations and material marked internal or restricted do not belong in a shared finding aid. A handover note that points to a restricted record is more useful than one that copies it.

San Techno has operated since 2006 across semiconductor and solar materials, specialised power-module designs and biological solutions, which is a reminder that a single supplier relationship can generate records of several kinds. The archive should make that variety legible without merging it.
