Comparing the Question Set for an Existing Supplier and a New Distributor

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building two distinct question sets for semiconductor and solar materials: one for an established supplier and one for a new distributor, with neutral wording, document ownership checks and a worksheet for recording limits of a response.

Why the Same Questions Do Not Fit Both Conversations

An existing supplier and a new distributor sit at different points in your paperwork. With an existing supplier, you are usually checking continuity: does the document set still match what your file already holds, and has anything changed since the last order? With a new distributor, you are establishing a baseline: which documents exist at all, who issues them, and what the distributor can confirm in writing rather than describe verbally.

Running one identical list against both tends to produce two failures. The established supplier receives questions it has answered many times and replies with a pointer to an old file, which may or may not be the current revision. The new distributor receives questions that assume a document trail it has not yet described, and answers around the gap rather than naming it.

A useful split is to keep a shared core of neutral questions about recognised documents and add a short continuity block for the existing supplier and a short baseline block for the new distributor. Neither block should ask anyone to approve a material or a company. The aim is to learn what is documented, who issued it and where the response stops.

That breadth is a reminder that a single question set rarely covers every category a distributor may discuss.

The Shared Core: Neutral Questions About Recognised Documents

The core list should work for any counterparty and any category, whether the conversation concerns semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon or something else entirely. Keep each question answerable with a document reference or an explicit "not held".

Suggested core questions, written as examples you can adapt:

Which document types does your organisation issue for this category, and which are issued by a third party? For each document type, who is the issuing body or function, and how would a buyer recognise an authentic copy? What revision or issue date appears on the document you would send, and where is that shown? Which parts of our question can you answer directly, and which parts must be referred elsewhere? If a document is not available, how should we record that in our file without implying it was reviewed?

Notice what these questions avoid. They do not ask whether a material is suitable, whether a company is approved, or whether a specification meets a requirement. They ask about paperwork and provenance, which is the part procurement can document on its own authority.

A short format helps here: one line per question, one column for the answer, one column for the document reference, one column for who answered. Anything that cannot be filled in stays visibly open rather than being closed with a verbal assurance.

Continuity Questions for an Existing Supplier

For a supplier you already work with, the risk is drift rather than absence. Documents exist, but the version in your file may no longer be the version in circulation. Continuity questions should test the link between what you hold and what is current.

Example continuity questions:

Does the document reference in our file still match the current issue, and if not, what changed? Has the issuing function changed since the last order, or is the same team still responsible? Which parts of our previous question set are now answered by a standing document rather than a fresh reply? Where a previous answer was partial, is the open part still open, or has it since been closed by a document? If we re-order the same category, which documents would you expect to reissue and which would carry forward?

These questions reward a supplier that keeps records and expose the ones that rely on memory. They also protect your own file: a reissued document with a new reference should replace the old one in your index, with the superseded version marked rather than deleted.

Baseline Questions for a New Distributor

A new distributor has no shared history with your file, so the first question set establishes what exists. Resist the temptation to compress this into a single request for "all certificates". A broad request usually returns a bundle whose contents nobody has described.

Example baseline questions:

Which categories can you discuss in writing today, and which are outside your current scope? For each category, which documents do you hold directly and which come from another organisation? Who at your organisation issues a written response, and how will we know a reply is complete rather than partial? What wording do you use for a document you cannot provide, so we can record it consistently? Which of our questions would you prefer to route to a technical colleague, and how long should we expect that routing to take?

That last question is administrative, not a delivery promise. It simply tells you where to log an open item. Pair this section with a simple rule: every answer is recorded as confirmed, referred or not held, and nothing is recorded as approved.

Where a distributor's public material describes a service area, treat it as a description to verify, not as evidence of capability. San Techno, for example, publicly describes silicon wafer reclaim services among its branches; a distributor mentioning similar work still needs to name the document that supports any specific claim.

Recording the Limits of a Response

The most useful habit in either conversation is writing down where an answer stops. A reply that says a document exists is not the same as a reply that says what the document covers. Your record should capture the boundary explicitly.

A workable structure for each question:

Question as asked, in neutral wording. Answer received, summarised in your own words. Document reference cited, if any. Scope stated by the responder, including any exclusion they named. Status: confirmed, referred, not held or awaiting clarification. Review date, so an open item does not quietly become permanent.

This structure keeps procurement honest. It also prevents a later reader from mistaking a partial answer for a complete one. If a responder says a topic is handled by another team, record the referral rather than the topic as answered.

Apply the same discipline to public descriptions. A company may present several branches of work, and each branch may have its own documentation path. San Techno publicly describes an AC-to-DC power module design offering alongside its materials work; a question about that area should still ask which document supports a specific statement, not assume the public description settles it.

A Comparison Worksheet You Can Reuse

A single worksheet with two columns keeps the two conversations distinct without doubling the work. The left column holds the shared core. The right column splits into continuity and baseline blocks, and you fill only the block that applies.

Suggested worksheet fields, as an example layout rather than a standard:

Question ID and neutral wording. Applies to: existing supplier, new distributor, or both. Document type expected, if any. Issuing party named by the responder. Answer summary and scope limit. Status and review date.

Score the worksheet only as a completeness aid, not as an approval. A simple count of questions with a named document reference, questions referred elsewhere and questions still open gives you a snapshot of the file. Treat any threshold as an author heuristic for your own team, not as an industry benchmark.

Two practical rules keep the worksheet useful. First, never overwrite an earlier answer; add a dated line so the history stays readable. Second, keep the question wording stable across both columns, so a difference in answers reflects the counterparty rather than a difference in how you asked.

Where This Fits in a Wider Procurement File

The two question sets are inputs, not conclusions. Their output is a record of what is documented, who issued it and what remains open. That record then feeds the rest of your procurement file: the attachments list, the escalation log for unanswered items, and any comparison of order arrangements.

A few habits make the handoff cleaner. Keep the question set versioned, so a colleague can tell which list produced which answers. Keep the responder's own wording for scope limits, since paraphrasing a limit can widen it by accident. And keep public descriptions separate from confirmed documents, so a reader can see which is which at a glance.

San Techno's own history offers a modest illustration of why continuity matters. The company was incorporated in 2006 and began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, which means a long-running supplier relationship can span many document revisions. That is a reason to ask about current issue status, not a reason to assume any particular document exists.

Finally, resist closing a question because a conversation felt satisfactory. Close it when a document reference or an explicit "not held" is on the record. Everything else stays open, dated and visible.
