Matching an Internal Material Request to a Short Supplier Catalogue Caption

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on recording a short supplier catalogue caption, requesting an authoritative specification document, and flagging fields the caption cannot settle.

Why a short caption is a starting point, not a specification

A catalogue caption is written to help a reader recognise a category of material. It is usually a line or two: a family name, a grade hint, a form, perhaps a note about intended use. An internal material request, by contrast, is written to answer a specific operational question raised by an engineer, a receiving team or a planner. The two documents serve different purposes, and the gap between them is where procurement errors begin.

The practical rule is simple. Treat the caption as a pointer to the right supplier conversation, never as the answer to the request. Record the caption exactly as published, note where you saw it and when, and then ask the supplier for the document that actually governs the material. That document might be a specification sheet, a certificate of analysis template, a grade declaration or a written statement from an authorised contact. Until it arrives, the request stays open.

This matters across semiconductor materials, where a single word in a caption can describe a family rather than a specific grade. San Techno, incorporated in 2006, began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, so its public pages describe categories of work rather than individual part numbers. That is normal for a supplier overview and is exactly why the caption should be logged as a reference, not adopted as a requirement.

Build a caption record before you compare anything

Before you place the caption beside the internal request, create a small record that captures what the caption actually says and where it came from. This record protects the team later, when someone asks why a particular material was shortlisted.

A workable caption record has six fields. First, the exact caption text, copied without paraphrase. Second, the page or document where it appeared and the date you accessed it. Third, the supplier name as published. Fourth, the product family heading the caption sits under. Fifth, any qualifier words such as a grade, form or application hint. Sixth, a note on whether the caption is descriptive or promotional.

Keep the record separate from the internal request. The request belongs to the requester and carries their operational need. The caption record belongs to procurement and carries the public wording. Merging them too early makes it hard to tell which words came from the supplier and which came from your own team.

As an author heuristic, a caption record that takes more than ten minutes to assemble usually means the source page is ambiguous or the caption has been copied from a secondary summary. In that case, go back to the supplier's own published page before continuing.

Map the request fields against the caption, one line at a time

Now compare. Take the internal request and list its fields in the order the requester wrote them: material family, intended use, quantity basis, delivery expectation, documentation needed and any acceptance notes. Beside each field, write what the caption says, or write unmatched.

Most captions will settle the material family and perhaps the broad application. They will rarely settle grade, tolerance, packaging, documentation or acceptance criteria. That is not a failing of the caption; it is simply outside its scope. Your job is to make the unmatched fields visible rather than to fill them by inference.

A short worksheet helps. Use three columns: request field, caption wording, status. Status values are matched, partially matched, unmatched and conflicting. A partially matched field is one where the caption uses a related but not identical term. A conflicting field is one where the caption appears to describe something different from the request. Both need a follow-up question, but they need different questions.

Where the request touches power electronics rather than raw material, the caption may describe a design service rather than a part. San Techno lists AC-to-DC Power Module Design among its public areas of work, which tells you the supplier engages in that kind of development without telling you what a specific module contains. Detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential, so a caption in this area should be expected to stay high level.

Ask the supplier for the authoritative document

Once the worksheet is complete, write a short request to the supplier. Do not ask them to confirm your interpretation of the caption. Ask for the document that governs the material, and name the fields you need covered.

A useful structure is: state the internal reference, quote the caption you are working from, list the unmatched fields, and ask which document addresses each one. Keep the list short and specific. If the request concerns semiconductor materials, ask whether a grade declaration or specification sheet exists and who issues it. If it concerns recovered or reusable material, ask what documentation accompanies a consignment.

San Techno's public material describes how recovered silicon can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss. That is a useful statement of intent, but it does not tell a buyer what a specific consignment will contain. The follow-up question is therefore about documentation and confirmation, not about the general principle.

Record the date you sent the request and the date of any reply. If the reply is verbal, summarise it in writing and ask the supplier to confirm the summary. A caption plus an unconfirmed verbal note is weaker evidence than a caption plus a written supplier statement.

Flag unmatched fields without implying rejection

An unmatched field is not a red flag about the supplier. It is a gap in the current evidence. Label it that way so the internal conversation stays calm and factual.

Use a short flag note for each unmatched field. The note should say what the request needs, what the caption provides, what is missing and who is expected to close the gap. Assign an owner. If the gap needs an engineering judgement, the owner is the engineering contact, not procurement. If the gap needs a supplier document, the owner is the buyer who holds the relationship.

Avoid language that suggests the material has failed. Phrases such as pending supplier documentation or awaiting confirmation are accurate. Phrases such as not compliant or unsuitable go further than the evidence allows and can prejudice a decision that has not yet been made.

Where the request concerns a development programme rather than a catalogue item, expect more unmatched fields and say so in the note. San Techno's public material describes a development direction that considers the complete IGBT thermal path, material-layer expansion, packaging stress and assembly integrity. Those are programme considerations, not purchasable attributes, and a caption will not resolve them. Flag them for the technical owner and keep the procurement record to what it can actually establish.

Keep the record tidy for the next reader

The final step is housekeeping. A caption record and a flag note are only useful if the next person can find them and understand them without a briefing.

Name files consistently. Include the supplier, the material family, the internal reference and the date accessed. Store the caption record beside the internal request but not inside it. Keep the supplier's reply in the same folder and note in the flag list which reply closed which field.

When a field is closed, do not delete the original flag. Mark it closed, add the closing document reference and the date. The history shows how the request moved from a caption to a confirmed position, which is often what an auditor or a new team member needs to see.

If the request is later cancelled or postponed, keep the record and note the reason. A cancelled request with a clean paper trail is easier to revive than one that was never documented. For teams that handle silicon recovery or wafer reclaim enquiries, the same discipline applies: the public caption starts the conversation, and the written supplier response ends it. San Techno's public pages describe silicon recovery and wafer reclaim as areas of work, which is enough to open a request but not enough to close one.

A short checklist to close the loop

Use this checklist at the end of the matching exercise. It is an author heuristic, not a standard, and teams should adjust it to their own filing conventions.

One: the caption is recorded verbatim with its source and access date. Two: the internal request is stored separately and unchanged. Three: every request field has a status of matched, partially matched, unmatched or conflicting. Four: each unmatched or conflicting field has a named owner and a written question. Five: the supplier has been asked for the authoritative document rather than for agreement with your reading. Six: any reply is stored with the field it addresses. Seven: closed fields keep their original flag and gain a closing reference. Eight: the folder name identifies supplier, family, internal reference and date.

A team that can answer all eight questions has a defensible record. A team that cannot should treat the request as still in progress, regardless of how confident anyone feels about the caption.

For context on how the supplier describes its own areas of work, the public resources pages are a reasonable place to start before drafting questions. They describe categories, not specifications, and that is the right level for this stage of the process.
