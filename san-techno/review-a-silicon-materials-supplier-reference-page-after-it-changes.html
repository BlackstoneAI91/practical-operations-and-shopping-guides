Reviewing a Silicon Materials Supplier Reference Page After It Changes Address

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on recording an old supplier reference, confirming the current public source, and marking internal files for review when a silicon materials supplier page moves.

Why a moved reference page matters to a buying team

A supplier reference page is one of those documents that sits quietly in the background until someone needs it. A buyer opens a saved bookmark to check a product family, a receiving clerk follows a link from an old purchase pack, or an auditor asks which public page supported a description in a file. If the address has changed, the link may fail, redirect somewhere unexpected, or land on a page that no longer says what the old note assumed.

The temptation is to fix the link and move on. A more careful approach treats the change as a small records event: capture what the old reference was, confirm what the current public source actually says, and flag internal files that quoted the old page so a person can review them. That sequence keeps the paper trail honest without pretending the supplier has changed anything about its services.

This guide is written for Singapore procurement teams who source, receive and document semiconductor and solar materials. It is administrative in focus. It does not tell you whether a material is suitable, and it does not assume that a new page address means new products, new availability or new terms. San Techno Pte Ltd, a Singapore-based company working across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, is used here as the named brand context. Nothing in this guide should be read as a statement that any particular service is currently offered beyond what the public pages say.

Step one: record the old reference before you touch anything

Before you update a bookmark or a shared link, write down what the old reference was. This is the single most useful habit in the whole process, because once a link is edited the previous state is easy to lose.

A simple old-reference record can hold: the address as it appeared, the date you last used it successfully, the page title you saw, the internal documents that cite it, and who owns each of those documents. Keep it in the same place your team keeps other procurement reference notes, and give it a version number so later edits are visible.

A worked example, entirely fictional: a buyer saved a supplier reference page in a shared folder in March. In August the link stopped resolving. The buyer records the old address, the March date, the page title, and the three internal files that mention it: a comparison sheet, a receiving note template and a training slide. Only then does the buyer look for the current page.

This ordering matters because it separates two questions that are easy to blur. The first is what we relied on. The second is what the supplier says now. Keeping them apart means a later reviewer can see exactly which internal statement rested on which public page, and can decide whether the statement still holds.

One caution: do not overwrite the old record with the new address. Add the new address as a separate field with its own date. If a colleague later asks why a file changed, the record shows both states rather than only the latest one.

Step two: confirm the current public source

Once the old reference is safely recorded, confirm the current public page. Open it directly rather than relying on a redirect, and check that you have landed on the page you expected. A redirect can be convenient, but it can also hide the fact that a page has been reorganised.

When you confirm the current source, note the page title, the date you checked it, and the main headings you can see. If the page groups offerings by theme, record those themes in your own words rather than copying blocks of text into an internal file. For San Techno, the public product page presents the work as three branches under one materials vision, covering semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design. That structure is a useful thing to note because it tells a reader how the supplier organises its public information, not because it confirms any specific order, price or availability.

It also helps to check the surrounding public pages so your internal note points to the right place. The resources page and the about page may carry context that the product page does not, and a reader following your note later will appreciate a pointer to the page that actually answers their question. Where the public pages describe a service in general terms, keep your internal summary general too. For example, the public material notes that San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and that these materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. Those are public statements about the supplier's work; they are not a confirmation that a particular batch, grade or quantity is available to you.

If the current page says less than the old one did, record that gap rather than filling it from memory. A missing statement is information too.

Step three: mark internal files for review, not for silent editing

The third step is where most of the risk sits. Internal files that quoted the old page now need a human to look at them, but they should not be quietly rewritten. Marking a file for review is different from correcting it.

A workable review mark has four parts: the file identifier, the reason for review (the reference page moved), the person who will review it, and a status such as open, reviewed or no change needed. Keep the mark visible in the file itself or in a small register, so a colleague opening the file knows it is under review and does not treat it as final.

A fictional example: a comparison sheet cites the old supplier page in a footnote. The reviewer opens the current page, confirms that the general description still matches, and updates only the footnote address and the checked date. The comparison itself is left alone because the reviewer is not qualified to re-assess the material. A second file, a training slide, is marked no change needed because it never quoted the page directly.

Two rules keep this step clean. First, the reviewer should be the owner of the file, not whoever noticed the broken link. Second, any change to a statement about the supplier should be traceable to a specific public page and a specific date. If a reviewer cannot point to the public wording that supports a change, the change should wait.

This is also the moment to separate administrative review from technical or commercial decisions. A moved page does not by itself change a specification, a quote or an approval. Those questions belong to the people who own them, and the review mark should say so plainly rather than implying that a link fix settles anything.

A short worksheet for the review

The following worksheet is an author heuristic, offered as an example rather than a standard. Adapt the fields to your own filing conventions.

Field one: old reference. Record the address as it appeared, the last successful use date and the page title. Field two: current source. Record the address you confirmed, the date checked and the main headings. Field three: affected files. List each internal file that cites the old page, with its owner. Field four: review status. Mark each file open, reviewed or no change needed, with initials and a date. Field five: open questions. Note anything the current page does not answer, and route it to the right owner rather than guessing.

A worked fictional pass might look like this. Old reference recorded on a Tuesday. Current source confirmed the same afternoon, with three headings noted. Four affected files listed, two marked reviewed with only the footnote address changed, one marked no change needed, one left open because the reviewer is on leave. One open question logged about whether a general service description still applies to a specific enquiry, routed to the commercial owner.

Scoring is optional, but if your team likes a simple signal, a three-point scale works: complete when every affected file has a status, partial when some files are still open, and blocked when the current source cannot be confirmed. Treat the score as a prompt for follow-up, not as evidence of anything about the supplier.

Keep the worksheet short enough that people actually fill it in. A long form that nobody completes is worse than a five-field note that everyone uses.

What to do when the current page is unclear or incomplete

Sometimes the current page is confirmed but does not answer the question that sent you there. Perhaps a heading has been renamed, or a general description has been trimmed. The right response is to record the gap and ask, not to reconstruct the old wording from memory or from a cached copy.

Record the gap in the open-questions field of your worksheet, name the owner who should follow up, and set a review date. If the question is commercial, route it to the commercial contact. If it is about how a material is described, route it to whoever owns the specification record. Keep the question neutral: ask what the current public position is, rather than asserting what it used to be.

It also helps to distinguish between a page that has moved and a page that has changed in substance. A move is an address change. A substantive change is a difference in what the page says. Your old-reference record lets you tell the two apart, because you can compare the recorded title and headings with what you see now. If the substance has changed, say so in the review mark and let the file owner decide what to do.

Where the public pages describe confidential areas, respect that boundary. San Techno's public material notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential. A procurement reviewer should not press for that detail through a reference-page review, and should not record speculation about it in an internal file. The same restraint applies to any information marked internal or restricted on your own side.

Keeping the record useful over time

A reference-page review is only worth doing if the record stays findable. A few small habits help.

Name the record consistently, for example with the supplier name, the words reference page and the review year, so it sorts alongside similar notes. Store it where the team already looks for procurement references rather than in a personal folder. Add a next-review date, even if it is only an annual prompt, so the record does not quietly age out.

When a later review happens, add a new dated entry rather than replacing the old one. Over time the record becomes a short history of what the team relied on and when it checked, which is exactly what a colleague needs when a question arises months later.

Finally, keep the scope modest. The purpose of this exercise is to make sure internal files point to a current public source and that any change is traceable. It is not a re-evaluation of a supplier, a re-approval of a material, or a substitute for the commercial and technical checks your team already runs. San Techno's public pages describe a company that has worked in semiconductor and solar materials since 2006, and that history is useful context for a reader trying to understand the supplier's public identity. It does not, on its own, tell you anything about a specific order. Treat the reference page as one small, well-kept part of a larger procurement record, and it will do its job.
