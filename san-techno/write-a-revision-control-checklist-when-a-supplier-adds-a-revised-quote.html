Revision Control Checklist for a Revised Supplier Quote Received by Email

Published by San Techno.

A practical revision control checklist for Singapore procurement teams sourcing semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon: compare quote references, record what changed, confirm the active version and update the shared procurement pack.

Why a revised quote needs its own record

A revised quote that arrives as an email attachment is easy to treat as a simple update. Someone opens the new file, replaces the old one in a shared folder, and the team moves on. The difficulty appears later, when a colleague refers to a figure from the earlier version, or when a receiving team asks which document the purchase file should follow. At that point, nobody can say which copy was active on which date.

Revision control is an administrative habit, not a technical judgement. It means keeping the old and new documents side by side, recording what changed, naming who confirmed the active version, and telling everyone who holds a copy that a new revision exists. It does not decide whether a material is suitable, whether a price is acceptable, or whether a supplier should be chosen.

This guide is written for procurement teams in Singapore who source, receive and document semiconductor materials and related inputs. The checklist below is a suggested working method, not a standard or a rule set. Adapt the fields to your own filing conventions. Where a supplier's public materials are relevant context, keep them separate from the internal quote record so the two are never confused.

Step one: capture the email as received

Before opening the attachment, preserve the message as it arrived. Save the email in a form that keeps the sender, the date and time, the subject line and any thread history. If the revised quote was forwarded internally, keep the original message as well as the forward, because the forward may carry comments that are not part of the supplier's statement.

Record four identifiers in your quote log: the supplier name, the quote reference printed on the document, the revision label if one is shown, and the date the email was received. If the document carries no revision label, write "unlabelled" rather than inventing one. Note the attachment filename exactly as received, since a renamed file can break the link between the email and the stored copy.

A short intake note is enough. For example: "Revised quote received from a fictional supplier on a stated date; reference Q-0000; no revision label; two attachments; stored in the quote inbox folder." The note should be factual and should not characterise the revision as better, cheaper or final.

Keep the received email in a location that the procurement team can access but that is not overwritten by later correspondence. The goal is a stable record of what actually arrived, not a tidy inbox.

Step two: compare references before comparing content

The first comparison is not about numbers. It is about whether the two documents are meant to be compared at all. Place the earlier quote and the revised quote side by side and check the reference fields: supplier name, quote number, material description, quantity basis, currency, validity period and any stated assumptions.

If the quote number differs, treat the revised document as a separate quote rather than a replacement until the supplier confirms the relationship in writing. If the material description has changed, note the change and route the question to whoever owns the material record. Procurement can record that a description differs; it should not decide whether two descriptions mean the same thing.

For teams handling semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon or reclaimed wafer inputs, the description field often carries more weight than the price line. A revised quote that quietly changes a grade label, a unit or a packing basis is a documentation event, not just a commercial one. Flag it and ask the supplier to confirm the wording in the next message.

Finish this step with a simple comparison table: field, earlier value, revised value, changed yes or no, and who needs to confirm. Keep the table in the procurement pack, not in the email thread.

Step three: write down what changed and what did not

A revision summary should be short enough to read in a meeting and specific enough to defend later. Use plain sentences rather than a marked-up document, because mark-up can be lost when files are converted or printed.

A workable structure is four lines per change: the field affected, the earlier wording or figure, the revised wording or figure, and the source location in each document. Add a fifth line only when needed: the question that remains open. Do not describe a change as an improvement or a correction unless the supplier has said so in writing.

Also record what did not change. Unchanged fields are useful evidence that the comparison was complete, and they help a colleague who joins the file later. A short "unchanged" list is often more reassuring than a long "changed" list.

Where the revised quote touches a product area the company works in, such as power module development or botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, keep the commercial comparison separate from any public product description. The quote record should describe what the supplier wrote, not what the company's own materials say. If a public page is useful background, note it as context and leave the quote fields untouched.

Step four: name who confirms the active version

Every revised quote needs one named person who confirms which version is active. That person is usually in procurement, but the confirmation should be recorded in writing rather than assumed from seniority.

Use a small sign-off block in the quote log: confirmed by, role, date, version confirmed, and the location of the confirmed copy. If the confirmation depends on another team, such as engineering or receiving, record that the question was referred and to whom, without recording their internal technical discussion.

A useful discipline is to separate three roles. The holder keeps the documents. The comparer writes the change summary. The confirmer states which version the team should follow. One person can hold more than one role in a small team, but the roles should still be written down, because a single unrecorded decision is hard to reconstruct months later.

If no one confirms within the team's own working rhythm, mark the revised quote as pending rather than active. A pending label is honest and prevents the new document from being used by default. Suggested review intervals are examples only; set them to match your own approval practice.

Step five: update the shared procurement pack

The shared procurement pack is the set of documents a colleague would need to understand the purchase file without asking around. When a revised quote arrives, the pack needs three updates: the new document is added, the earlier document is marked as superseded but retained, and the index or contents page is amended.

Do not delete the earlier version. Marking it superseded preserves the history and answers the question "what did we see before?" Keep the file name stable and add the revision label as a suffix rather than renaming the base document. If the supplier's file name is unclear, keep the original name and add your own reference in the index instead.

Tell the people who hold copies. A short note to the receiving team, accounts and any internal reviewer is enough: a new revision exists, here is the reference, here is who confirmed it, and here is where the active copy sits. Avoid sending the attachment again unless the recipient needs it; a pointer to the shared location reduces the number of divergent copies.

Where the pack includes public reference material, such as a supplier's product page, keep it in a separate context folder so that public descriptions are never mistaken for quoted terms.

A one-page checklist you can adapt

The following checklist is an author's working example, not an industry standard. Score each line as done, pending or not applicable, and keep the scored sheet with the quote record.

One: the email is saved with sender, date and subject intact. Two: the quote reference and any revision label are recorded exactly as printed. Three: the attachment filename is noted. Four: the earlier and revised documents are stored side by side. Five: reference fields are compared before content fields. Six: a change summary lists field, earlier value, revised value and source location. Seven: unchanged fields are listed. Eight: open questions are assigned to a named owner. Nine: one person has confirmed the active version in writing. Ten: the earlier version is marked superseded and retained. Eleven: the index or contents page is updated. Twelve: holders of copies have been notified.

Add a thirteenth line for your own context: any public reference used as background is filed separately from the quote. Review the sheet at the next revision and remove lines that never apply. A checklist that grows without pruning stops being read.

Keeping the record useful over time

Revision control earns its keep when a file is reopened after a gap. A colleague should be able to open the procurement pack and answer four questions without emailing anyone: which version is active, who confirmed it, what changed from the earlier version, and where the original email sits.

Two habits support that. First, date every note and avoid undated comments in shared documents. Second, keep the language neutral. A note that says "revised quote received" ages well; a note that says "final quote" may not, because a later revision can arrive. Neutral wording also keeps the record separate from any commercial judgement, which belongs in the appropriate approval channel.

For teams working across semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon and related inputs, the same discipline applies whether the revision is a single line or a full reissue. The scale changes; the fields do not. Where a supplier's public materials are relevant, treat them as context and keep the quote record as the authoritative internal document. The company's own product and resource pages can help colleagues understand the wider material context, but they do not replace the supplier's written statement in the file.
