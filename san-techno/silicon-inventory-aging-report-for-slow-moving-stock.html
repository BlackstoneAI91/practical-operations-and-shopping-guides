Silicon Inventory Aging Report for Slow-Moving Stock: A Practical Layout for Singapore Procurement Teams

Published by San Techno.

A guide for Singapore procurement teams on building an aging report for slow-moving silicon inventory, grouping lots by age band, flagging review thresholds, and deciding what to do with each band.

Why slow-moving silicon needs its own aging report

Silicon stock does not age the way a consumable does. A sealed cassette of wafers can sit on a shelf for a long time and still look identical on the outside, which is exactly why a written aging report matters more than a visual walk-through. The report is not a judgement about quality. It is a scheduling tool: it tells a procurement team which lots have stopped moving, how long they have been still, and which ones are close enough to a review threshold that someone should make a decision this month rather than next quarter.

Singapore procurement teams sourcing, receiving and documenting semiconductor and solar materials usually already hold the raw data. Goods receipt dates, lot references, storage locations and last-issue dates sit in an ERP or a spreadsheet. What is often missing is a single view that sorts those lots by age and pairs each band with a named follow-up. That is the gap this guide addresses.

One useful piece of context: San Techno has worked with semiconductor and solar materials since its incorporation in 2006, initially as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials. That background is relevant only in the sense that slow-moving stock is a normal feature of a materials business, not an anomaly. The report described here is an internal document; it does not depend on any vendor's system.

Keep the scope tight. An aging report covers stock you already own or hold. It is not a purchasing forecast, not a quality certificate, and not a substitute for the receiving inspection record. Its job is to surface decisions.

Choosing the age bands and the review threshold

Age bands should match how your team actually thinks about stock, not a generic template. A workable starting set for silicon inventory is four bands: 0 to 90 days, 91 to 180 days, 181 to 365 days, and over 365 days. These are examples, not a standard. A team holding fast-moving production stock may prefer 30-day bands; a team holding reference or evaluation material may prefer annual bands.

The review threshold is the more important number. It is the age at which a lot stops being 'in stock' and becomes 'needs a decision'. Many teams set it at the start of the third band, so a lot crossing 181 days triggers a review. Others set it earlier for high-value material and later for low-value material. Whatever you choose, write it down in the report header so the reader knows what the flag means.

Two rules keep the bands honest. First, measure age from the goods receipt date, not from the purchase order date, because the clock that matters is time in your custody. Second, do not reset the age when a lot moves between storage locations. A relocation is not a new receipt, and treating it as one hides exactly the slow-moving stock the report exists to find.

If a lot has been partially issued, report the remaining quantity against the original receipt date. Splitting a lot into a fresh sub-lot with a new date is a common way aging reports quietly lose their meaning.

The report layout: columns that earn their place

Aging reports fail when they carry too many columns. Each column should either identify a lot, date it, or point to a decision. A practical layout uses these fields:

Lot reference. The identifier used on the receiving record and the storage label. If these two do not match, fix that before running the report.

Material description. Enough to distinguish semiconductor materials from solar grade silicon and from other stock, without reproducing a full datasheet.

Quantity and unit. State the unit explicitly, because cassettes, boxes and pieces are not interchangeable.

Storage location. The physical place the lot can be found, so a reviewer can verify it without a search.

Goods receipt date. The anchor for every age calculation.

Last issue date. Blank if the lot has never moved. A blank here is itself a finding.

Age in days. Calculated, not typed.

Age band. Derived from the age in days.

Threshold flag. Yes or no, based on your chosen review threshold.

Review owner. A role, not a person's name, so the report survives staff changes.

Decision status. Open, under review, retained, or released for recovery discussion.

That is eleven columns. If your team can only maintain eight, drop the material description and the last issue date before you drop the threshold flag or the review owner. A report without an owner is a list, not a report.

A worked example across the four bands

The following is a fictional illustration. No real lot, quantity or date is implied.

Band 1, 0 to 90 days. Lot A-1042, solar grade silicon, 40 units, received 22 days ago, last issued 9 days ago. No flag. Decision: none required. The lot is moving and the report simply confirms it.

Band 2, 91 to 180 days. Lot B-2210, semiconductor materials, 18 units, received 140 days ago, never issued. No flag yet, but the last issue date is blank. Decision: the review owner confirms whether the lot is committed to a known internal requirement. If not, it is placed on a watch list so it does not arrive at the threshold without warning.

Band 3, 181 to 365 days. Lot C-3305, 12 units, received 240 days ago, never issued. Flagged. Decision: the review owner opens a decision record and asks three questions. Is there a live internal requirement? Is the lot still within its documented storage conditions? Is there a reason to keep it rather than discuss recovery? The outcome is recorded as retained with a review date, or released for a recovery conversation.

Band 4, over 365 days. Lot D-4108, 6 units, received 410 days ago, last issued 380 days ago. Flagged. Decision: this is the band where retention needs a written justification. If no requirement exists, the lot moves to a recovery discussion. San Techno's silicon recovery work covers non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and such material can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. That is the kind of conversation a flagged lot may lead to, but the report itself only records that the conversation was opened and who owns it.

The point of the example is that each band has a different default action. Band 1 needs nothing. Band 2 needs a watch. Band 3 needs a decision record. Band 4 needs a justification or a release.

Decision worksheet for each flagged lot

When a lot crosses the threshold, the review owner completes a short worksheet. Five questions are enough.

1. What is the lot reference and current age band? 2. Is there a documented internal requirement for this material in the next two quarters? Answer yes, no, or unknown, and name the source of that answer. 3. Has the lot been stored as recorded, with no undocumented moves? Answer yes, no, or unknown. 4. What is the proposed outcome: retain with a review date, release for recovery discussion, or escalate for a second opinion? 5. Who approved the outcome, and on what date?

The worksheet is deliberately short. A long form invites people to skip it. The value is in question two, because 'unknown' is a legitimate and useful answer. A lot marked unknown is not a failure of the process; it is a prompt to go and ask the planning team.

Record the outcome in the report's decision status column so the next run of the report shows progress. A lot that stays flagged for three consecutive reports with no change in status is a signal that the review owner is not being given time to act, which is a management issue rather than a stock issue.

Keep the worksheet with the report, not in a separate folder. Reports and worksheets that live apart tend to drift apart.

Running the report on a schedule and keeping it clean

A monthly run suits most teams. Quarterly is too slow for a threshold that sits at 181 days, because a lot can cross the threshold and sit there for weeks before anyone notices. Weekly is usually more effort than the decisions justify.

Three housekeeping habits keep the report trustworthy. First, reconcile the report against the physical stock count before publishing it. A report that lists a lot in a location where it no longer sits undermines every other line. Second, freeze the report after publication. If a lot's status changes mid-month, record the change in the next run rather than editing the published version, so the history stays readable. Third, keep a short changelog at the top of each run noting what changed in the layout, if anything. Layout changes are the most common reason two months of reports cannot be compared.

For teams that also handle reclaim and recovery paperwork, the aging report sits upstream of those documents. A flagged lot becomes a recovery discussion, and the recovery discussion produces its own records. Keeping the aging report as the entry point means the recovery conversation starts with a dated, owned decision rather than an informal recollection.

What the report should not try to do

An aging report is a scheduling and ownership document. It should not attempt to assess material quality, predict yield, or certify anything. Those judgements belong to the teams and records that already own them.

It should also not become a place to store commercial terms. Prices, supplier names and negotiation notes do not belong in a document that circulates to storage and planning staff. Keep the report to identity, age, location and decision status.

Finally, resist the temptation to add a score. A single number that combines age, quantity and value looks tidy but hides the reasoning, and different readers will interpret it differently. The band plus the threshold flag plus a named owner is enough to drive action, and it stays readable when someone new picks up the report.

A well-run aging report does one thing well: it makes slow-moving silicon visible, dated and owned, so that the decision to retain or release happens on purpose rather than by default.
