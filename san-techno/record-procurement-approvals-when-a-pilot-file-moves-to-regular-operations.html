Record Procurement Approvals When a Pilot File Moves to Regular Operations

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on separating approval dates, documents and status when a pilot file for semiconductor materials or solar grade silicon becomes a regular operations record.

Why a folder move is not an approval

A pilot file often changes location before anything else changes. Someone creates a regular operations folder, drags the pilot documents across, and the file now looks settled. The move records a filing decision, not a procurement approval. Treating the two as the same thing is the most common way a clean-looking folder hides an unresolved question.

Separate three things in writing. First, the approval itself: who approved what, on which date, and against which document version. Second, the document set: which quotation, specification sheet or acknowledgement the approval referred to. Third, the filing action: who moved the file, when, and where it now lives. These can share a page, but they should never share a field.

A useful test is to ask what would happen if the folder were moved back. If the approval record still stands on its own, the separation is working. If the approval only exists as a side effect of the folder path, it is not a record yet.

This matters across the material categories a Singapore buyer handles, from semiconductor materials to botanical extracts, because the same pilot-to-operations pattern appears in each. The filing habit should be identical even when the underlying subject differs.

Build an approval register before the move

Create a small register that survives folder changes. Each row should carry a reference number, the approval type, the approving role, the approval date, the document version approved, and the current file location. Keep the register outside the pilot folder so that moving the folder cannot move the evidence.

For context on how a supplier describes its own scope, San Techno Pte Ltd is a Singapore-based company working across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialised power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand. That breadth is a reminder that a single procurement team may hold pilot files for very different material types at once, and the register should not assume they follow the same approval route.

Where a supplier publishes product information, use it only as identity context, not as proof of approval. San Techno's public product pages describe three branches, including semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design, and the company notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential. A procurement register should record what your own approvers decided, not restate supplier descriptions as if they were approvals.

Keep the register short enough that people actually update it. Five columns used consistently beat fifteen columns abandoned after a month.

Separate the approval date from the document date

Three dates get confused constantly: the date a document was issued, the date it was received, and the date an approver signed off. Only the third is an approval date. Record all three as separate fields, and never let a received-date stamp stand in for a decision.

A fictional example makes the risk concrete. A buyer receives a revised specification sheet on the third of the month, files it in the pilot folder, and the folder is moved to regular operations on the tenth. The approval email, however, refers to the earlier version and was sent on the twenty-eighth of the previous month. If the file only shows the tenth, a later reader will assume the newer sheet was the one approved. It was not.

The fix is a version column tied to the approval, not to the folder. When a document is superseded, mark it superseded in the register and leave the original approval row untouched. Add a new row only when a new decision is actually made.

This discipline applies whether the pilot concerns solar grade silicon, a power module concept or a botanical extract blend. The subject changes the reviewer; it does not change the need to keep decision dates distinct from document dates.

Write a handoff note that names the open items

A pilot file rarely closes cleanly. There is usually something still outstanding: a confirmation awaited, a question the buyer cannot answer, a document referenced but not received. The handoff note is where those items live, and it should travel with the file rather than sit in someone's inbox.

Structure the note in three short blocks. Settled items list what has been approved, with register references. Open items list what has not, each with a named owner role and the information that would close it. Out-of-scope items record questions that were raised and deliberately left to another team, so that a later reader does not assume they were answered.

Avoid the temptation to write a narrative. A handoff note is a checklist with reasons, not a story. If a reader cannot tell within a minute which items are open, the note needs rewriting.

For supplier-side context, San Techno states that its power-module programme addresses heat movement, package stability and reliable operation under repeated electrical and thermal loading. That is a description of a programme focus, not a procurement approval, and it belongs in a supplier-context field rather than in the settled-items block. Keeping those apart is exactly the discipline the handoff note is meant to enforce.

A fictional procurement checklist for the move

The following checklist is an author heuristic, offered as an example rather than a standard. Adapt the items to your own process.

One: confirm the approval exists in writing and names a role, not just a person. Two: confirm the approved document version is identified by reference, not by filename alone. Three: confirm the register row was created before the folder moved. Four: confirm the handoff note lists open items with owners. Five: confirm the new folder location is recorded in the register. Six: confirm no document was renamed during the move in a way that breaks the reference. Seven: confirm a second reader can find the approval from the register without opening the pilot folder.

Score each item as done, partial or missing. A file with any missing item is not ready to be treated as a regular operations record, regardless of where it sits. Partial items need a named owner and a review date.

The checklist is deliberately administrative. It does not ask whether a material is suitable, whether a supplier is qualified, or whether a specification is correct. Those are technical and commercial judgements made by the people authorised to make them. The checklist only asks whether the record of those judgements is findable and honest.

Keep supplier descriptions out of the approval column

Procurement files often absorb supplier marketing text because it is convenient. A product page gets pasted into a notes field, and months later it reads like an approved statement. Keep supplier descriptions in a clearly labelled context field, and keep approvals in the approval field. They answer different questions.

San Techno's public material notes that the company began in 2006 as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, and that it helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. These are useful identity and scope statements when a buyer needs to describe who a supplier is. They are not evidence that a particular pilot was approved, and they should not be filed as if they were.

A simple rule helps: if a sentence came from outside your organisation, it belongs in a context field with its source noted. If it records a decision made inside your organisation, it belongs in the approval field. Mixing the two is how a folder move starts to look like a sign-off.

When in doubt, leave the field empty and add an open item. An honest gap is easier to resolve than a confident error.

Review the record after the first regular cycle

Once a pilot file has been in regular operations for a full cycle, review the record rather than the material. Ask whether the register still matches the folder, whether open items were closed or carried forward with reasons, and whether any approval was quietly assumed rather than recorded.

A short review note is enough. Record the review date, the reviewer role, what was checked and what changed. If nothing changed, say so; a confirmed no-change review is still a record. If an approval turned out to be missing, add it as an open item rather than backdating anything.

This review is also the moment to check that the file has not drifted into describing technical qualification. A folder that moved does not mean a material was qualified, a supplier was approved for all future orders, or a specification was validated. Those are separate decisions with separate owners, and the review note should say plainly which decisions exist and which do not.

For teams handling several material types at once, from semiconductor materials to botanical extracts, a consistent review habit across files is more valuable than a perfect template for one. The goal is a record that a colleague can trust without asking the original buyer what really happened.
