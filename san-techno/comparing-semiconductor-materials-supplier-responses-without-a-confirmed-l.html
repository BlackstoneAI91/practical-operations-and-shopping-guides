Comparing Semiconductor Materials Supplier Responses Without a Confirmed Lead Time

Published by San Techno.

A procurement guide for Singapore teams comparing semiconductor and solar materials supplier responses when no lead time is confirmed, organised by confirmation milestone, missing documentation and follow-up ownership, with fictional examples and a reusable worksheet.

Why an unconfirmed lead time is a comparison problem, not a waiting problem

A supplier reply that says the material is available but gives no date is not a quote you can rank. It is a set of open questions wearing the shape of an answer. When several replies arrive in the same week, the temptation is to sort them by the number that looks most definite, or by whichever email arrived first. Both shortcuts hide the real difference between suppliers: how far each one has actually moved toward a confirmed commitment.

The practical move is to stop treating the lead time as a single missing field. Treat it as the last milestone in a chain, and compare suppliers by how many earlier milestones they have already cleared. A reply that names a material grade, a packaging unit, a sample quantity and a named person responsible for the next update is further along than a reply that names only a price and a vague month, even if the second one sounds more confident.

This matters especially for semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon, where the buyer often cannot verify condition from an email alone. San Techno Pte Ltd, a Singapore-based company incorporated in 2006, began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, which is useful context for understanding why a distributor's reply may depend on an upstream confirmation the buyer never sees. That dependency is not a fault. It is a fact to record.

Keep one rule throughout: never write a date into your comparison sheet that the supplier did not write in their own message. If you infer a date, mark it as your inference and keep it in a separate column.

Build the comparison around confirmation milestones

Instead of columns for price and lead time, build columns for milestones. A workable set for a first-pass comparison looks like this:

Milestone 1, identity confirmed: the supplier has named the material, grade or reference specification in writing, and it matches the name on your enquiry.

Milestone 2, quantity basis confirmed: the reply states the unit, pack size or sample quantity so the number can be compared with other replies.

Milestone 3, condition or documentation basis confirmed: the supplier has said what documentation accompanies the material, or has stated plainly that none is available yet.

Milestone 4, date basis confirmed: the supplier has given a date, a date range, or an explicit statement that no date can be given yet, together with the reason.

Milestone 5, owner confirmed: a named person or role has accepted responsibility for the next update, with a date for that update.

Score each reply by the highest milestone it has fully cleared, not by how promising it sounds. A fictional example: Supplier A clears milestones 1 to 3 and says a date depends on an upstream confirmation expected in two weeks. Supplier B clears milestone 1 only but quotes a delivery month. On a milestone score, A leads, because A has told you what the date depends on. B's month is unanchored and may not survive contact with reality.

This is an author heuristic, not a researched scoring model. Adjust the milestones to your own category. For wafer reclaim enquiries, milestone 3 often carries the most weight, because the documentation basis determines whether the lot can be assessed at all.

Record missing documentation as a finding, not a gap to fill in later

When a reply arrives without supporting documents, the instinct is to note it as pending and move on. Better to record it as a finding with a category, because missing documents cluster into recognisable types and each type implies a different follow-up.

Type one, not yet produced: the supplier has the information but has not sent it. Follow-up is a request with a date.

Type two, not applicable: the document does not exist for this material or this transaction. Follow-up is a written confirmation that it is not applicable, so the file is complete.

Type three, withheld: the supplier holds the document but will not release it at this stage. Follow-up is a question about what would need to change for release.

Type four, unknown: the supplier has not said whether the document exists. Follow-up is a direct question.

Type three deserves particular care. Some information legitimately stays internal. San Techno states that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential, which is a normal boundary rather than a red flag. If a supplier declines to share a document, record the decline and the stated reason, then decide whether your evaluation can proceed without it. Do not treat a refusal as evidence of a problem, and do not treat silence as consent to assume the document exists.

For materials intended to become reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, the documentation basis often matters more than the date, because the receiving site needs to know what it is accepting before it can plan anything.

Assign follow-up ownership on your side before you chase

Most stalled comparisons stall on the buyer's side, not the supplier's. Two people assume the other is chasing, or one person chases three suppliers and loses track of which question went to whom.

Before sending any follow-up, write down four things for each supplier: the question you are asking, the person you are asking, the person on your side who owns the answer, and the date you will escalate if no reply arrives. Keep this in the same sheet as the milestone scores so the two views never drift apart.

A fictional example of how this reads in practice: Supplier C was asked on 3 March to confirm the packaging unit and to state whether a certificate accompanies the material. The buyer-side owner is the receiving coordinator. The escalation date is 10 March. On 10 March, if no reply, the owner sends a single consolidated message listing both open questions rather than a third partial chase.

Consolidating questions is the discipline that matters most. Each partial chase resets the supplier's sense of urgency and adds a new thread to reconcile later. One message, all open items, one named recipient, one stated date for the reply you need.

If your organisation routes sourcing questions through a single point of contact, use it, and keep the record in one place rather than scattered across personal threads.

A worked fictional comparison, end to end

Here is a short illustration using invented suppliers and invented dates. Nothing below describes a real company, a real price or a real delivery commitment.

Enquiry sent 14 April for a quantity of semiconductor materials, with a request for a sample and a statement of accompanying documentation.

Supplier Northgate replies 16 April. Names the material and reference specification. States the sample quantity in grams. Says documentation will follow but does not say which document. Gives no date. No named owner. Milestone score: 2.

Supplier Rivermark replies 17 April. Names the material. States the sample quantity in wafers, which is a different unit from Northgate and must be converted before comparison. States that a certificate accompanies the material and will be sent with the sample. Says a date cannot be given until an upstream confirmation arrives, expected within ten working days. Names a coordinator who will update on 1 May. Milestone score: 5.

Supplier Kestrel replies 21 April. Names the material only, and asks for the enquiry to be resent to a different address. Milestone score: 1.

On the milestone view, Rivermark leads clearly, even though Rivermark is the only supplier that gave no date at all. That is the point of the method. Rivermark has told you what the date depends on, who owns the update and when it will come. Northgate's silence on documentation and Kestrel's restart both cost more time than Rivermark's honest gap.

The unit mismatch between grams and wafers is a separate problem and should be resolved in its own column before any commercial comparison. Do not let a unit conversion hide inside a lead-time discussion.

When the material is a recovered or reclaimed stream

Comparisons get harder when the material is not a standard catalogue item. Recovered silicon and reclaimed wafers carry a history, and the supplier's reply may legitimately depend on an assessment that has not happened yet.

San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, which illustrates why a recovery-related reply may be staged rather than immediate. The supplier may need to see the material before committing to anything, and a reply that says so is more useful than a reply that guesses.

In these cases, add a milestone for assessment: has the supplier stated what they need to see, and in what form, before they can give a date? A reply that names the assessment inputs is further along than one that simply says the material must be inspected.

Keep the comparison focused on documentation and handoff. Your sheet should record what was asked, what was answered, what remains open and who owns the next step. It should not attempt to direct any process, and it should not record assumptions about what will be done with the material. Those belong to the parties handling it, under their own controls.

A reusable worksheet and a closing checklist

Copy this structure into your own sheet. Each row is one supplier. Each column is one field.

Columns: supplier name; date of enquiry; date of reply; material name as written by the supplier; whether it matches your enquiry (yes, no, unclear); quantity and unit as written; documentation stated (yes, no, not applicable, withheld, unknown); date basis (exact date, range, dependency stated, none); named follow-up owner at the supplier; date of promised next update; your side's owner; your escalation date; highest milestone cleared; notes.

Before you close the comparison, run this checklist:

Every date in the sheet appears in a supplier message, or is clearly marked as your own inference. Every unit is recorded exactly as written, with conversions kept in a separate column. Every missing document has a category, not just a blank. Every supplier has a named owner on your side and an escalation date. Every open question has been sent once, consolidated, to a named recipient. No supplier has been ranked on tone, speed of first reply or apparent confidence.

A short note on expectations. This worksheet is a bookkeeping tool, not a prediction. It will not tell you when material will arrive. It will tell you which supplier has given you the most to work with, and which questions are still unanswered. That is usually enough to decide where to spend the next hour.
