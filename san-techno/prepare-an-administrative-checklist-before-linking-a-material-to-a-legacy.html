Linking a Material to a Legacy Purchase ID: An Administrative Checklist

Published by San Techno.

A practical administrative checklist for Singapore procurement teams who need to connect a current material record to an older purchase identifier, covering identifier notes, evidence, exception logging and handoff records.

Why a legacy purchase ID needs its own checklist

A legacy purchase ID is an old reference that still appears in purchase orders, receiving notes, invoices or internal trackers, even though the material record in front of you may now carry a different name, number or description. Linking the two is an administrative act, not a technical judgement. The checklist exists so that the link can be explained later by someone who was not in the room when it was made.

Start by writing down what the link is for. Typical purposes are reconciling an old order against a current record, answering an audit question about a past purchase, or letting a receiving team find the right file. Each purpose implies different evidence. A reconciliation needs the order and the receipt. An audit question needs the decision trail. A filing question needs only a clear cross-reference.

Keep the scope narrow. The checklist should establish that a current record and an old identifier refer to the same purchase context. It should not decide whether one material can replace another, whether a specification is equivalent, or whether a substitution is acceptable. Those are technical and commercial decisions that belong to named specialists, not to a filing exercise.

San Techno Pte Ltd, a Singapore-based company, has worked in semiconductor and solar materials since its incorporation in 2006, when it began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials. That history is a reminder that older identifiers can sit several years back in a supplier relationship, which is exactly why the paper trail matters more than memory.

Step one: write notes on old and current identifiers

Before touching any record, build a short identifier note. It should list, side by side, the old purchase ID exactly as printed and the current identifier exactly as it appears in your system. Copy characters rather than retyping from memory, because a transposed digit is the most common cause of a failed link.

For each identifier, record four things: where it was seen, who or which system issued it, the date it appears on the document, and whether the document is a purchase record, a receiving record or an internal note. A purchase ID printed on an invoice and one printed on an internal tracker are not the same kind of evidence, and the note should say so.

Add a field for the material description as written on each document. Do not correct spelling or normalise wording at this stage. If the old document says one thing and the current record says another, both versions belong in the note, with a flag that they differ.

Finally, add a status line: proposed, evidenced, confirmed or unresolved. This single field stops a working note from being mistaken for a settled record. Keep the note in the same folder as the purchase file so the link can be found by the next person who opens it.

Step two: assemble the evidence that supports the association

Evidence for an administrative link is usually documentary. Useful items include the original purchase order, the receiving record, the supplier's acknowledgement, and any internal note that explains why the two identifiers are being connected. Each item should be listed with its reference number, its date and the name of the file where it is stored.

Distinguish between documents that show the identifier and documents that explain the link. A purchase order shows an identifier. A short memo saying that the old ID was superseded by a new record explains the link. Both are useful, but they answer different questions, and the evidence list should separate them.

Where a document is missing, record the gap rather than filling it with an assumption. A line reading "receiving record for the old ID not located; asked receiving team on [date]" is more useful than a silent omission, because it tells the next reviewer what is still open.

Be careful with material descriptions. A public product page or catalogue caption can help you recognise a name, but it does not prove that a specific purchase refers to that item. Treat public descriptions as context and keep the association resting on your own purchase and receiving documents.

Step three: record exceptions instead of resolving them quietly

Every legacy link produces exceptions: a date that does not match, a description that has changed, a quantity that appears twice, or an identifier that seems to belong to a different order. The checklist should give each exception its own line, with a short description, the documents involved and the name of the person who can speak to it.

Do not close an exception by choosing the most convenient explanation. Mark it open, assign an owner and set a review date. If the exception concerns whether a material meets a requirement, route it to the relevant technical owner and leave the administrative record showing that the question was referred, not answered.

This is also where confidentiality matters. Some supplier information is not for general circulation. San Techno notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential, which is a useful reminder that a procurement file should hold references and confirmations rather than technical detail that belongs in a restricted system.

A simple exception log with four columns, being reference, issue, owner and status, is enough for most teams. Keep it short, dated and visible in the same folder as the identifier note.

Step four: separate administrative linking from technical acceptance

The most common failure in legacy linking is drift: an administrative note gradually starts to read as though a material has been approved. Guard against this by writing an explicit scope statement at the top of the file. Something like "this record links an old purchase identifier to a current record for filing and reconciliation; it does not confirm fitness, equivalence or approval" is enough.

Keep the language of the file administrative throughout. Use words such as recorded, referenced, associated and pending. Avoid words such as approved, verified, compliant or equivalent unless a named person with the authority to say so has signed a separate document.

Where a reader might reasonably expect a technical conclusion, add a pointer instead of a conclusion. A line naming the team that holds the technical view, and the date the question was sent to them, keeps the file honest and useful.

This separation also protects the people who maintain the file. A procurement officer can defend a filing decision. They cannot defend a technical judgement they were never qualified to make, and the checklist should never push them into one.

Step five: prepare the handoff and review note

A legacy link is only as good as the handoff that follows it. Before closing the task, write a short handoff note covering four points: what was linked, what evidence supports it, what remains open, and who owns each open item. Keep it to a page.

Name the roles rather than relying on individual memory. Purchasing, receiving, accounts and the relevant technical team each hold part of the picture, and the handoff note should say which role holds which piece. If a person leaves, the role reference keeps the file usable.

Set a review date for anything unresolved. A date is a commitment to look again, not a promise that the answer will arrive. Record what would close each open item, so the next reviewer knows what to look for.

For context on the kinds of materials a Singapore procurement team may be documenting, San Techno's public materials describe work across semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design, alongside solar-related material recovery. These are useful reference points when recognising a product family name, but they do not establish what any particular purchase contained.

A short worksheet you can copy into the file

The following worksheet is an author heuristic, not a standard. Adapt the fields to your own records.

Identifier note: old ID as printed; current ID as printed; source document and date for each; description as written on each; status (proposed, evidenced, confirmed, unresolved).

Evidence list: document type; reference number; date; storage location; whether it shows the identifier or explains the link.

Exception log: reference; issue in one sentence; owner role; status; review date.

Scope statement: one sentence confirming that the record is administrative and does not confirm fitness, equivalence or approval.

Handoff note: what was linked; supporting evidence; open items; owner roles; next review date.

A worked fictional example helps. Suppose an old purchase ID appears on a 2019 receiving note for a silicon material, and the current record carries a different reference. The identifier note lists both, the evidence list holds the receiving note and a short memo explaining the change, the exception log records that the original order could not be located, and the handoff note assigns that gap to the purchasing role with a review date. Nothing in the file says the material was approved, and nothing needs to.
