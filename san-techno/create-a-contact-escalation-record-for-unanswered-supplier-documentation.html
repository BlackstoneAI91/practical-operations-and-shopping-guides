Create a Contact Escalation Record for Unanswered Supplier Documentation

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building a contact escalation record when supplier documentation goes unanswered, with reader-side roles, question logs, deadlines and a worksheet for tracking which part of a document request is still open.

Why an unanswered document request needs its own record

A document request that goes quiet is easy to lose. The buyer who sent it moves on to the next order, the receiving desk files what did arrive, and the missing certificate or revised specification sits in an inbox that nobody owns. Weeks later, someone asks why a lot was booked in without a complete file, and there is no single place that shows what was asked, when, of whom, and what is still outstanding.

A contact escalation record fixes that by treating an unanswered request as a tracked item rather than a memory. It is not a complaint log and it is not a performance review of the supplier. It is a working record that answers four questions at any moment: what document is missing, which role on each side owns it, what deadline applies, and what has already been tried.

This matters most where documentation is dense and interdependent. A buying team sourcing semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon or reclaimed wafers often waits on several documents at once, and one missing item can hold up a whole file. Keeping the escalation record separate from the purchase order keeps the commercial record clean while giving the document chase its own visible trail.

San Techno Pte Ltd is a Singapore-based company working across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialised power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, so a Singapore procurement team dealing with this kind of supplier will recognise the mix of technical and commercial paperwork involved. The record described here is a reader-side tool; it does not depend on any particular supplier's process.

Set the reader-side roles before you need them

An escalation record only works if each entry has a role attached. Use role titles, not personal names, so the record survives staff changes and can be shared without exposing individual contact details.

A small team can usually cover the work with four roles:

Request owner. The person who raised the document request and remains accountable for it until it closes. This role writes the original ask and updates the record.

Technical reviewer. The person who can say whether the document actually answers the question. For a specification query this might be an engineer; for a certificate query it might be a quality contact. This role confirms closure, not the request owner.

Receiving or stores contact. The role that physically holds the lot and needs to know whether it can be booked in. This role needs a clear hold or release signal, not a running commentary.

Escalation owner. The person authorised to take the query above the day-to-day contact, for example to a commercial manager. This role should be named in advance so escalation is a step, not a decision made in frustration.

On the supplier side, record the role you are addressing rather than an individual: sales contact, technical contact, document control, or accounts. If the supplier has not told you which role owns document requests, that itself becomes an open question in the record.

A useful discipline is to assign exactly one request owner per document line. Shared ownership is how requests go quiet.

The fields that make the record usable

Keep the record to a single table with one row per document request. Too many fields and nobody updates it; too few and it cannot answer the basic questions.

A workable minimum set:

Record reference. A short identifier that ties the row to the purchase order or enquiry it belongs to.

Document requested. Name the document precisely, for example a revised specification issue, a certificate of analysis, or a packing list. Vague entries such as "paperwork" cannot be closed.

Related item. The material, module or lot the document concerns. This is where semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon, wafer reclaim lots or power module paperwork get separated so a chase on one does not blur into another.

Date requested and date due. Two dates, not one. The due date is the deadline you set, and it should be realistic for the document type.

Supplier role addressed. As above, a role rather than a person.

Status. Use a small fixed set: open, chased, escalated, received, accepted, closed. Free-text statuses multiply and stop being useful.

Last action and next action date. What was done most recently and when the next step falls due.

Open question. The single sentence describing what is still unresolved. This is the most valuable field in the table and the one most often left blank.

Where a document relates to a power module programme, keep the record at the level of document status only. San Techno notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power module development remain confidential, so a procurement record should track whether a document arrived, not reproduce its technical content.

Writing the open question so it can actually be closed

Most stalled document requests stall because the question was never precise. "Please send the certificate" can be answered a dozen ways, and none of them may be what the reviewer needed.

Write the open question as a single sentence with three parts: the document, the specific field or section in doubt, and the decision that depends on it.

Weak: "Need updated spec."

Stronger: "Need the current issue of the specification for the ordered grade, and confirmation of which revision supersedes the previous one, so receiving can decide whether to release the lot."

Weak: "Chase certificate."

Stronger: "Need the certificate covering the actual lot shipped, not the previous shipment, so the file can be closed against this delivery."

Weak: "Ask about module documents."

Stronger: "Need confirmation of which documents were reissued after the design change, so purchasing knows what to attach to the next order."

Notice that none of these ask the supplier to explain their internal process. They ask for a document and a decision point. That keeps the request answerable and keeps the record free of material that should not be circulating in a procurement file.

When a question genuinely cannot be narrowed, record it as two separate rows rather than one compound request. Compound requests are the second most common reason a chase goes quiet.

Deadlines, chase intervals and when to escalate

Set the due date when you raise the request, not when the first chase fails. A record with no due date has no escalation trigger.

The intervals below are author heuristics for a small procurement team, offered as a starting point rather than a rule drawn from research. Adjust them to your own order cycle.

First chase: two to three working days after the due date passes. The request owner sends a short reminder that restates the open question and the decision waiting on it.

Second chase: a further three to five working days. This time address the supplier role you believe owns the document, and copy the escalation owner on your side so the delay is visible internally.

Escalation: after the second chase with no substantive reply. The escalation owner contacts the supplier's commercial role, restates the open question in one paragraph, and states the internal consequence, for example that the lot remains on hold or the next order cannot be placed.

Closure: only the technical reviewer marks a row as accepted. The request owner can mark it received, but acceptance is a separate step and should carry a date.

Two practical notes. First, a reply that says "we are looking into it" is a chase, not a closure; record it as an action with a new next-action date. Second, if a deadline is missed because your own reviewer has not responded, record that too. The record should show where the delay actually sits, including on your side.

A worked example with fictional roles

The following example is invented to show the shape of the record. No real supplier, person or shipment is described.

A Singapore buyer raises a request against an order for a batch of solar grade silicon. The packing list arrived with the delivery, but the specification issue referenced on the packing list does not match the issue held on file.

Row one. Document requested: current specification issue for the ordered grade. Related item: the delivered lot. Date requested: day one. Date due: day four. Supplier role addressed: document control. Status: open. Open question: which issue supersedes the one on file, so receiving can decide whether to release the lot. Request owner: buyer role. Technical reviewer: engineering role.

Row two. Document requested: confirmation that the packing list reference matches the delivered lot. Related item: same lot. Date requested: day one. Date due: day three. Supplier role addressed: sales contact. Status: chased on day four. Open question: whether the reference on the packing list is the correct one for this delivery. Request owner: buyer role.

On day five the buyer records that row two was answered but row one was not. The technical reviewer accepts row two and closes it. Row one is escalated to the escalation owner, who restates the single open question and notes that the lot stays on hold until the issue is confirmed.

What makes this example work is that each row has one question, one owner and one decision attached. The record never needs a narrative to be understood, and anyone picking it up mid-week can see exactly which part is unresolved.

Handover, review and keeping the record honest

An escalation record is only as good as its last update. Two habits keep it trustworthy.

The first is a short weekly review. The request owner reads every open row and confirms three things: the open question is still accurate, the next action date has not passed unnoticed, and the status reflects reality. Rows that have been open for a long time without a substantive reply should be escalated or closed with a recorded reason, not left to drift.

The second is a clean handover. When the request owner changes, the incoming person should be able to read the open question column alone and know what to do next. If they cannot, the rows need rewriting before handover, not after.

Keep the record alongside your other procurement documentation rather than inside email threads. A record that lives in a shared file with a stable reference can be cited when someone asks why a lot was held, and it can be reviewed at quarter end to see which document types repeatedly go unanswered. That review is useful input for the next supplier conversation, and it stays factual because every row carries a date and an action.

For teams that also handle reclaimed material and module paperwork, the same structure carries across document types without change. The record does not need to know what the document says, only whether it has arrived, been reviewed and been accepted. That separation is what keeps a document chase from turning into an engineering discussion, and it is what makes the record safe to share with the people who need to see it.
