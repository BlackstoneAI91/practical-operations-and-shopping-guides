Combine Two Supplier Onboarding Calendars Without Merging Their Approval Histories

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on keeping two supplier onboarding calendars separate while sharing scheduling visibility, with worksheets for timelines, notifications and decision logs.

Why two calendars should stay two calendars

When two suppliers are being onboarded at the same time, the temptation is to merge their calendars into one master view. That can help scheduling, but it can also blur the line between two separate approval histories. Each supplier has its own set of documents, reviewers and decision points. If those histories are merged, a later question about which approval covered which supplier becomes hard to answer.

The safer approach is to keep two independent calendars and create a third, read-only scheduling layer that shows both timelines side by side. The scheduling layer answers the question "what is happening when?" The two source calendars answer the question "what was approved, by whom, and on what basis?" This separation matters for semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon purchases, where documentation often moves through several hands before a purchase order is raised.

A simple rule: the combined view may show dates and status labels, but it must not become the place where approvals are recorded. Approvals stay in the source calendar or the system of record that each supplier's file already uses. The combined view is a coordination tool, not an approval log.

Set up independent timelines before combining anything

Before you build any combined view, make sure each supplier's timeline is complete on its own. A timeline is not just a list of dates. It should show the sequence of onboarding steps, the owner of each step, the document or confirmation that closes it, and the current status. If one supplier's timeline is missing a step, the combined view will hide that gap rather than reveal it.

A useful worksheet for each supplier has four columns: step, planned date, owner role, and closing evidence. The closing evidence column is the key one. It records what proves the step is finished, such as a received document reference or a written confirmation. Without that column, a date can look complete when nothing has actually been confirmed.

Keep the two worksheets in separate files or separate tabs with clear names. Do not copy approval notes from one into the other. If a step is shared, such as a joint review meeting, record it in both timelines with the same date but keep each supplier's approval note in its own file. This preserves the independence of each approval history while still allowing coordination.

Build a read-only combined view with clear labels

The combined view should be deliberately limited. It can show supplier name, step name, planned date, status and owner role. It should not show approval comments, document contents or decision rationale. Those belong in the source calendars.

Use a status vocabulary that makes the difference between scheduling and approval obvious. For example: planned, in progress, awaiting document, confirmed by source calendar. The last label is important because it tells the reader that the combined view is not the authority. It points back to the source calendar where the approval actually lives.

When you add a row to the combined view, include a reference to the source calendar entry. That reference can be a simple identifier such as a supplier code plus a step number. It does not need to be a link. The point is to make it possible to trace any combined-view row back to its source without merging the two histories.

This approach is consistent with the kind of documentation discipline used in semiconductor materials procurement, where traceability of each record matters more than the convenience of a single view.

Distinguish redundant notifications from real updates

When two calendars are running, notifications can multiply. A single document received by one supplier may trigger an update in that supplier's calendar, a reminder in the combined view, and an email to the procurement team. The same event can appear three times.

A simple notification map helps. For each type of event, decide which system sends the notification and which systems only record it. For example, a document receipt might be notified by the source calendar and recorded silently in the combined view. A date change might be notified by the combined view because it affects coordination, while the source calendar records the change without sending a separate alert.

The goal is not to eliminate notifications but to make each one meaningful. If a notification does not require an action, it should be a record, not an alert. This is especially useful when onboarding involves multiple document types, such as material certificates, packing records and delivery confirmations. Each type may have its own rhythm, and a combined view that alerts on everything quickly becomes noise.

Review the notification map at the start of each onboarding cycle and adjust it as the two timelines evolve.

Keep decision records separate even when dates align

Two suppliers may reach the same onboarding stage on the same day. That does not mean their approvals should be combined. Each approval decision belongs to the supplier file it was made for. If a reviewer signs off on a document for Supplier A, that sign-off should not appear as a shared approval for Supplier B, even if the documents look similar.

A decision log for each supplier should record the decision, the date, the role of the person who made it, and the document or reference it applies to. The combined view can show that a decision was made, but it should not reproduce the decision itself. Instead, it can show a status such as "decision recorded in source calendar" and a reference to the decision log entry.

This separation is particularly important when the materials involved are different. A procurement team sourcing semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon may be onboarding two suppliers with different document sets. Aligning their calendars for convenience should never imply that one supplier's approval covers the other. The decision logs remain the authoritative record for each supplier.

Use a handover note when the combined view changes hands

The combined view is a coordination tool, and like any tool it needs a handover note when responsibility moves to a new person. The handover note should explain what the combined view contains, what it does not contain, and where the source calendars and decision logs are kept. It should also list any open items that are waiting on one supplier but affecting the other.

A short handover note can be structured as three lists: what is confirmed in each source calendar, what is still open, and what the combined view is currently showing. This makes it clear that the combined view is a snapshot, not a replacement for the source records.

For teams working with silicon recovery or wafer reclaim documentation, the handover note is also a chance to confirm that no approval history has been copied between suppliers. A simple check is to ask whether every approval mentioned in the handover note can be traced back to a specific source calendar entry. If not, the note is incomplete.

San Techno's own work in silicon recovery and reusable raw materials for solar illustrates how material flows can be documented across separate stages. The same principle applies to onboarding calendars: each stage keeps its own record, even when the stages are coordinated.

A short checklist for keeping histories separate

Before you combine any two onboarding calendars, run through this checklist. It is a set of prompts, not a certification.

One: does each supplier have its own timeline with a closing evidence column? Two: is the combined view read-only and limited to dates, status and owner roles? Three: does every combined-view row reference a source calendar entry? Four: are notifications mapped so that each event has one notifying system and the others record silently? Five: are decision logs kept per supplier and not reproduced in the combined view? Six: is there a handover note that explains the boundary between coordination and approval?

If any answer is no, fix that point before adding more detail to the combined view. The aim is not to make the combined view richer but to make the separation between the two approval histories unmistakable.

For procurement teams sourcing semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon or related documentation, this discipline supports clearer conversations with suppliers and fewer internal questions about which approval applies to which file.
