Pickup Readiness Checklist Before a Silicon Scrap Recycling Collection

Published by San Techno.

A day-before checklist for Singapore procurement teams preparing a silicon scrap recycling pickup: package counts, labels, manifests, access arrangements and handover records.

Why the day before matters more than the day of

A silicon scrap recycling collection is a handover of material that no longer serves its original purpose but still carries a paper trail. By the time the vehicle arrives, the site should already know what is leaving, how it is packed, who is releasing it and which document proves the release. The day before is the last quiet window to close those gaps.

This guide is written for procurement and receiving teams in Singapore who source, receive and document semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon. It is a preparation checklist, not a set of handling or disposal instructions. Where a step touches how material is physically processed, that belongs with your own site procedures and the receiving party's requirements, not with this page.

Its silicon recovery work covers non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and those materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. That context explains why the paperwork matters: a collection is the point where a site's internal record becomes someone else's intake record.

Section 1: Confirm the package count against your own record

Start with the number you control. Before the collection, count the packages, cassettes, cartons or containers that will be released and write the figure down with the date and the name of the person counting. Then compare it with the figure on the collection request or manifest you sent out.

Three outcomes are possible, and each has a different response:

Match. Record the confirmed count and move on.

Site count higher than the manifest. Something has been added since the request was raised, or a line was never captured. Decide whether the extra material travels with this collection or waits for the next one, and update the manifest before the vehicle arrives.

Site count lower than the manifest. A package has moved, been consumed, or been mislabelled. Do not simply reduce the number on the manifest; find out where the missing package went, because a manifest that quietly shrinks is harder to reconcile later than one that carries a note.

A useful habit is to count in the same unit the manifest uses. If the manifest counts cartons but the site counts inner packages, convert once, write the conversion down, and keep it with the collection record. Mixed units are one of the most common reasons a pickup stalls at the loading point.

Section 2: Check that every label matches the manifest line

Labels are where a collection most often goes wrong quietly. A carton can be correctly counted and still carry the wrong lot reference, an old revision code, or a description that no longer matches what is inside.

Work through the manifest line by line and, for each line, confirm four things on the physical package:

- The reference on the label matches the reference on the manifest. - The quantity on the label matches the quantity recorded for that line. - Any revision or status marking is current, not superseded. - The package is the one the manifest describes, not a similar package from a neighbouring line.

Where a label is handwritten, illegible or taped over, replace it before the collection rather than explaining it at the loading bay. A relabel should be recorded, with the old reference and the new one both noted, so the change is visible in the file.

This is also the moment to separate material that is genuinely part of the collection from material that is being held back. If a package is on the manifest but should not travel, remove it from the manifest and note why. A short reason line is enough; the point is that the record explains the difference between what was planned and what was released.

Section 3: Assemble the manifest pack in one place

The manifest is not a single sheet. By the day before collection it should be a small pack that a person who was not involved in preparing the material could follow. A practical pack contains:

A cover sheet with the collection reference, the site, the planned date and the name of the person releasing the material.

The line-by-line manifest, showing reference, description, quantity and unit for each line.

Any conversion notes, where units differ between the site record and the manifest.

A note of anything removed from or added to the original request, with a one-line reason for each change.

A copy of the collection request or confirmation that established the pickup in the first place.

Keep the pack together and keep a second copy with the person who will sign at handover. If the pack lives in two places, the loading point and the office, make sure both copies are the same revision. A stale copy in the wrong hands is worse than no copy, because it invites a signature against the wrong figures.

Procurement teams that handle semiconductor materials regularly often find it useful to keep a standing template for this pack, so that each collection starts from the same structure rather than being rebuilt from memory.

Section 4: Settle access, timing and who signs

Access arrangements are the part of a collection that depends least on the material and most on the site. The day before is when to confirm them, not the morning of the pickup.

Confirm and write down:

- The arrival window agreed with the collecting party, and the name of the person who will meet the vehicle. - The route the vehicle will take on site, and whether any gate, lift or loading area needs to be booked or opened. - Whether the collecting party needs anything from the site before loading begins, such as a visitor pass or a safety briefing, arranged through your own site procedures. - Who is authorised to sign the handover on the site side, and who signs on the collecting side. - What happens if the vehicle arrives outside the window, including who is contacted and what the fallback is.

Name people, not roles, in the day-before note. A role can be vacant on the day; a name can be reached. Where a named person may be unavailable, add a named alternate.

This is also the point to confirm that the person signing at handover has seen the manifest pack and understands which figures they are signing against. A signature on a collection note is an acknowledgement of a handover, and it should be given by someone who can see the count and the references at the moment of release.

Section 5: Run a five-minute pre-pickup check

The final step is a short, repeatable check that the person releasing the material completes the day before. It is deliberately small, because a long checklist completed under time pressure tends to be signed without being read.

A worked example of the check, using fictional references:

- Package count confirmed: 14 cartons counted on site, 14 lines on the manifest. Pass. - Labels checked: one carton relabelled from an old reference to the current one; change noted on the manifest. Pass with note. - Manifest pack assembled: cover sheet, line list, one conversion note, one removal note. Pass. - Access confirmed: arrival window agreed, named contact and alternate recorded, loading area booked. Pass. - Signatory confirmed: named site signatory has seen the pack. Pass.

If any line fails, the response is not to proceed and hope. Either fix the item before the window, or record the exception and agree with the collecting party how it will be handled at handover. An exception that is written down before the vehicle arrives is a manageable exception; one discovered at the loading point is a delay.

Treat the five-line structure as an author's heuristic for keeping the check short, not as an industry standard. Adapt the lines to what your own site actually controls.

Section 6: Close the loop after the vehicle leaves

Readiness does not end when the vehicle is loaded. The day after collection, the site should be able to answer three questions from its own records: what was released, who acknowledged it, and whether the released quantity matches the manifest that was signed.

A short close-out note is enough. Record the final count released, the name of the person who signed on each side, the collection reference, and any exception that was noted before or during the pickup. File it with the manifest pack rather than separately, so the pack tells the whole story from request to release.

Where the collecting party later raises a query about a line, the close-out note is what lets the site respond from evidence rather than recollection. That is the practical value of the day-before checklist: it turns a physical handover into a record that can be checked later without anyone having to remember what happened at the loading bay.
