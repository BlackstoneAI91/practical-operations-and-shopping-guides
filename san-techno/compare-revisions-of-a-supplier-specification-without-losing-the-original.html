Compare Revisions of a Supplier Specification Without Losing the Original

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on comparing supplier specification revisions while keeping the original intact, with example timestamps, a change log, and an approval and escalation matrix for semiconductor and solar materials buying.

Why the original revision must survive the comparison

A supplier specification rarely arrives once and stays fixed. A first issue is followed by a corrected issue, then a revised issue after a query, and often a further version after a commercial discussion. The comparison itself is easy. The hard part is proving, months later, what the document said when a purchase decision was made.

Procurement teams handling semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon sit between two pressures. Engineering wants the newest document. Finance and audit want the document that supported the order. If the comparison process overwrites the earlier file, both needs collapse into one undated copy and nobody can reconstruct the sequence.

The rule that prevents most of this trouble is simple: never edit a received specification in place. Treat every incoming revision as a new, separately stored record, and treat the comparison as a derived working document that points back to both originals. The supplier's file stays untouched; your analysis lives beside it.

This guide is written for a small procurement team in Singapore that sources, receives and documents semiconductor and solar materials. It covers document handling, timestamps, a change log and an approval matrix. It deliberately stays away from material engineering procedures, which belong to technical colleagues rather than to the buying desk.

Set up a revision register before the first comparison

The comparison is only as good as the register behind it. Before comparing anything, create a small register that gives every received file a stable identity. A register with six columns is usually enough for a small team:

Record ID: a short internal code, for example SPEC-0142. Supplier and product family: a plain description, not a marketing name. Revision label as printed by the supplier: copy it exactly, including any suffix. Date received: the date your team logged it, not the date on the document. Source: how it arrived, such as a portal download or an attachment to a query response. Status: current, superseded, or under review.

Two disciplines matter here. First, the revision label is copied, never interpreted. If a supplier writes a label that looks like a typo, record it as written and note the query separately. Second, the date received is your own timestamp and should never be replaced by the document date, because the two answer different questions.

Keep the register in one place that the whole team can see, and make the register the only authority on which revision is current. When someone asks which version is live, the answer comes from the register, not from whichever file happens to be at the top of a folder.

A useful habit is to store each revision in its own folder named with the record ID and the received date, so the folder listing itself becomes a rough timeline. This is a documentation convention, not a technical control, and it should be described that way to colleagues.

Example timestamps for a small procurement team

Timestamps do more work than they appear to. They let a reviewer reconstruct the order of events without interviewing anyone. The examples below are fictional and are offered as a format, not as a recommendation about how fast any team should move.

Example sequence for one fictional record, SPEC-0142:

2026-03-02 09:14 — Revision A received by attachment and logged by the buying desk. 2026-03-02 11:40 — Revision A stored in its own folder and marked current in the register. 2026-03-09 15:22 — Query raised about an unclear field; the query references Revision A. 2026-03-16 10:05 — Revision B received in response to the query and logged. 2026-03-16 14:30 — Comparison worksheet opened; Revision A marked superseded but retained. 2026-03-17 09:50 — Change log completed and routed for approval. 2026-03-18 16:10 — Approval recorded; Revision B marked current.

Notice what the sequence protects. Revision A is never deleted, the query is tied to the revision that prompted it, and the approval is dated after the comparison rather than before it. If a colleague later asks why an order was placed against a particular revision, the timestamps answer the question without guesswork.

Use one time zone consistently across the register and the change log, and state it once at the top of the log. Mixed time zones are a common source of confusion when a supplier in another market sends a file late in their working day.

Build the change log so each difference has an owner

A change log is not a list of everything that differs. It is a list of differences that someone must act on, each with an owner and a decision. A workable change log has these columns:

Line number: a simple counter so the log can be referenced in conversation. Field or section: where the difference sits, described in plain words. Revision A value and Revision B value: copied, not paraphrased. Difference type: added, removed, reworded, or value changed. Impact area: commercial, documentation, or technical. Owner role: who must decide, using a role rather than a person's name. Decision: accept, query, or escalate. Decision date and reference: when it was settled and where the evidence sits.

Two practical rules keep the log honest. First, record rewordings even when the meaning appears unchanged, because a reworded clause can matter later. Second, never mark a technical difference as accepted by procurement alone. The impact area column exists precisely so that technical items are routed rather than absorbed.

When a supplier's paperwork covers several product families at once, the log can become long. Grouping lines by impact area, rather than by page order, usually makes the review faster because each reviewer sees only their own block.

For teams that also handle reclaimed material and power module documentation, the same log structure carries over with different field names. The San Techno resources section is a sensible place to look for related templates before inventing a new format.

Which fields need approval and which need engineering escalation

The most common failure in revision comparison is not a missed difference. It is a difference that was noticed, discussed, and then quietly absorbed by whoever happened to be reading the document. A simple matrix prevents that.

Fields that typically need a procurement approval before the new revision becomes current:

Commercial terms, delivery windows and order references. Supplier identity details and document references. Packaging and labelling descriptions, where they affect receiving checks. Any field that changes what the buying desk will order next.

Fields that typically need engineering escalation rather than procurement sign-off:

Any technical parameter, grade description or performance statement. Any change to how a material is described in a way that could affect its use. Any difference in test or inspection references named in the document.

Fields that usually need both, in sequence:

A change that alters a technical description and also affects what is ordered. Engineering confirms the technical reading first; procurement then confirms the commercial consequence.

This matrix is an author heuristic for organising decisions, not an industry standard. Adapt the categories to your own approval structure and record the adaptation so that later reviewers understand why a field sits where it does.

One caution applies throughout. A revision label is an identifier, not proof that the underlying material is equivalent. Two documents with similar labels can describe different things, and the comparison should surface that possibility rather than assume it away.

Keep the original intact when the comparison is finished

Closing a comparison is a small ceremony that protects the record. A short close-out routine works well:

Confirm that both original files are still stored, unedited, in their own folders. Confirm that the register shows the older revision as superseded rather than deleted. Attach the completed change log to the register entry for the newer revision. Record the approval reference and date against each accepted line. Note any open queries and who owns them, so the file does not close with silent gaps.

Retention is a matter for your own records policy, so this guide does not set a period. What it does suggest is that the retention decision be written down once, in the register's header, rather than decided case by case.

A final check is worth the two minutes it takes: ask a colleague who was not involved to open the register and explain, from the record alone, which revision is current and why. If they cannot, the record is incomplete regardless of how thorough the comparison felt at the time.

San Techno's own work spans silicon recovery and reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, alongside power module development and botanical extracts. A buying team that sources across such different families will meet very different revision habits, which is all the more reason to keep one consistent register and one consistent change log across the whole portfolio.

A one-page worksheet you can copy

The worksheet below condenses the guide into a single page. It is a format suggestion, not a scored assessment, and the fields should be trimmed to fit your team.

Header: record ID, product family, time zone used, retention note owner. Revision block A: label as printed, date received, source, storage location, status. Revision block B: the same five fields for the newer revision. Comparison summary: number of lines logged, split by impact area. Approval block: procurement approver role, engineering reviewer role, dates, references. Open items block: query reference, owner role, target response date. Close-out block: originals confirmed, register updated, change log attached, sign-off date.

Two habits make the worksheet last. First, keep it short enough that people actually complete it. Second, review the worksheet itself once a quarter and remove any field that nobody has used, because unused fields train people to skip the whole page.

When a comparison involves power module documentation, the same worksheet applies but the escalation column will carry more weight, since design details in that area are treated as confidential and are not for general circulation. Procurement's job is to route the question to the right colleague, not to interpret the technical content.

Used consistently, the register, the timestamps, the change log and the worksheet turn a routine document comparison into a record that survives staff changes, supplier queries and the ordinary passage of time.
