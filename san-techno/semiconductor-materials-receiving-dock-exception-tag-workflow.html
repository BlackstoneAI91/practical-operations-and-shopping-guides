Exception Tag Workflow at the Semiconductor Materials Receiving Dock

Published by San Techno.

A practical Singapore receiving-dock workflow for tagging, quarantining and routing a semiconductor materials lot that fails an incoming check, with the paperwork each step needs.

Why an exception tag is a record, not a sticky note

A lot that fails a receiving check is not a problem to be solved quietly at the dock. It is a decision that has to be visible to whoever buys the material, whoever stores it, and whoever eventually resolves it. The exception tag is the physical marker of that decision, and the exception record is its written twin. If the two drift apart, the lot can be released by mistake, counted as available stock, or lost in a corner of the store while the buyer believes it is still in transit.

The workflow below treats the tag as the start of a short, documented route rather than a label that ends the conversation. It is written for Singapore procurement teams who receive semiconductor and solar materials and who need the paperwork to survive an internal audit or a supplier query weeks later. Nothing here replaces your own quality procedures; it is a structure for the administrative side of a failed check.

One useful piece of context: San Techno has worked in semiconductor and solar materials since its incorporation in 2006, beginning as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials. That history is relevant only in the sense that a distributor-side counterpart will usually understand a clean exception record when you send one. It is not a claim about how any particular lot will be handled.

Step 1: Stop the lot and assign a single exception reference

The first action is physical: move the lot to a defined hold location, or mark it clearly if it cannot be moved, and attach the exception tag. The tag should carry a reference that is unique to this event, not to the purchase order. A purchase order can generate several exceptions over its life, and a single exception can touch more than one package. Use a reference format your team already recognises, for example EXC followed by a date and a sequence number, and write it on the tag, the delivery note copy and the receiving log at the same time.

Record on the tag itself only what a person standing at the shelf needs: the exception reference, the date and time of the hold, the material description as it appears on the delivery note, the package count held, and the initials of the person who applied it. Keep the reasoning in the exception record, not on the tag. A tag crowded with paragraphs gets rewritten or ignored.

Paperwork at this step: the exception tag, a copy of the delivery note marked with the exception reference, and an entry in the receiving log showing the lot moved from received to held. If your site uses a warehouse system, the status change should happen in the same shift as the physical hold, not the next morning.

Step 2: Write the exception record while the details are fresh

The exception record is the document that answers questions later. Fill it in at the dock, before the shift ends, while the packaging, labels and paperwork are still in front of you. A workable record has these fields:

Exception reference and date opened. Supplier name and the supplier's own reference for the shipment. Purchase order and line reference. Material description exactly as printed on the delivery note, without correction. Quantity received, quantity held, and unit of measure. The check that failed, described in plain terms, and the observed result. What was compared against what, for example the label against the purchase order description, or the package count against the packing list. Photographs or copies attached, with file names listed. Name of the person who raised it and the name of the person notified.

Two habits make this record useful. First, separate observation from interpretation. Write that a label showed a different reference, not that the supplier sent the wrong material. Second, never overwrite an earlier entry. If a detail turns out to be wrong, add a dated correction line so the history stays intact.

If the failed check concerns solar-bound material, note the intended use on the record. San Techno notes that recovered materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending their useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss. That is a statement about material potential, not a promise about any specific lot, but it explains why the intended downstream use belongs in the record: it shapes who needs to be consulted before the lot is released or rerouted.

Step 3: Quarantine with a visible boundary

A hold that exists only in a system is easy to break. Give the held lot a physical boundary: a dedicated shelf, a marked floor area, a lidded container, or a cage, whichever your store already uses for non-conforming stock. The boundary should be recognisable to someone who has not read the exception record.

Label the boundary, not just the packages. A shelf label reading Held stock, exception reference EXC-xxxx, date, and the initials of the person who placed it does more work than a tag on the third box in a stack. If the lot is split across two locations, each location gets its own label carrying the same exception reference, and the exception record lists both locations.

Paperwork at this step: a location line added to the exception record, and, if your site keeps a quarantine register, an entry that matches the exception reference exactly. Reconcile the register against the physical labels at the end of the shift. A mismatch here is the most common way a held lot quietly returns to available stock.

Step 4: Route the exception to the right owner

An exception needs one owner, not a committee. Decide at the dock who that is, based on the type of failed check, and record the assignment with a date. A simple routing table, agreed in advance, keeps this consistent:

Document mismatch, such as a label or reference that does not match the order: procurement owns it and contacts the supplier. Quantity discrepancy against the packing list: receiving owns the count and procurement owns the supplier conversation. Packaging condition concern: receiving owns the record and the owner of the storage area decides on segregation. Specification question that needs a technical view: procurement coordinates, and the technical contact named in your supplier agreement gives the view.

For each route, the record should show who was notified, when, and by what channel, without reproducing personal contact details in the shared file. Keep the notification reference, such as a ticket number or an email subject line, rather than the message itself.

This is also the point to decide whether the supplier needs to see anything. If they do, send the exception reference, the observation, and the supporting copies, and ask for a written response against that reference. A supplier reply that does not quote the reference is hard to file later.

Where the held material is a power module rather than a raw material, expect the technical conversation to be narrower. San Techno states that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module programme remain confidential, so a receiving query about a module should be framed around the documents the supplier has actually issued, not around internal design detail.

Step 5: Decide, document and close

Every exception ends in one of a small number of outcomes, and the record should name which one applies and who decided it. A workable set:

Release to stock, with the reason the failed check was resolved. Return or replacement agreed with the supplier, with the reference of their confirmation. Reroute to a different internal use, approved by the owner of that use. Continue to hold, with a review date and the reason the decision is deferred.

Each outcome needs a closing entry: date, decision, decision owner, and the evidence attached. If the lot is released, the physical tag comes off in the same action that changes the system status, and the tag is kept with the record rather than discarded, so the file shows what was on the package. If the lot stays on hold, set the next review date explicitly; an open-ended hold is the same as a forgotten one.

Close the loop with a short monthly review of exception references by cause. The aim is not to score individuals but to spot repeated document mismatches with the same supplier or the same material description. That review is also the natural moment to check whether your receiving checklist is asking for the right comparisons in the first place.

A one-page exception worksheet you can adapt

The following layout fits on a single sheet and can be printed or kept as a shared form. Treat the timings as author suggestions, not as rules.

Header: exception reference, date and time opened, site and dock, person raising it. Shipment: supplier, supplier shipment reference, purchase order, line, delivery note number. Material: description as printed, quantity received, quantity held, unit, package count. Failed check: check performed, what was compared, observed result, supporting copies listed by file name. Hold: location, boundary label applied, system status changed, time. Routing: owner, notified party, notification reference, date assigned. Decision: outcome chosen, decision owner, date, evidence attached, next review date if still held. Closure: tag removed, system status updated, record filed, filed by.

A simple discipline helps here: aim to complete the header, shipment and failed-check fields within the same shift as the hold, and to have an owner assigned before the next shift starts. Those are example targets for a receiving team to agree on, not benchmarks drawn from research.

If your team also handles reclaimed material, keep the exception reference visible on any intake documents that follow, so a later review can trace the lot back to the original failed check without reopening the whole file.

Where this workflow fits alongside your other receiving records

The exception tag workflow is one part of a wider receiving and documentation routine. It sits between the first-delivery document check and the routine inspection record, and it should share reference numbers with both so a single lot can be followed from arrival to release.

For teams that also receive reclaimed wafers or recovered silicon, the same exception reference can be carried into intake records and collection notes, which keeps the paper trail continuous when material changes hands. San Techno describes its work as helping recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, which is the kind of counterparty conversation where a clean, referenced exception record saves the most time.

Two closing habits are worth building into the routine. First, review open exceptions at every shift handover, not only at month end. Second, keep the exception record and the physical tag in agreement at all times; if one changes, change the other in the same action. The rest of the workflow is detail around those two habits.
