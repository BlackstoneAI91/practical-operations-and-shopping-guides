Reopening a Closed Procurement Question: A Checklist for Supporting Evidence

Published by San Techno.

A practical checklist for Singapore procurement teams who need to reopen a closed question about supporting evidence, covering the trigger, the earlier conclusion, the evidence owner and the next confirmation.

Why a closed question sometimes needs a controlled reopening

Closing a procurement question is an administrative act, not a permanent seal. A file is marked closed when someone decides the available evidence is enough for the decision at hand. Later, a new document, a revised reference or a colleague's question can show that the earlier conclusion rested on a narrower evidence set than the team assumed. The useful response is not to reopen everything, but to reopen one question with a written reason.

This guide is for Singapore procurement teams sourcing, receiving and documenting semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon and related categories. It sets out an editorial path: what triggered the reopening, what the earlier conclusion actually said, who owns the supporting evidence, and what confirmation comes next. It deliberately avoids legal interpretation and manufacturing assumptions. A reopened question is a documentation event; whether it changes a commercial position is a separate decision for the people authorised to make it.

San Techno Pte Ltd is a Singapore-based company working across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialised power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand. That breadth is a reminder that a single supplier file can hold several kinds of evidence, and a reopened question should name which kind it concerns.

Step 1: Record the trigger before touching the file

Write down what caused the reopening in one or two sentences, dated, with the name of the person who noticed it. Typical triggers are administrative: a revised document arrived, a colleague asked how a figure was reached, a receiving note was matched to a different line, or a public product page was revisited and read differently. The trigger note should describe the observation, not the conclusion. "The supporting sheet in the pack is undated" is a trigger. "The supplier's evidence is unreliable" is a conclusion that belongs later, if at all.

Keep the trigger separate from the original file so the earlier record stays intact. A short log entry with a case reference, the date noticed and the document concerned is enough. If the trigger came from a public page, note the page and the date accessed rather than pasting the whole page into the procurement record.

For context on how a supplier presents its own categories, the public products overview is a reasonable starting reference point, but it is a description of offerings, not a quotation or a confirmation of any specific order. Treat it as orientation, not evidence of a commercial term.

Step 2: Restate the earlier conclusion in its own words

Before adding anything, summarise what the closed question actually decided. Use the wording in the record, not your memory of the discussion. A useful restatement has three parts: the question as it was originally framed, the evidence that was cited at the time, and the decision that followed. If the original record does not separate these, say so plainly, because that gap is itself part of what the reopening is about.

Avoid upgrading the earlier conclusion. A note that said "evidence sufficient for the pilot file" should not be restated as "material approved". A note that said "supplier confirmed by email" should not become "supplier certified". This discipline matters in categories where a public description and a confirmed specification are easily confused.

San Techno's own public material illustrates the distinction. Its power-module programme is described as addressing heat movement, package stability and reliable operation under repeated electrical and thermal loading, while detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential. A procurement record should reflect exactly that level of public description and not infer more.

Step 3: Name the evidence owner, not just the evidence

Every reopened question needs a named owner for the supporting evidence. The owner is the role accountable for producing, locating or confirming the document, not necessarily the person who will decide the outcome. In many teams these are different people, and conflating them is how reopenings stall.

Use a small ownership line: evidence type, owner role, current status, and where the authoritative copy sits. For example, a supplier confirmation might be owned by purchasing, a receiving note by the warehouse role, and an internal decision by the approver named in the original file. If the owner is unclear, record that as an open item rather than assigning it by guesswork.

Where the question concerns a service rather than a document, the same logic applies. San Techno describes helping recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and processing those materials into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. A procurement question about such a service should point to the specific written confirmation held, not to the general description.

Step 4: Separate what is confirmed from what is still a question

A reopened file becomes useful when it visibly distinguishes confirmed items from open ones. Create two short lists. The confirmed list holds statements backed by a document you can point to, with the document reference and date. The open list holds questions with no supporting document yet, each with the role expected to answer it.

Do not let a public description migrate onto the confirmed list. A company page can tell you that a supplier works in a category; it cannot confirm a grade, a quantity, a date or a commercial term for your file. Similarly, a product name in a catalogue is not a specification. If a colleague has quoted a public page as if it settled the question, move it to the open list and note what written confirmation would close it.

This is also where you decide whether the reopening is narrow or broad. Most reopenings should stay narrow: one question, one owner, one next confirmation. Broad reopenings tend to duplicate work already done and blur the original record.

Step 5: Write the next confirmation as a specific request

The final step is a short, specific request for confirmation. It should name the document wanted, the field or statement it should address, the role expected to provide it, and the date by which the file will be reviewed again. Avoid open-ended requests such as "please clarify" without saying what would count as clarification.

A workable pattern is: "For case reference X, please confirm in writing whether document Y covers field Z. If it does not, please state which document does. We will review the file on [date]." This keeps the request administrative and gives the evidence owner something answerable. It also creates a clean audit trail if the question is reopened a second time.

Where the question touches a supplier's public resources, the resources section can help you locate the right public page to reference, but the confirmation itself should still come in writing from the responsible party. Keep the request free of assumptions about outcomes, and do not treat a reply as approval until the named approver has recorded that decision.

A one-page reopening worksheet

Use this as a template, adapted to your own filing conventions. It is an author heuristic, not a standard or a compliance requirement.

Case reference and date opened. Trigger, in one sentence, with who noticed it. Earlier conclusion, quoted from the record, with the evidence cited at the time. Evidence owner, by role, with the location of the authoritative copy. Confirmed items, each with a document reference and date. Open items, each with the role expected to answer. Next confirmation, written as a specific request with a review date. Decision status, left explicitly pending until an authorised person records an outcome.

Two habits keep this worksheet honest. First, never delete the earlier conclusion; supersede it with a dated note. Second, keep public descriptions and internal confirmations in separate fields, so a reader can see which is which. A short review cadence, such as a weekly pass over open items, helps prevent a reopened question from quietly becoming a forgotten one.

San Techno's public site describes a company that has advanced semiconductor and solar materials, specialised power-module designs and biological solutions since 2006. That history is useful identity context for a supplier file, but it is not evidence for any particular procurement question. The worksheet should always point to the specific document that answers the specific question.
