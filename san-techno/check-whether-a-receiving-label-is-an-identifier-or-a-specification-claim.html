Check Whether a Receiving Label Is an Identifier or a Specification Claim

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on reading receiving labels: separating lot identifiers, grade descriptions and verified inspection documents, with fictional examples and a worksheet.

Why the label question matters at the receiving desk

A carton arrives at the dock. The label carries a short code, a grade word, a quantity and a date. Someone scans it, someone else reads it aloud, and a third person writes a line into the receiving record. Within a minute, a string of characters has been treated as if it were a statement about what is inside the box.

That is the moment this guide is about. A receiving label can serve two very different jobs. It can be an identifier, meaning a handle that lets you find the right record, the right lot and the right paperwork. Or it can read like a specification claim, meaning a statement that the contents meet a described grade, condition or property. The two jobs are not interchangeable, and treating one as the other is a quiet source of mismatched records.

For a Singapore procurement team sourcing semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon, reclaimed wafers or power modules, the practical risk is administrative rather than dramatic. A lot gets booked in under a grade word that nobody verified. A query is raised against the wrong reference. A document set is filed under a label that turns out to be a supplier's internal shorthand. None of these are failures of the material; they are failures of reading.

This page gives you a way to tell the two apart before anything is accepted, using fictional examples and a short worksheet you can adapt to your own receiving desk.

Three things a label is often confused with

Before the worksheet, it helps to name the three artefacts that get mixed together on a receiving dock.

First, the identifier. This is a code, a lot number, a pallet reference or a supplier's own tracking string. Its only job is to point at a record. It does not assert quality. If a label says something like LOT-4471-B, the useful question is not what it means but where it is defined and which document it links to.

Second, the grade description. This is wording that describes a category or condition, such as a grade name, a surface descriptor or a product family term. Grade words are meaningful only against a definition. Two suppliers can use the same word for different things, and one supplier can use it loosely on a carton while using it precisely in a specification. A grade word on a label is a pointer to a definition, not the definition itself.

Third, the verified inspection document. This is the record that actually carries the checked result, the date, the method and the person or body responsible. It is the only one of the three that can support a statement about what was verified, and even then only for the fields it covers.

The confusion arises because all three can appear in the same square centimetre of a carton. A label may show an identifier, a grade word and a tick mark that looks like an inspection result. Only one of those is a handle, one is a pointer, and one may be a claim. Your receiving record should say which is which.

A fictional example: the same carton, read three ways

Consider a fictional delivery to a fictional Singapore receiving desk. The carton label reads: STX-2291 / Grade A / 24 pcs / 12-03.

Read as an identifier, the useful part is STX-2291. The receiver's job is to find where STX-2291 is defined, confirm it matches the purchase order line, and attach the label reference to the receiving record. Nothing about quality has been asserted.

Read as a specification claim, the label appears to say the contents are Grade A. But Grade A is undefined in this example. It might be the supplier's internal sorting term, a commercial shorthand, or a genuine reference to a published grade. Until someone locates the definition and the document that confirms it, the label has told you a word, not a fact.

Read as a verified inspection document, the label tells you nothing at all. The date 12-03 could be a packing date, a label print date or an inspection date. The tick mark, if there is one, is not a result.

The disciplined reading is to log STX-2291 as the identifier, log Grade A as a quoted label term pending definition, and log the inspection status as not yet evidenced. That third entry is the one that stops a lot being booked in as if it had been checked when it has only been labelled.

A second fictional example shows the reverse problem. A label reads WFR-88 / Reclaim / 25 pcs. Here Reclaim is a category word, not a grade. The receiver should not treat it as a statement that the wafers meet any particular condition. It is a description that needs to be matched against the supplier's own definition and the accompanying documents before the lot is recorded as anything more than a category.

A receiving label worksheet you can adapt

The worksheet below is a suggested format, not a standard. Adapt the column names to your own records system. The aim is to force a decision on each label field before the lot is booked in.

Column one: label field as written. Copy the characters exactly, including punctuation and case. Do not tidy them.

Column two: proposed role. Choose one of identifier, grade description, quantity, date, or unknown. If you cannot choose, write unknown. Unknown is a legitimate and useful entry.

Column three: definition source. For an identifier, name the record where it is defined. For a grade description, name the document that defines the term. If there is no source, leave it blank and flag it.

Column four: supporting document. Name the document that would confirm any claim the field appears to make. If none has arrived, write pending.

Column five: decision. Choose one of accept as identifier, hold for definition, hold for document, or query supplier. Keep the list short so it is used.

Column six: owner and date. A named role, not a person's contact details, and the date the decision was made.

A worked fictional row might read: STX-2291, identifier, receiving register, purchase order line, accept as identifier, receiving lead, 12-03. A second row might read: Grade A, grade description, none located, pending, hold for definition, receiving lead, 12-03.

The value of the worksheet is not the form itself. It is that it makes the receiver write down which role each field is playing. A label that has been assigned a role cannot quietly become a claim later.

When the label and the documents disagree

Disagreement is normal and should be expected. The label says one thing, the packing list says another, and the specification uses a third term. The question is what your team does next.

A simple rule helps: the label never wins an argument with a verified document, and a verified document never silently overrides an identifier. If the label code does not match the purchase order line, that is an identifier problem and it should be resolved before anything else. If the grade word on the label does not match the grade word in the specification, that is a definition problem and it should be routed to whoever owns the definition. If an inspection document is missing, that is a document problem and the lot should be held rather than accepted on the strength of the label.

For teams handling power modules, the same logic applies with different vocabulary. A label on a double-sided cooling module carton may carry a package reference and a date. Neither is a statement about thermal performance. The public description of San Techno's power-module programme notes that it addresses heat movement, package stability and reliable operation under repeated electrical and thermal loading, and that design validation draws on thermal modelling, finite-element analysis, power cycling and infrared measurement. Those are descriptions of a development programme, not fields you can read off a carton label. Keep them where they belong: in your understanding of the supplier, not in your receiving record.

A useful habit is to keep a short disagreement log. One line per mismatch, with the field, the two sources, the decision and the owner. Over a few months, the log shows which label fields repeatedly cause trouble, and that is the evidence you take to a supplier review rather than a general impression.

Handing the decision to the next shift

A receiving decision made at four in the afternoon is only useful if the next shift can find it. That means the worksheet and the disagreement log need a handover convention.

Keep the handover short. One page is enough: open label queries, lots held for definition, lots held for documents, and the owner of each. Do not restate the whole receiving record. The next shift needs to know what is unresolved and who is expected to resolve it.

Separate the three hold types clearly, because they route differently. An identifier hold goes back to the buyer or the order owner. A definition hold goes to whoever maintains your controlled vocabulary of grade terms. A document hold goes to the supplier query owner. Mixing them into a single pending list is how a lot sits untouched for a week.

Finally, close the loop in writing. When a held label is resolved, record which document resolved it and which role made the call. That single line is what turns a receiving desk's daily judgement into a record that survives staff changes, audits and the next delivery of the same product.

The broader lesson is modest but durable: a label is a prompt to look something up, not a conclusion. Teams that treat it that way spend less time correcting records and more time moving material through the dock.
