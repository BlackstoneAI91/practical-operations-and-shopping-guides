A Wafer Reclaim Record When Intake Photos Arrive After the Collection Note

Published by San Techno.

An administrative reconciliation guide for Singapore procurement teams: how to log timestamps, receipt checks and release controls when wafer reclaim intake photos arrive after the collection note, with a fictional exception note and a reusable worksheet.

Why the order of records matters more than the order of events

A collection note and a set of intake photos describe the same handover from two different positions. The collection note is written at the point of dispatch, usually by the party releasing the material. The intake photos are taken at the receiving end, sometimes hours later, sometimes the next working day. When the photos arrive after the note, nothing has necessarily gone wrong. What has changed is the sequence of evidence, and that sequence is what a procurement file has to explain.

The practical problem is not the delay itself. It is that a reviewer opening the file later sees a note dated before the photos and cannot tell whether the gap was a normal transit interval, a late upload, or a genuine discrepancy that was never resolved. A reclaim record that answers that question in one place is worth more than a folder of images with no narrative.

This guide is written for Singapore procurement teams who source, receive and document semiconductor and solar materials. It covers record structure, timestamp fields, receipt checks and release controls. It does not describe handling, processing or disposal steps, and it does not replace your own quality or compliance procedures. San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. That is the commercial context in which a reclaim record usually sits.

The three clocks in a reclaim file

Most confusion in a late-photo file comes from treating every date as the same kind of date. Separate them into three clocks and the reconciliation becomes much simpler.

Clock one is the commercial clock: when the collection was agreed, when the note was issued, and which reference number the note carries. Clock two is the physical clock: when the material left the releasing site and when it was received. Clock three is the evidence clock: when the photos were captured and when they were filed against the record.

A late photo is only a problem when the evidence clock is confused with the physical clock. If the photo file metadata shows capture on the same day as receipt, but the upload happened two days later, the physical timeline is intact. If the capture date itself is after the receipt date, you have a genuine gap that needs an explanation before the record can be closed.

A simple field set handles this without software. For each lot, record: collection note reference, note issue date and time, dispatch date and time, receipt date and time, photo capture date and time, photo filing date and time, and the name of the person who filed them. Add one free-text field for the reason the photos arrived late. That last field is the one reviewers actually read.

Where the material is being routed toward reclaim rather than resale, the record should also state which destination the lot is being documented for, so that the file is not later confused with a different inquiry. Receipt checks that do not depend on the photos

The temptation when photos arrive late is to let them carry the whole verification burden. That is a mistake, because photos are the weakest link in the chain precisely when they are late. Build the receipt check on things that exist independently of the images.

Start with the collection note itself. Confirm that the reference on the note matches the reference on the receiving log, that the stated package or carrier count matches what was signed for, and that the description of the material is consistent between the two documents. If the note says one thing and the receiving log says another, the photos will not settle it; only a written clarification will.

Next, confirm the parties. The note should identify who released the material and who accepted it. A late photo set often arrives with a different name attached than the one on the note, which is normal when a receiving team works in shifts, but the record should name both and state the relationship.

Finally, confirm the scope. A reclaim record should say what is being documented, not just that something was collected. If the lot is part of a broader silicon recovery arrangement, the record should reference the arrangement rather than restating it. San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. These materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending their useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss. Those are the outcomes the file is supporting; the receipt check is what makes the file credible.

A useful discipline is to mark each receipt check as confirmed, queried or not applicable, with the initials of the checker and the date. Three states are enough. More than that and people stop filling it in.

A fictional exception note for a late photo set

The following is an invented example for illustration only. It shows the shape of a short exception note, not a real transaction.

Lot reference: RC-EXAMPLE-0417. Collection note issued on a Monday at 09:40 by the releasing site. Material acknowledged as received on the same Monday at 15:20 by the receiving team. Intake photos captured on the Monday at 15:35 but filed against the record on Wednesday at 11:05, after the receiving supervisor returned from leave.

Exception recorded: the evidence clock lags the physical clock by approximately two working days. Cause: filing delay, not a transit delay. Supporting items: the photo capture timestamp, the receiving log signature, and a short email from the supervisor confirming the filing date. Resolution: the record is marked complete with the delay noted, and the lot is cleared for the next administrative stage. No quantity or description discrepancy was found.

What makes this note useful is that it separates the delay from any substantive issue. A reviewer can see at a glance that the material arrived when it was supposed to and that only the paperwork lagged. Compare that with a note that simply says "photos received late" and leaves the reader to guess whether the delay hid something.

Write the exception note in the same structure every time: reference, dates, what lagged, why, what supports the explanation, and the resolution. Consistency is what allows a reviewer to scan twenty records quickly rather than reading each one from scratch.

Release controls: who signs off, and on what

A reclaim record is not closed when the photos are filed. It is closed when someone with the right authority releases it. Two separate decisions usually sit inside that release, and conflating them is a common source of delay.

The first decision is documentary: is the file complete enough to stand on its own? That question belongs to whoever owns the record, and it should be answerable against a checklist rather than a feeling. The second decision is commercial or operational: is the lot cleared to move to the next stage? That question belongs to a different person, often in operations or quality, and it should not be made until the first decision is recorded.

A workable split is to have the record owner sign the completeness check and a second approver sign the release. Where the two roles sit with the same person, note that explicitly, because a single signature on both lines invites questions later.

Release controls should also state what happens when the exception is unresolved. The safest default is that an unresolved exception holds the record open and the lot stays in a pending state, with a named owner and a review date. A pending state with a date is far better than a closed record with a silent gap.

For teams documenting a lot that may later be routed toward disposal rather than reclaim, keep the release decision separate from the routing decision. The two inquiries have different evidence needs, and merging them in one approval tends to produce a file that satisfies neither.

A worksheet you can reuse

The following fields can be copied into a spreadsheet or a document template. They are offered as an author's heuristic, not as an industry standard.

Header block: lot reference, material description, originating site, receiving site, collection note reference, note issue date and time.

Timeline block: dispatch date and time, receipt date and time, photo capture date and time, photo filing date and time, elapsed gap in working days, reason for gap.

Receipt check block: note reference matched (confirmed, queried, not applicable), package or carrier count matched, description consistent, releasing party named, receiving party named, checker initials, check date.

Exception block: exception reference, what lagged, cause, supporting items, resolution, resolution date, resolver name.

Release block: completeness sign-off name and date, release approval name and date, pending state if unresolved, review date, named owner.

A short scoring aid can help triage a backlog. For example, treat a record as green when all receipt checks are confirmed and the gap is explained; amber when one check is queried but the gap is explained; red when any check is queried and the gap is unexplained. That three-level scheme is a suggested convention, not a measured benchmark, and teams should adjust it to their own risk appetite.

Keep the worksheet version-controlled. When the template changes, note the change date and which records were completed under the earlier version, so that a later reviewer is not comparing files built to different rules.

Where this record fits in the wider materials file

A single reclaim record rarely stands alone. It usually sits inside a procurement file that also covers specification comparison, supplier correspondence and, where relevant, power module or botanical extract lines. Keeping the reclaim record structurally distinct from those other documents makes the whole file easier to audit.

One practical habit is to give every document in the file a type label at the top: collection note, intake record, exception note, release approval, specification sheet. A reviewer can then sort by type rather than by date, which is often the faster route to the document they need.

Another habit is to record the destination of each inquiry at the point the record is opened. A lot being documented for reclaim, a lot being documented for collection, and a lot being documented for disposal are three different files even when they concern similar material. Naming the destination early prevents the most common late-stage problem, which is a complete record attached to the wrong inquiry.

Finally, keep the record factual. A reclaim file should describe what was collected, when, by whom, and what evidence supports it. It should not contain conclusions about material quality or future use unless those conclusions were separately approved and referenced. The record's job is to make the handover legible, and a legible handover is what allows the next team to act without re-interviewing everyone involved.
