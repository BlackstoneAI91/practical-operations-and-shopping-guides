Include a Controlled Translation Field in a Silicon Materials Comparison

Published by San Techno.

A practical worksheet for Singapore procurement teams that keeps original wording, translation, translator notes and unresolved fields separate when comparing silicon materials documents, without assuming a translated grade name proves equivalency.

Why a translated grade name is not a comparison result

A comparison table that mixes original wording and translated wording in the same cell looks finished long before it is. A buyer reads a grade description in English, assumes it maps cleanly onto the supplier's original-language document, and books the lot against a name that may only be an approximation. The problem is not translation itself; it is losing the trail between what the document said and what the buyer understood.

This guide sets out a worksheet that keeps four things apart: the original wording, the translation, the translator's notes, and the fields nobody has resolved yet. It is written for Singapore procurement teams who source, receive and document semiconductor materials and solar materials, and who need a comparison record that survives an audit or a shift change.

The four-column rule that keeps a comparison honest

The core discipline is simple: never let a translated term sit in the same column as the original term. Build the worksheet with at least these columns.

Column one, Original wording: copy the exact characters or words from the supplier document, including any grade label, suffix or qualifier. Do not tidy spelling or normalise punctuation here.

Column two, Translation: the working English rendering, marked as a translation rather than a quotation.

Column three, Translator notes: who produced the translation, what source they used, and any term they were unsure about. A note such as "literal rendering; supplier uses this term for two different lines" is more useful than a confident but unverified gloss.

Column four, Status: one of Confirmed, Provisional or Unresolved. Only a supplier's own bilingual document or a written clarification should move a field to Confirmed.

A fifth column for the evidence reference, such as a document name and revision, ties each row back to its source. Keep it short; the point is traceability, not a filing system inside a spreadsheet.

This structure matters most where a translated name looks reassuringly familiar. Familiarity is not confirmation. A grade name that reads naturally in English may still describe a different scope, a different qualifier or a different document revision than the buyer assumes.

Where silicon recovery and solar material descriptions create translation risk

Descriptions of recovered and reusable material are especially prone to loose translation, because the same material can be described by what it was, what it became, or what it is intended for. San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and these materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending their useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss. Those two statements describe a flow, not a grade. A translated label that compresses the flow into a single product name has quietly lost information the buyer may need.

When a supplier document describes incoming material in another language, keep the original description of the source and the intended use in separate rows. A row that says only "recovered silicon" tells the receiving team very little; a row that records the original phrase, the translation, and the translator's note about which stage of the flow it refers to gives the team something to check.

A worked fictional example of the worksheet in use

The following is an invented example for illustration only; it does not describe any real supplier, grade or document.

A buyer receives a two-page material description in another language. Row one records the original grade label exactly as printed, a working translation, a note that the translator was unsure whether the label refers to a wafer category or a packaging category, and a status of Unresolved. Row two records a second label that appears in the same document, its translation, a note that the supplier's own bilingual summary uses a different English word for the same term, and a status of Provisional. Row three records a quantity unit, its translation, and a status of Confirmed because the supplier's bilingual summary matches.

The value of the example is in what the buyer does next. The Unresolved row goes to the supplier as a written question, not to the purchase order. The Provisional row is flagged for the next document revision. The Confirmed row can be used in the comparison table. Nothing in the worksheet claims that the translated name proves the material is equivalent to anything else; it only records what is known and what is not.

A useful habit is to date each status change and note who made it. A status that flips from Unresolved to Confirmed without a recorded reason is a warning sign, not progress.

Separating translation questions from commercial and technical questions

Translation questions get tangled with commercial and technical questions when they share one inbox thread. Keep them apart.

A translation question is about meaning: what does this term refer to, and does the supplier's own bilingual document agree with our rendering? A commercial question is about scope, quantity, timing or validity. A technical question is about whether the material meets a requirement, and that belongs with engineering, not with the buyer's glossary.

Route each type to its own owner and record the routing in the worksheet. A translation query that is really a technical question should be reclassified rather than answered by the buyer. A commercial query that depends on an unresolved translation should wait until the translation is confirmed, or be marked as conditional.

This separation also protects the comparison from false confidence. A buyer who has resolved every translation question may still have an open technical question, and the worksheet should show that clearly rather than letting a tidy translation column imply the whole comparison is settled.

A short checklist before the comparison is circulated

Use this as an author heuristic, not as an audited standard. Score each item Yes, No or Not applicable, and treat any No on the first four items as a reason to hold circulation.

One: every translated term sits in its own column, separate from the original wording. Two: every row has a status of Confirmed, Provisional or Unresolved. Three: every Confirmed row cites a supplier bilingual document or a written clarification. Four: every Unresolved row has a named owner and a question recorded. Five: translator notes are present wherever the rendering was uncertain. Six: the worksheet records the document name and revision it was built from. Seven: commercial and technical questions have been routed away from the translation column. Eight: the comparison states plainly that a translated grade name is not evidence of equivalency.

A worked scoring example: a team scores six Yes, one No and one Not applicable. The No is on item three, because two Confirmed rows cite only an internal email. The team downgrades those rows to Provisional and circulates with a note. That is the checklist doing its job.

Keep the completed checklist with the worksheet so the next reviewer can see how the statuses were reached.

Keeping the worksheet useful after the comparison closes

A comparison worksheet has a second life after the purchase decision. It becomes the record of what was understood at the time, which is exactly what a receiving team or an auditor needs when a later document uses a different word for the same thing.

Retain the worksheet with its revision reference, its status history and its unresolved questions, even after every row is closed. When a supplier issues a new document revision, open a fresh copy rather than overwriting the old one, and carry forward only the rows whose evidence still applies. A translated term that was Confirmed against one revision may be Provisional against the next.

Finally, resist the temptation to publish the worksheet as a definitive glossary. A glossary implies settled definitions; a comparison worksheet records a specific reading of specific documents at a specific time. Keeping that distinction visible is what makes the controlled translation field worth the effort.
