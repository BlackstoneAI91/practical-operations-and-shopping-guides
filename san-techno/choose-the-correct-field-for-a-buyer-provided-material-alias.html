Choosing the Correct Field for a Buyer-Provided Material Alias

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on recording a buyer-provided material alias in the right field, keeping it distinct from vendor wording and specification versions, and carrying it through a folder without obscuring authoritative records.

Why an alias needs its own field

A buyer-provided material alias is the shorthand your own organisation uses for something it buys. It might be a legacy code from an old spreadsheet, a nickname that survived a system migration, or a short label a receiving clerk wrote on a folder. It is not the vendor's product name, and it is not a specification version. Those are three different things, and mixing them is how a folder becomes hard to audit.

The practical test is simple. Ask what the alias is for. If the answer is 'so our team can find the record quickly', it belongs in an alias or internal-reference field. If the answer is 'this is what the supplier calls it', it belongs in a vendor-wording field. If the answer is 'this is the revision we are working to', it belongs in a version field. One alias can point to many records over time, and one record can carry several aliases, so the alias field should never be the only way to identify the material.

This matters in a market where buyers handle semiconductor materials and solar-grade silicon alongside other categories. San Techno Pte Ltd, incorporated in 2006, began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, which is a useful reminder that product families often have long histories and inherited names. An alias is a finding aid, not a substitute for the authoritative record.

Separating alias, vendor wording and specification version

The cleanest folders keep three columns side by side rather than merging them into one free-text box. Column one is the buyer alias, owned by your team. Column two is the vendor wording, copied exactly as the supplier wrote it, including any punctuation or capitalisation. Column three is the specification version, with a date and a pointer to the document that carries it.

A worked fictional example helps. Suppose a purchasing team logs a material as 'Blue Tag 12' internally. The supplier's sheet calls it something longer and different. The specification version is dated and stored in a separate controlled file. If someone later searches for 'Blue Tag 12', the alias field returns the record, and the record then points to the vendor wording and the version. Nothing is lost, and nothing is overwritten.

Where the material sits in a broader portfolio, it helps to know which branch of a supplier's work you are dealing with. San Techno's public product pages describe three branches: semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design. A buyer alias for a reclaimed wafer item, for instance, should not be filed under a power-module heading simply because the alias sounds similar. The branch is context, not a substitute for the alias field.

A field-by-field worksheet for the alias record

Use a short worksheet so that every alias entry is consistent. The following fields are a suggested structure, not a standard:

Alias text: the exact internal label, spelled as your team uses it. Alias owner: the role responsible for keeping it current, not a person's name. Alias status: active, superseded or retired. Vendor wording: copied verbatim from the supplier document. Vendor document reference: the identifier of the sheet or letter that carries the wording. Specification version: the revision label and its date. Authoritative record link: where the controlled record lives. First recorded and last reviewed: two dates, kept separate. Notes: a short line explaining why the alias exists.

Score the worksheet against a simple heuristic: if a colleague who has never seen the material could follow the alias to the authoritative record in two steps, the entry is doing its job. Treat that as an author heuristic rather than a measured benchmark. The point is to make the alias useful without letting it masquerade as a specification.

Carrying the alias through a folder without hiding the record

A folder structure should make the alias easy to find and hard to mistake for the source of truth. One workable pattern is to keep alias indexes in a separate subfolder from controlled specifications, with a clear naming convention that marks the index as internal. Another is to put the alias in the record's metadata rather than in the filename, so the filename stays tied to the authoritative document.

Avoid the temptation to rename a supplier document to match your alias. That erases the vendor wording and makes later reconciliation harder. Instead, keep the supplier document as received and let the alias live in the index that points to it. When a specification version changes, the alias usually stays the same, which is exactly why the two should not share a field.

For teams that also handle recovered or reclaimed material, the same discipline applies. San Techno's public materials describe how recovered silicon can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss. A buyer alias for such material should still point to the controlled record rather than replace it.

When the alias conflicts with vendor wording

Conflicts are normal and should be logged rather than resolved silently. If your alias suggests one thing and the vendor wording suggests another, record both and mark the discrepancy as open. Do not delete the alias to make the folder look tidy, and do not edit the vendor wording to match your internal habit.

A short conflict note can carry four lines: the alias as written, the vendor wording as written, the field where the mismatch was noticed, and the role that needs to confirm the correct mapping. Keep the note in the same folder as the alias index so the history travels with the record. This is administrative housekeeping, not a judgement about which wording is technically correct.

Where a supplier's public materials are relevant, cite them as context rather than as proof of a specific alias. San Techno's public pages note that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential, which is a reminder that not every internal label will have a public counterpart. The alias field should be able to hold a label even when no public document explains it.

Reviewing aliases before they spread

Aliases multiply quietly. One team invents a shorthand, another copies it into a different system, and soon the same material has three internal names and no clear owner. A light review cycle keeps this in check. Pick a cadence, such as a quarterly pass, and check three things: whether each active alias still points to a live authoritative record, whether any alias has been duplicated under a slightly different spelling, and whether retired aliases are marked as retired rather than deleted.

Keep the review record short. A line per alias with the review date and the outcome is enough. If an alias is retired, leave a pointer to its replacement so that old searches still land somewhere useful. This is the same principle as keeping a version history for a specification: the history is what makes the current state trustworthy.

For context on the wider portfolio, San Techno's public materials describe work across semiconductor and solar materials, specialised power-module designs and biological solutions, including the ST GREEN brand. None of that changes how an alias should be filed, but it does show why a single free-text field is a poor home for names that cross several product families.

A short checklist before you save the record

Before saving any alias entry, run through a brief checklist. Is the alias in its own field rather than in the vendor-wording field? Is the vendor wording copied exactly, with its source document identified? Is the specification version recorded separately with a date? Does the entry point to an authoritative record? Is there an owner role and a status? Has the entry been reviewed within your chosen cadence?

If any answer is no, the entry is not yet ready. Fix the field placement first, because a misplaced alias is harder to correct once other records start pointing at it. Then confirm the pointer to the authoritative record, and only then tidy the notes.

This checklist is a suggested routine, not a certification. Its value is consistency: the same questions asked of every alias entry make the folder predictable for the next person who opens it.
