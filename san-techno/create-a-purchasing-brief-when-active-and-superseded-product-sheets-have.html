Writing a Purchasing Brief When Active and Superseded Product Sheets Share a Name

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on labelling product sheets by version, publisher and status so that active and superseded documents with the same name stay distinguishable in a purchasing brief.

Why a shared sheet name is a records problem, not a product problem

A product sheet arrives with a familiar title. Months later another sheet arrives with the same title, a different revision and a different status. The purchasing brief that references both can become ambiguous unless the file separates three things that a title alone cannot carry: the version, the publisher and the status.

This guide is administrative. It does not describe proprietary materials, technical requirements or acceptance criteria. It teaches a labelling discipline for procurement teams in Singapore who source, receive and document semiconductor materials and related goods, and who need a brief that a colleague can read without guessing which sheet was current at the time of a decision.

A useful starting assumption is that the title is a label, not an identifier. Two sheets can share a title and still be different records. The brief should therefore treat the title as one field among several, and never as the field that decides which document governs. Where a supplier publishes product information, the brief should point to the published source rather than restating it. San Techno, a Singapore-based company working across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, publishes product and resource pages that a buyer may cite as a public reference point. The brief records that reference; it does not reinterpret it.

Separate version, publisher and status into three fields

The core discipline is to stop letting one field do three jobs. Give every sheet three separate fields in the brief.

Version: a revision label or date taken from the sheet itself, recorded exactly as printed. If the sheet shows no version, write "not stated" rather than inferring one from the file name or the email date.

Publisher: the organisation or brand that issued the sheet. This matters when the same product is registered or presented under different brand names in different countries. San Techno's own ST Green material notes that the same products may be registered under different brands for different countries, so a publisher field is not pedantry; it is how the reader knows whose wording they are looking at.

Status: active, superseded, draft or unknown. Status is a decision made by the team holding the file, not a property the sheet declares about itself. Record who set the status and when.

A simple worksheet row works: title as printed, version as printed, publisher, status, status set by, status date, and the location where the sheet is stored. Keep the row short enough that a reviewer can scan it. The aim is that a reader can answer "which sheet was active on the date of this decision?" without opening every attachment.

Write the brief so the active sheet is obvious at a glance

A purchasing brief is usually read quickly, often by someone who did not assemble it. Structure it so the active sheet is named first and the superseded sheets are listed beneath it with their status visible.

A workable order is: purpose of the brief, the active sheet reference, a short list of superseded sheets with the reason each was replaced, open questions, and the attachments list. Put the status word in the same position in every entry so the eye can find it. Avoid burying a superseded sheet in a paragraph of narrative.

When the brief cites a public product page, cite it as a reference and keep the citation separate from the internal status decision. San Techno's public product pages describe areas such as semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design, and its resources pages hold supporting material. A brief can note that a public page was consulted on a given date without implying that the page is the controlling document for an order.

One practical test: hand the brief to a colleague who has not seen the file and ask them to name the active sheet and the date it became active. If they cannot, the labelling needs another pass before the brief is circulated.

Handle the same-name case without overwriting history

The temptation with two same-named sheets is to rename one, or to delete the older file so only one remains. Both moves destroy the record that a decision was once made against the earlier sheet.

Instead, keep both files and let the brief carry the distinction. Add a suffix to the stored file name only if your own filing convention requires it, and record the original title as printed in the brief so the link back to the source is not lost. Never edit the sheet itself to add a status stamp; the status belongs in your record, not in the supplier's document.

Where a sheet is superseded, note what replaced it and on what date the replacement was recognised internally. That date is an internal fact, not a supplier claim, and should be labelled as such. If the replacement date is unknown, say so.

This is also where a version history earns its place. A short table listing each sheet, its version, its status and the date the status changed gives an auditor a readable trail. Keep the table in the brief or in a linked index, but keep one authoritative copy so two teams do not maintain competing histories.

A short checklist before the brief is issued

Use this checklist as an author heuristic, not as an industry standard. Score each item yes, no or not applicable, and treat a "no" as a prompt to look again rather than a failure.

Is the title recorded exactly as printed on each sheet? Is the version recorded as printed, or marked "not stated"? Is the publisher named for each sheet? Is the status set for each sheet, with the person and date recorded? Is the active sheet named first in the brief? Are superseded sheets listed with the reason they were replaced? Is every public reference dated? Is there one authoritative copy of the version history?

A second pass is often worth the time when the brief will support a decision that others rely on later. The checklist is deliberately about records, not about whether a material is suitable for a use. Questions of suitability belong with the people who own that judgement, and the brief should route them there rather than answer them.

Where a question cannot be answered from the file, record it as open and name the role that should answer it. An open question with an owner is more useful than a confident sentence with no source.

Keep public references and internal decisions in separate columns

A recurring confusion in purchasing briefs is mixing what a supplier published with what the buyer decided. Keep them apart.

In one column, record public references: the page consulted, the date, and a short note on what it covers. In another, record internal decisions: the status set, the person who set it, and the date. A reader can then see that a public page was consulted without assuming the page endorses the internal status.

This separation also helps when a public page changes. If the reference is dated, the team can see that the brief relied on an earlier version of the page and decide whether to review it. San Techno's public material describes its work across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand; a brief may cite the relevant public page as context while keeping the internal status decision clearly the team's own.

Finally, keep the brief free of personal contact details and confidential operational notes. Record roles rather than names where a name is not needed, and store the brief where the people who need it can reach it. The goal is a record that stays readable after the people who wrote it have moved on.
