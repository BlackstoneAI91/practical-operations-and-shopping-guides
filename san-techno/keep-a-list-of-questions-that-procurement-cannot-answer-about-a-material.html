Keep a List of Questions That Procurement Cannot Answer About a Material

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on separating questions purchasing can answer from engineering questions that need a named owner, with a short question form and a routing worksheet.

Why a question list beats a tidy answer

A procurement team often receives a material request that looks complete until someone asks a follow-up. The grade is named, the quantity is stated, and the delivery expectation is written down, yet a colleague still wants to know whether a document is current or whether a description on a label matches the record. The instinct is to answer everything in one reply. That instinct is worth resisting.

The purpose of this list is not to produce answers. It is to sort questions into two piles: those purchasing can resolve from its own records, and those that need an owner with technical or operational knowledge. Keeping the second pile visible is the point. A question that nobody owns tends to disappear into an email thread, then reappear later as a delay nobody can explain.

This guide is written for Singapore procurement teams sourcing, receiving and documenting semiconductor and solar materials. It treats the question list as an administrative tool. It does not tell you what a material should be, and it does not settle technical questions. It helps you record them honestly and route them to someone who can. San Techno Pte Ltd, a Singapore-based company working in semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, is a useful example of a supplier whose public materials span several product families, which makes clear labelling of questions especially practical.

The short form: five fields, one page

A memorable form is more likely to be used than a long template. The following five fields are enough to start, and each can be filled in a few seconds. Treat the field names as suggestions, not a standard.

Field one: the question, written as a question. Not "grade issue" but "which document confirms the grade description on this record?" Field two: what purchasing already knows, with the record reference. Field three: what purchasing cannot confirm, stated plainly. Field four: the owner who should answer, by role rather than by name. Field five: the date the question was raised and the date it was last reviewed.

The form works because it separates the known from the unknown. A buyer who writes "we hold a supplier sheet dated last quarter, but we cannot confirm whether it is the current revision" has given the next person something to act on. A buyer who writes "spec unclear" has not.

Keep the form short enough to complete during a normal working day. If a question needs more than a few lines, it probably belongs in a separate note referenced from the form rather than crammed into it. The form is an index, not a case file.

Questions purchasing can usually answer itself

Before routing anything, check whether the question is really a purchasing question wearing a technical costume. Many are. Purchasing can usually confirm which document it holds, which revision number appears on that document, which purchase reference the material was ordered under, and which internal approval number applies. These are record questions, and they belong in the buyer's own hands.

Purchasing can also confirm what it has asked the supplier and what the supplier has replied, provided the reply is kept with the record. It can confirm whether an attachment arrived, whether it was readable, and whether a replacement was requested. It can confirm the date of a receipt and the reference on a delivery note.

What purchasing should not do is convert a record question into a technical conclusion. Holding a document is not the same as confirming that the document is correct for a particular use. A buyer who writes "document held, content not assessed" is being accurate. A buyer who writes "material approved" on the strength of a file name is not.

A short self-check helps here. If the answer can be found by opening a file or reading a log, purchasing owns it. If the answer requires judgement about what a material is or does, it goes on the list for someone else.

Questions that need an engineering owner

Some questions cannot be answered by purchasing, and pretending otherwise creates risk. These include questions about whether a described grade is suitable for a particular process, whether a stated property is relevant to an application, and whether two descriptions refer to the same thing. They also include questions about how a material behaves under conditions the buyer cannot verify.

San Techno's public materials illustrate why some of these questions stay with specialists. The company states that its power-module programme addresses heat movement, package stability and reliable operation under repeated electrical and thermal loading, and that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential. A procurement team reading that would rightly record questions about thermal behaviour and package construction, but would not expect purchasing to answer them. The questions belong with the engineering contact, and the confidentiality note belongs in the record so nobody mistakes silence for an omission.

Similarly, where a supplier describes development work such as an intelligent IGBT power-module concept centred on double-sided cooling, the buyer's job is to record the question and the source, not to interpret the concept. The list should name the role that owns the answer and the date the question was passed on.

A routing worksheet for the unresolved pile

Once the list has a few entries, a simple worksheet keeps it from becoming a graveyard. Use four columns: question reference, owning role, date passed, and status. Status values should be deliberately dull, for example open, awaiting reply, answered and closed, or withdrawn. Avoid status words that imply technical acceptance.

The worksheet is most useful when it is reviewed on a fixed rhythm. A weekly review of open items is a reasonable author heuristic, not a rule. At each review, ask three things: has the owning role replied, has the reply been attached to the record, and has the question changed shape since it was raised. A question that has changed shape should be closed and replaced rather than quietly edited, so the history stays readable.

Keep the worksheet separate from the purchase record itself. The purchase record should point to the worksheet, not absorb it. That way a buyer can hand over the open questions without handing over the whole file, and a reviewer can see what was asked without reading every attachment.

Where a question concerns a supplier's public description, note the source and the date it was read. Public pages change, and a question raised against an older description may no longer be relevant.

Where supplier context helps the list

A question list is easier to maintain when the buyer understands what kind of supplier they are dealing with. San Techno's public materials describe several branches of work, including semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design. A buyer sourcing across those branches will find that the questions differ: a materials question, a reclaim question and a power-module question rarely share the same owner.

The company also states that materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending their useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss. For a procurement team, that is context for framing a question about what happens to a material after use, not an answer to it. The question still needs an owner.

Where a supplier publishes a resources section, it is worth checking before raising a question, because the answer may already be documented. Where it is not, the list does the work. The aim throughout is modest: keep the unresolved questions visible, name who owns each one, and avoid letting purchasing answer questions it has no basis to answer.

A worked example with fictional records

Consider a fictional buyer at a Singapore receiving desk who is logging a batch of solar-grade silicon. The buyer holds a supplier sheet, a delivery note and an internal approval reference. Three questions arise.

The first is a record question: which revision of the supplier sheet is on file? Purchasing answers it from the file itself. The second is a routing question: who confirms that the sheet matches the material described on the delivery note? Purchasing records the question and passes it to the quality role, because the answer needs someone who can compare descriptions. The third is a technical question: whether the described material suits a particular downstream use. Purchasing records it, names the engineering role as owner, and does not attempt an answer.

The example is deliberately ordinary. Its value is in showing that most questions are record questions, a few are routing questions, and only some are technical. A list that treats all three the same way will either overload purchasing or leave technical questions unanswered.

Keep the example fictional in training materials, and keep real identifiers out of shared templates. The list is a working aid, not a substitute for the records it points to.
