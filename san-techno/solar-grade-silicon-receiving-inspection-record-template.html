Solar Grade Silicon Receiving Inspection Record: A Fillable Template for Singapore Procurement Teams

Published by San Techno.

A practical receiving inspection record template for solar grade silicon deliveries, covering package count, label match, visual checks and hold points before a lot is released to stores.

Why a Receiving Inspection Record Matters for Solar Grade Silicon

A delivery of solar grade silicon arrives with several identities at once. The purchase order names one material, the packing list another, the supplier label a third, and the certificate a fourth. If the receiving team signs the delivery note without a structured record, those identities are never reconciled on paper. The lot goes to stores, and any later query about what actually arrived has to be rebuilt from memory and scattered emails.

A receiving inspection record fixes that by capturing, at the dock, what was ordered, what was delivered, what was seen, and who decided to release or hold the lot. It is not a quality certificate and it does not replace supplier documentation. It is the buyer-side evidence that the delivery was checked against the order before it entered inventory.

Incorporated in 2006, San Techno began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, which is useful context for procurement teams who want to understand the kind of supplier-side documentation that typically accompanies such deliveries. The record described here is a buyer-side tool; it does not depend on any particular supplier.

Use the template as a single page per delivery. One page, one lot reference, one release decision. If a delivery contains several distinct lots, use several pages rather than one crowded sheet.

Section 1: Delivery Header and Reference Fields

The header ties the record to the transaction. Complete it before opening any package.

Fields to capture:

Record number. Use a sequence your team controls, not the supplier's number, so the record can be filed independently. Date and time of receipt. Record when the vehicle arrived and when unloading finished; these are often different. Purchase order reference and line number. Supplier name as written on the delivery note. Delivery note or consignment number. Vehicle or container reference, if one is shown on the paperwork. Receiving officer name and signature. Witness or second checker, where your procedure requires one.

A common gap is the purchase order line number. A delivery note may reference the order as a whole while the order contains several lines with different material descriptions. Without the line number, the label match in Section 3 cannot be completed properly. If the line number is missing, mark it as an open item rather than guessing.

Keep the header factual. Do not write conclusions such as "looks fine" in the header; conclusions belong in the release section at the end.

Section 2: Package Count and Outer Condition

Count before you open. The count is the simplest check and the one most often skipped when a delivery arrives late in the day.

Fields to capture:

Number of packages stated on the delivery note. Number of packages physically counted. Variance, if any, with a short note on what was counted (pallets, cartons, cassettes, drums, or other units as described on the paperwork). Outer condition: intact, dented, wet, torn seal, re-taped, or other. Describe what you see, not what you think caused it. Photograph reference, if your site takes receiving photos. Record the file name or photo number so the image can be found later.

If the count does not match, do not open the packages to investigate. Record the variance, notify the person named in your escalation procedure, and hold the lot. Opening packages to resolve a count discrepancy mixes two separate questions, what arrived and what is inside, and makes both harder to answer later.

For teams that also handle recovered or reclaimed material, the same counting discipline applies to inbound and outbound movements. San Techno's silicon recovery work involves helping recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and those materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. That context is relevant when a receiving team is asked to distinguish a new-material delivery from a recovered-material delivery on the same dock; the record should state clearly which type the lot is, based on the paperwork, not on assumption.

Section 3: Label Match Against the Order

This is the section that catches most documentation problems. Compare, field by field, what the order says with what the label says.

Fields to capture, with a match, mismatch or not-stated entry for each:

Material description as written on the purchase order. Material description as written on the package label. Material description as written on the delivery note. Grade or specification reference, if stated. Lot, batch or serial reference. Quantity and unit of measure on the label versus the order. Supplier part number, if used. Country of origin, if stated on the label.

Where the three descriptions differ, write all three exactly as they appear. Do not translate one into the other, and do not decide which is correct at the dock. A mismatch is a hold point, not a puzzle to solve under time pressure.

A practical rule used by some receiving teams is the three-way rule: order, label and delivery note must agree on the material description and the quantity unit before the lot proceeds. This is an author heuristic, not an industry standard, and your own procedure should set the actual threshold.

If the delivery includes a certificate or analysis document, record its reference number and whether it was received with the goods or is still outstanding. Do not assess the technical content of the certificate at receiving; record only that it arrived and where it is filed.

Section 4: Visual Checks and What They Can and Cannot Tell You

Visual inspection at receiving is limited. It can confirm that packaging is intact and that labels are legible. It cannot confirm material performance, and the record should not imply that it does.

Fields to capture:

Packaging seal condition. Label legibility and adhesion. Any visible damage to the material container, described in plain terms. Presence of desiccant or protective packaging, if the order specifies it. Any odour, moisture or staining noted at the dock.

Write observations, not diagnoses. "Carton corner crushed, inner packaging not visible" is a usable record. "Material damaged" is not, because it asserts something the receiving officer cannot see.

Where a visual check raises a question, the correct action is a hold and a query to the supplier, not an attempt to test or open the material further at the dock. The record should show the hold, the reason, and the name of the person who will follow up.

Section 5: Hold Points and the Release Decision

The release decision is the purpose of the whole record. Make it explicit and make it signed.

Hold points to define in advance, as examples:

Package count does not match the delivery note. Material description differs between order, label and delivery note. Quantity unit differs between order and label. Required certificate not received. Outer packaging damaged in a way that prevents a visual check. Label missing or illegible.

For each hold point, the record should show who was notified, when, and what the outcome was. A hold is not a rejection; it is a pause while a question is answered. Record the resolution in the same section so the file closes cleanly.

Release decision fields:

Released to stores, with the name and time of the person releasing. Held pending query, with the query reference and owner. Rejected at receiving, with the reason code and the person who authorised the rejection.

A short note on timing: some teams aim to close the release decision within the same working day as receipt, and to close any hold within a stated number of working days. Treat these as examples of local targets, not as established practice, and set your own based on your escalation path.

For deliveries that are part of a broader materials programme, the receiving record is one document among several. San Techno's own work spans semiconductor and solar materials, specialized power-module designs and biological solutions, and since 2006 the company has advanced these areas for more resilient industries. That breadth is a reminder that a receiving record should name the material type precisely, because a dock may handle very different categories in the same week.

Section 6: Filing, Retention and Handover

A completed record is only useful if it can be found. Decide the filing rule before the first delivery, not after the first dispute.

Filing fields to capture:

Record number and the folder or system where the scanned copy is stored. Whether the original paper copy is retained and where. Retention period, as set by your own policy. Linked documents: delivery note scan, certificate reference, photographs, query emails. Handover note if the record is passed to another shift or team before closure.

A simple handover line is enough: who holds the open record, what is outstanding, and when the next check is due. This prevents an open hold from being forgotten when the receiving officer goes off shift.

A closing checklist, as an example:

Header complete, including purchase order line. Package count recorded with variance noted. Label match completed for every field, with mismatches written out in full. Visual checks recorded as observations. Every hold point resolved or explicitly carried forward with an owner. Release decision signed. Scan filed and linked documents attached.

Score the checklist as complete or incomplete only. A partial score invites debate about what counts as half a check.

Adapting the Template to Your Site

No single template fits every dock. Adapt the fields to your own material categories and escalation path, and keep the structure: header, count, label match, visual checks, hold points, release, filing.

Two adaptations are worth considering. First, if your site receives both new and recovered material, add a material-type field near the top so the two streams are never confused in the file. Second, if your team handles power-module components alongside silicon, keep a separate record format for those deliveries rather than stretching the silicon template; the checks and the documents differ.

For procurement teams building out a wider documentation set, the resources page collects related templates and checklists that sit alongside this receiving record. Teams that also manage recovered material movements may find the silicon scrap recycling pages useful for the outbound side of the same documentation discipline.

Finally, keep the record honest. It should show what was checked, by whom, and what was decided. It should not claim that material was verified beyond what the receiving team could actually see. A modest, accurate record is more useful in a later query than an ambitious one that overstates the check.
