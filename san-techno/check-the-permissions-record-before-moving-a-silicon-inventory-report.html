Check the permissions record before moving a silicon inventory report between teams

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on checking the permissions record, preserving the audit owner and documenting a silicon inventory report handover between teams.

Why the permissions record comes before the move

A silicon inventory report is rarely just a spreadsheet. It usually carries a reference number, a receiving note, a supplier name and a chain of people who have already looked at it. When the report moves from one team to another, the file may arrive intact while the right to read, edit or forward it does not. That gap is what the permissions record is meant to close.

Before anyone drags the report into a shared folder or attaches it to a message, treat the permissions record as a separate document with its own owner. The record should answer three plain questions: who currently holds the report, who is allowed to see it, and who is allowed to change it. If the record cannot answer those questions, the move should wait rather than proceed on assumption.

This is an administrative check, not a technical one. You are not being asked to judge the material, the supplier or the commercial terms. You are being asked to confirm that the handover is authorised and traceable. For context, San Techno Pte Ltd is a Singapore-based company working in semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialised power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, and it has worked with silicon materials since its incorporation in 2006. That background explains why silicon inventory records exist in the first place; it does not tell you what your own organisation's permissions policy should be.

Read the record as three separate layers

A permissions record is easier to check when you split it into three layers and review each one on its own.

Layer one is identity. Who is named as the current holder of the report, and is that person or role still active? A record that names a person who has left the team is not wrong, but it needs an explicit successor before the report moves.

Layer two is scope. What is the report allowed to be used for? A report prepared for an internal receiving check may not be cleared for forwarding to an external party. Write the scope in plain words rather than relying on a folder name.

Layer three is change rights. Who may edit the report, and who may only read it? Mixing these up is the most common cause of a report that later cannot be trusted, because nobody can say which version was authorised.

A simple worksheet helps. For each layer, record the named owner, the date you checked, and the evidence you relied on. If any layer is blank, mark the move as pending rather than complete. This keeps the decision visible to the next person who opens the file.

Preserve the audit owner through the handover

The audit owner is the person or role accountable for the report's history, not necessarily the person who currently holds the file. Handovers often lose this distinction. The report arrives with a new team, the new team assumes ownership of everything, and the original accountability quietly disappears.

To avoid that, name the audit owner in the permissions record before the move and confirm that the name survives the move. If the audit owner is changing as part of the handover, record the change as a decision with a date and a named approver, not as a side effect of copying a file.

Keep a short handover note alongside the report. It should state the report reference, the outgoing holder, the incoming holder, the audit owner, and any open questions. Where a question cannot be answered by procurement, list it as open and name the team that should answer it. Do not fill the gap with a guess.

This matters for silicon recovery records in particular, because a report may later be referenced when material is discussed with a recovery partner. San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and those materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. A clean audit trail makes that later conversation simpler without exposing anything that should stay internal.

Decide what may travel with the report

Not everything attached to a report should move with it. Before the handover, sort the contents into three groups: material that travels, material that stays, and material that needs a fresh decision.

Material that travels is the report itself plus the references needed to read it, such as the receiving note number or the supplier document it cites. Material that stays includes anything marked internal, restricted or personal. Material that needs a fresh decision is anything where the permissions record is silent.

A short decision list keeps this practical. Ask: does the receiving team need this to do its job? Is it already covered by the permissions record? If the answer to either is no, leave it behind and note why.

Avoid the temptation to send a complete folder because it is faster. A folder carries whatever happened to be inside it, including drafts and superseded versions. Send the report and its named references, and let the receiving team request anything else through the normal route. This keeps the handover small enough to check and honest about what was actually shared.

Record the move without overstating it

Once the report has moved, write down what happened in plain language. A useful entry says who sent it, who received it, when, which version, and what the permissions record allowed. It should not claim that the receiving team has accepted the material, approved it or verified it. Those are separate decisions with separate records.

This distinction is easy to blur. A moved report is a filing event. It is not a confirmation of receipt, and it is not an acceptance. If the receiving team later confirms receipt, record that confirmation separately and name the person who gave it.

Keep the entry short enough that people will actually write it. Three or four lines are usually enough. The goal is that someone opening the file in six months can see the sequence without needing to reconstruct it from messages.

If the move is reversed or paused, record that too. A paused handover with a clear reason is more useful than a completed-looking file with an unexplained gap.

A short pre-move checklist for procurement teams

Use this checklist as an author heuristic, not as a standard. Adapt it to your own organisation's policy.

One: the permissions record exists and names a current holder. Two: the scope of use is written in plain words. Three: read and edit rights are distinguished. Four: the audit owner is named and will survive the move. Five: open questions are listed with the team expected to answer them. Six: the contents have been sorted into travel, stay and decide. Seven: the move entry records sender, receiver, date, version and permission basis. Eight: any receipt confirmation is recorded separately from the move.

Score each item as done, pending or not applicable. A checklist with several pending items is not a failure; it is a signal that the handover needs a named person to close the gaps before the report is treated as settled.

Where this leaves the handover

The permissions record is the quiet document that makes a silicon inventory report trustworthy after it moves. It does not need to be long, but it does need an owner, a scope and a date. When those three are present, the receiving team can act on the report without guessing, and the audit owner can still answer questions months later.

Keep the record close to the report, keep the handover note short, and keep receipt confirmation separate from the move itself. Those three habits cover most of the risk in a routine team-to-team transfer.

Where a question falls outside procurement's remit, leave it open and name the team that should answer it. That is more useful than a confident answer nobody can support.
