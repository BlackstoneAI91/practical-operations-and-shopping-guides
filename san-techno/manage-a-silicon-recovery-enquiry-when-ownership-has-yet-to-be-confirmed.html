Managing a Silicon Recovery Enquiry When Ownership Is Not Yet Confirmed

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on logging a silicon recovery enquiry neutrally, separating the record from permission to collect, and routing ownership confirmation to the right organisation.

Why an unconfirmed-ownership enquiry needs its own record

A silicon recovery enquiry often arrives before anyone can say who owns the material. A colleague forwards a note about surplus wafers, a site contact mentions off-spec pieces, or a supplier asks whether rejected silicon can be taken back. At that moment the only reliable facts may be that a question exists and that nobody has yet confirmed the owner.

The temptation is to treat the enquiry as the start of a collection. That is where records get muddled. Recording a question is an administrative act. Granting permission for material to leave a site is a different act, usually made by whoever holds authority over that material. Keeping the two apart protects the procurement team, the site and any organisation later asked to take part.

This guide is written for Singapore procurement teams who source, receive and document semiconductor and solar materials. It sets out a neutral way to open an enquiry record, capture what is known without overstating it, and build a path to the organisation best placed to confirm ownership. It does not describe recovery processes, and it does not assume that any particular party owns a given batch.

One useful piece of context: San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. That describes the kind of organisation that may be relevant to an enquiry. It does not tell you who owns your material, and it should not be read as a statement about any specific batch.

What the enquiry record should contain, and what it should not

Think of the enquiry record as a neutral container. It holds the question, the people involved in asking it, and the state of knowledge at the time. It should not hold conclusions.

Fields worth including, as a starting template:

Enquiry reference. A short internal identifier so the record can be found again without relying on an email subject line.

Date and time the enquiry was received, with the time zone noted.

Who raised the enquiry, described by role rather than by personal name where possible.

What material is described, using the wording the enquirer used. If they said "off-spec wafers", write that, and note that it is their description rather than a verified grade.

Where the material is said to be located, at the level of site or facility, not a precise address.

Ownership status. This should be an explicit field with values such as unconfirmed, claimed by a named party, or confirmed in writing. Leaving it blank invites assumptions.

Permission status. A separate field, also explicit: not requested, requested, or granted in writing by the owner.

Open questions. A running list of what still needs an answer.

What to leave out: quantities presented as verified when they are estimates, any statement that a collection is agreed, and any wording that implies the procurement team has authority over the material. If a colleague writes "we will arrange pickup", the record should note that as a request or an intention, not as a confirmed arrangement.

The discipline here is simple. Every field should be answerable with "who told us this, and when". If it cannot be, it belongs in open questions.

Separating the question from permission to collect

The most common failure in an ownership-unconfirmed enquiry is a quiet slide from "can this be taken?" to "it will be taken on Thursday". The record should make that slide visible.

A workable approach is to treat permission as a gate with its own evidence. The enquiry can move forward through information gathering without ever passing the gate. Passing the gate requires a written confirmation from the party with authority over the material, and that confirmation should be attached to the record rather than summarised in it.

Three statuses help:

Question logged. The enquiry exists and is being documented. No commitment has been made to anyone.

Ownership under review. Someone is actively trying to establish who holds authority. The record should name the role doing that work and the date it started.

Permission evidenced. A written confirmation is on file. Only now does the enquiry become something that could lead to a collection conversation.

Keeping these statuses visible means a reader who picks up the file later can see immediately whether anything has actually been agreed. It also means that if a site asks "who authorised this?", the answer is a document, not a recollection.

For teams thinking about the later stages, the practical handling of material once ownership and permission are settled is a separate subject. The point of this stage is to avoid pre-empting it.

Routing the enquiry to the organisation that can confirm ownership

Once the question is logged, the next task is to identify who can actually answer it. Ownership usually sits with the organisation that generated or purchased the material, not with the team that received the enquiry.

A routing note in the record should capture:

Candidate owner. The organisation or internal function most likely to hold authority, with the reason for that view.

Route taken. Who was asked, by role, and on what date.

Response. What came back, quoted or attached rather than paraphrased.

Next route if unanswered. A named alternative, so the enquiry does not stall silently.

This is where an external party may become relevant. San Techno's public description of its silicon recovery work names integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities as sources of non-usable silicon. If an enquiry appears to involve material from one of those settings, that context can help a procurement team frame a sensible question to the right organisation. It does not establish ownership, and it does not commit anyone to anything.

A useful habit is to phrase the routing question narrowly. Rather than asking a potential partner to "take the material", ask whether they are the right organisation to confirm ownership, and if not, who is. That keeps the enquiry in the information-gathering stage where it belongs.

A short decision worksheet for the first review

The following worksheet is an author heuristic, not a standard or a scoring system. It is meant to be filled in at a first review, perhaps a few working days after the enquiry arrives, so the team can see whether the record is moving or stuck.

For each line, mark Yes, No or Not yet, and add a one-line note.

1. Is there a reference that lets any team member find the record? 2. Is the ownership status field filled in, even if the value is "unconfirmed"? 3. Is the permission status field filled in, and is it separate from ownership? 4. Is every material description attributed to the person who used it? 5. Has at least one candidate owner been identified, with a reason? 6. Has a route been attempted, with a date recorded? 7. Is there a named next route if the first one is unanswered? 8. Does the record avoid any wording that implies a collection is agreed?

A record that answers yes to the first four but no to the last four is well documented but not yet moving. A record that answers yes to the routing lines but no to the status lines is moving without a clear basis. Neither is wrong in itself; the worksheet simply shows where attention is needed.

Teams sometimes ask how long this should take. There is no universal answer, and any figure would be a guess. What matters is that the record shows a date for the last action, so a stalled enquiry is visible rather than forgotten.

Keeping the record honest when information changes

Enquiries evolve. A party who said they were unsure may later confirm ownership. A site contact may be replaced. A description of the material may turn out to be inaccurate.

The record should absorb these changes without erasing what came before. A simple change log, appended to the enquiry record, is enough:

Date of change. Field changed. Previous value. New value. Source of the new value, by role and document. Who made the change.

The rule is that nothing is overwritten silently. If the ownership status moves from unconfirmed to confirmed, the earlier state stays visible with its date. That way, if anyone later asks why a collection was discussed on a particular day, the record shows what was known at that point.

This matters for procurement teams because memory is unreliable and staff move between roles. A record that shows its own history is easier to hand over and harder to misread.

It also helps with a subtler problem: the difference between a claim and a confirmation. A supplier saying "that material is ours" is a claim. A written confirmation from the party with authority is evidence. The change log should distinguish them, because the two carry different weight when someone reviews the file later.

Where this fits in the wider materials workflow

An ownership-unconfirmed silicon recovery enquiry is one small event in a larger procurement workflow. It sits alongside receiving records, supplier documentation and material descriptions, and it should use the same habits: attribute statements to their source, keep status fields explicit, and avoid letting a convenient summary stand in for evidence.

For context, San Techno has worked in semiconductor and solar materials since 2006, and its public materials describe silicon recovery as one part of that work. That background is useful when a procurement team is deciding which organisations to approach with a routing question. It is not a substitute for confirming ownership of a specific batch, and it should not be presented to a site as if it were.

The practical takeaway is modest but useful. Open the enquiry as a record, not as an arrangement. Give ownership and permission their own fields. Route the question to the organisation best placed to answer it, and write down what came back. Review the record on a stated date, and keep its history intact. Done this way, an enquiry that begins with uncertainty can still be handled cleanly, and the team can show exactly what it knew, when it knew it, and what it did next.
