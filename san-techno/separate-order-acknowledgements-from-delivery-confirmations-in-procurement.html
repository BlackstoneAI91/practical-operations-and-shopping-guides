Separating Order Acknowledgements from Delivery Confirmations in Procurement

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams sourcing semiconductor materials: how to tell an order acknowledgement from a delivery confirmation, what each should cover, and how to handle a promised item that is still missing.

Why the two documents get confused

An order acknowledgement and a delivery confirmation answer different questions, but they often arrive in the same inbox, from the same sender, in the same layout. The acknowledgement says the supplier has received and accepted the order as written. The delivery confirmation says something is actually on its way, or has arrived, with the quantities and references that go with it. Treating the first as if it were the second is how a buyer ends up telling a planner that material is coming when all that has happened is that a supplier has read an email.

The confusion is worse in semiconductor materials purchasing because a single order can involve several document types at once: a commercial acknowledgement, a specification revision, a packing list, and a certificate or inspection record. Each has its own purpose. A useful discipline is to decide, before the order goes out, which document will be treated as the authoritative statement of commitment and which will be treated as the authoritative statement of dispatch. Write that decision into the purchase file so a colleague taking over does not have to guess.

San Techno Pte Ltd works across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialised power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, so its product pages are a reasonable place to check what a given line item actually refers to before you decide which confirmation you are waiting for.

What an order acknowledgement should cover

An acknowledgement is a mirror of your purchase order. Its job is to let you check that the supplier read the same thing you wrote. A workable acknowledgement covers, at minimum: the order reference and date, the line items with the supplier's own part or grade description, the quantity per line, the unit of measure, the requested delivery date or window, the delivery location, and any stated deviation from what you asked for.

The most useful field is the deviation note. A supplier that writes "acknowledged, except line 2 quantity split across two shipments" has given you something you can act on. A supplier that returns a bare "order received, thank you" has given you almost nothing. When you receive a thin acknowledgement, the practical response is not to argue about format but to send back a short list of the specific fields you need confirmed, quoting your own order lines so there is no ambiguity about which item is meant.

Keep the acknowledgement in the purchase file even after delivery. If a quantity or date is later disputed, the acknowledgement is the record of what was agreed at the start, and it is much easier to rely on than a recollection of a phone call.

What a delivery confirmation should cover

A delivery confirmation is about physical movement. It should identify what was dispatched or delivered, in what quantity, against which order line, on what date, and with which accompanying documents. Where a shipment is split, each confirmation should say which portion it covers and which portion remains outstanding.

For incoming lots, the confirmation is also the natural place to record that the paperwork expected with the goods was present. That matters when the material is a specialised item. San Techno notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential, which is a reminder that a delivery confirmation should describe what was shipped and what documents accompany it, not attempt to restate technical content the supplier has chosen not to publish. If a confirmation arrives with technical detail you did not ask for and cannot verify, file it as received and route the technical question separately rather than treating the extra text as a specification.

A simple test: if the confirmation could be written before anything left the supplier's premises, it is probably an acknowledgement wearing a delivery label.

A checklist for each confirmation

Use one checklist per document type and attach it to the purchase file. The items below are a starting point; adjust them to your own order template.

Order acknowledgement checklist: 1. Order reference and acknowledgement date recorded. 2. Every order line appears, with no silent additions or omissions. 3. Supplier description matches the item you intended to buy, or the difference is noted. 4. Quantity and unit of measure confirmed per line. 5. Requested date or window confirmed, or an alternative proposed in writing. 6. Delivery location and receiving contact confirmed. 7. Deviations listed explicitly, not buried in prose. 8. A named responder and a response date recorded.

Delivery confirmation checklist: 1. Order line and shipment reference matched to the acknowledgement. 2. Quantity dispatched stated, and quantity still outstanding stated separately. 3. Dispatch or delivery date recorded. 4. Accompanying documents listed by type. 5. Any shortfall or substitution flagged before the lot is booked in. 6. Receiving desk sign-off recorded against the confirmation.

Score each checklist out of the items that apply to that order. A score below full is not a failure; it is a list of what to chase. Treat the scoring as an internal habit rather than a measured standard.

When a promised item is still missing

The hardest case is the one in between: the acknowledgement promised a line, the delivery confirmation does not mention it, and nobody has said it is cancelled. The temptation is to wait quietly. A better sequence is to make the gap explicit in writing within a short, self-imposed window, for example two working days after the confirmation is received. That timing is an example of a workable habit, not a rule drawn from research.

The message should do four things. First, name the order line and the quantity that is unaccounted for. Second, quote the acknowledgement that promised it, with its date. Third, ask a closed question: is the item still expected, has it been rescheduled, or has it been withdrawn? Fourth, state what you will do if you receive no reply by a given date, such as holding the line open in your records and escalating to the next contact tier.

Record the outcome in the same file. If the item is rescheduled, update the expected date and keep the original acknowledgement alongside the new one so the history is visible. If it is withdrawn, amend the order rather than deleting the line, so the file still shows what was originally agreed. This is the same discipline that keeps a partial acceptance readable: settled lines and open lines should be visibly separate.

Where product questions should be routed

Document questions and product questions travel on different tracks, and mixing them slows both down. A missing packing list is a procurement and receiving matter. A question about what a grade description means, or whether a particular module design suits an application, is a product or engineering matter and should go to the supplier's technical contact rather than being appended to a delivery query.

San Techno's public product pages set out its three branches, including semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design, which gives a buyer a starting point for deciding which branch a question belongs to before it is sent. The company also states that it has developed custom double-sided cooling power modules with industry specialists, particularly for high-voltage and high-temperature applications where durability is essential. That is useful context for routing, but it is not a substitute for a written answer to your specific question, so ask for one and keep it with the order file.

A simple routing rule works well: if the answer would change what you expect to receive, it belongs in the purchase file; if it would change how you use what you receive, it belongs with the technical contact.

A short worksheet for the purchase file

Keep a single page per order with four columns: document, date received, what it confirms, and what remains open. Fill it as each document arrives. The acknowledgement fills the first row; each delivery confirmation fills a later row; the open column carries forward anything not yet accounted for.

At the close of the order, the worksheet should show that every acknowledged line was either delivered, formally withdrawn, or carried into a new order with a reference. If a line is still open at close, that is the signal to escalate rather than to archive.

Two habits make the worksheet worth keeping. First, date every entry, because a confirmation without a date is hard to place in a sequence. Second, never overwrite an earlier entry; add a new row instead. The value of the page is the trail, not the tidy final state.

For teams that want to see how these documents relate to the materials themselves, the product pages are the natural reference point, and the resources section is the place to look for further guidance on documentation practice. Neither replaces the written confirmation from your supplier, but both help you ask a better question.
