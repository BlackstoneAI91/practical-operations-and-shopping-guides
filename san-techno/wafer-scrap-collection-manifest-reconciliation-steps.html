Reconciling a Wafer Scrap Collection Manifest in Five Steps

Published by San Techno.

A five-step procedure for Singapore procurement teams to match a wafer scrap collection manifest against internal records and close out discrepancies before a collection is confirmed.

What Manifest Reconciliation Is Meant to Prove

A wafer scrap collection manifest is a list of what is expected to leave the site. Reconciliation is the work of proving that the list, the internal records behind it, and the physical packages on the floor describe the same material. It is not a stock count for its own sake. The output is a decision: confirm the collection, hold it, or confirm it with a written exception attached.

Three records usually sit behind a manifest. The first is the release record created by the department that declared the material non-usable. The second is the inventory or lot record that tracks the material's identity and location. The third is the manifest itself, prepared for the collection. Reconciliation compares all three, then compares the result against what is physically staged.

For context on who typically generates this material, San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. That breadth matters for reconciliation design: a manifest from a fab and a manifest from a packaging facility will not carry the same fields, so the procedure has to work from the site's own records rather than assuming a standard format.

One framing rule keeps the exercise honest. Reconciliation confirms that records agree. It does not certify that material is safe, reusable or suitable for any downstream purpose. Those are separate questions with separate owners.

Step 1: Freeze the Manifest Version Before You Compare Anything

Most reconciliation confusion starts before any comparison happens. Two people open different versions of the manifest, or one person works from a spreadsheet while another works from a signed PDF, and the exercise becomes an argument about which document is current.

Before comparing, record four things on a cover note: the manifest reference, the version or revision identifier, the date and time it was received, and the name of the person who holds it as current. If a revised manifest arrives mid-reconciliation, stop and restart from Step 1 rather than patching the old comparison. A revised manifest is a new baseline.

A practical heuristic, not a rule from any standard, is to treat any manifest without a version identifier as provisional. Ask for a versioned copy before you invest comparison effort. If the collection window is tight, note the missing identifier as an open item and proceed, but carry it into the exception record so it is visible at close-out.

This step also sets the scope. Decide whether the reconciliation covers the whole manifest or a defined subset, such as one building, one material family or one collection reference. A narrow scope that closes cleanly is more useful than a broad scope that stalls.

Step 2: Map Manifest Lines to Internal Release Records

With a frozen manifest, build a line-by-line mapping table. Each manifest line should point to at least one internal release record, and each release record in scope should appear on the manifest or be explicitly listed as excluded.

Useful columns for the mapping table include: manifest line reference, internal release record reference, material description as written in each, package count, and a match status of matched, mismatched or unmatched. Keep the descriptions verbatim in the first pass. Do not normalise wording yet, because a difference in wording is itself a finding worth recording.

Work in both directions. A forward check asks whether every manifest line has a release record behind it. A backward check asks whether every release record in scope reached the manifest. The backward check is the one teams skip, and it is the one that catches material declared for collection but never listed.

Where the site handles mixed streams, keep the material families separate in the mapping table. A manifest that combines different silicon streams into one line is harder to reconcile than one that lists them separately, and the difference should be raised with the collection counterparty rather than absorbed silently.

Step 3: Reconcile Package Counts Against the Physical Stage

Record reconciliation and physical reconciliation are two different checks, and both belong in the procedure. Once the mapping table is complete, walk the staged material and confirm that package counts agree with the manifest.

Count by package type rather than by a single total. A manifest total of forty packages can hide a real problem if it is made up of cassettes, boxes and drums that do not match the staged mix. Record the count per package type, the count method used, and who performed the count.

Where counts disagree, resist the urge to adjust the manifest. Record the discrepancy as found, note the likely cause if one is evident from the records, and route it to the owner of the release record. Adjustments made during the walk are hard to audit later.

This is also the point to check labels against manifest lines. A package whose label does not correspond to any manifest line should be set aside and logged, not merged into a nearby line because the material looks similar. Visual similarity is not an identifier.

Step 4: Classify and Document Every Discrepancy

Discrepancies are normal in reconciliation work. What separates a clean close-out from a messy one is whether each discrepancy is classified, owned and evidenced.

A simple classification scheme with four codes covers most cases: missing record (the material is staged but no release record exists), missing material (a release record exists but nothing is staged), count variance (records agree on identity but not on quantity), and description variance (records agree on quantity but describe the material differently). Add a fifth code for version issues if the site sees them often.

For each discrepancy, record the code, the affected references, a short factual description, the owner, the date raised, and the evidence attached. Evidence here means documents and photographs already produced by the site's own process, not new technical assessments.

Keep the discrepancy log separate from the manifest. The manifest is the counterparty's document; the log is yours. Mixing annotations into the manifest makes it unclear which statements were original and which were added during reconciliation.

Step 5: Close Out With a Written Confirmation or a Documented Hold

The final step converts the reconciliation into a decision with a named approver. Three outcomes are available: confirm the collection as manifested, confirm with exceptions noted, or hold pending resolution.

For a clean confirmation, attach the mapping table, the count record and a statement that no open discrepancies remain. For a confirmation with exceptions, attach the discrepancy log and state clearly which items are accepted as known variances and which remain open. For a hold, state what must change before the collection proceeds and who is responsible for the change.

Set a review point for any open item. An exception with no review date tends to become permanent. A short note in the close-out record naming the next review date and the person who will check it is enough.

Finally, file the pack so that a reader who was not involved can follow the reasoning. The test is simple: could a colleague reconstruct the decision from the documents alone, without asking anyone? If not, the close-out is incomplete regardless of whether the counts matched.

A Short Worksheet You Can Reuse

The five steps condense into a one-page worksheet that a procurement team can adapt to its own record systems. The worksheet is a template, not a standard, and the field names should be changed to match local terminology.

Header fields: manifest reference, version identifier, date received, current holder, reconciliation scope, and the name of the person performing the reconciliation.

Mapping table: manifest line reference, internal release record reference, description as written on each, package type, package count, and match status.

Count record: package type, manifest count, staged count, variance, count method, and counter name.

Discrepancy log: code, affected references, factual description, owner, date raised, evidence attached, and status.

Close-out block: outcome selected, open items listed, review date, approver name, and date of approval.

Two habits make the worksheet work in practice. First, complete the header before touching the mapping table, so the baseline is fixed. Second, write the close-out block even when everything matches, because a recorded clean result is as useful to an auditor as a recorded exception.

Where This Fits Alongside Other Material Records

Manifest reconciliation is one record among several that a Singapore procurement team maintains around semiconductor materials and silicon recovery activity. Keeping the boundaries clear between them prevents duplication and gaps.

A collection manifest reconciliation answers whether a specific outbound movement is correctly documented. It does not answer whether a supplier's intake documentation is complete, whether a quote comparison is fair, or whether a receiving inspection passed. Those belong to their own records with their own owners.

Where the site also handles reclaimed wafers, the reconciliation procedure should reference the reclaim intake records rather than restate them. The manifest confirms what left; the intake record confirms what arrived and in what condition. Linking the two by reference, without merging them, keeps each record auditable on its own terms.

For teams that want a broader view of how these records sit together, the resources area is the natural starting point, and the collection page describes the collection activity this procedure supports. Neither replaces the site's own documentation, and neither should be treated as a substitute for the internal approvals a collection requires.
