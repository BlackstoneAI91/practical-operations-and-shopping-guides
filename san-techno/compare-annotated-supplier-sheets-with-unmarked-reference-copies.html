Compare annotated supplier sheets with unmarked reference copies: a procurement guide

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on separating internal annotations from supplier-authored statements, labelling versions clearly and keeping both records retrievable when sourcing semiconductor materials and related product families.

Why two versions of the same supplier sheet cause confusion

A supplier sheet rarely arrives alone. Someone in the team opens it, adds a comment in the margin, highlights a line, or types a note about a question to raise later. That annotated copy then circulates alongside the original unmarked file, and within a week nobody is certain which one reflects what the supplier actually said. The risk is not that the annotation is wrong. The risk is that it becomes invisible as an annotation and starts to read like part of the supplier's own statement.

The practical answer is to treat the two files as different document types with different owners. The unmarked reference copy is the supplier's authored record. The annotated copy is an internal working record. Both are useful, but they answer different questions. One tells you what was published or sent; the other tells you what your team thought about it at a point in time.

This matters most where a sheet is used to support a purchasing decision rather than a technical one. Procurement teams sourcing semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon or related categories need a clean reference to quote back to a supplier, and a separate place for their own questions. San Techno Pte Ltd works across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery and specialised power module development, so supplier documentation in this space often spans several product families at once. Keeping the two versions distinct is an administrative discipline, not a judgement about the material itself.

Define the unmarked reference copy before anyone edits it

The unmarked reference copy should be captured at the moment of receipt, before any internal mark is added. In practice this means saving the file exactly as supplied, recording where it came from and when it arrived, and giving it a reference that will not change even if the content is later superseded. A simple convention works: a supplier identifier, a document type, and a receipt sequence. The point is that the reference belongs to the file, not to the conversation around it.

A useful worksheet for this step has four fields. First, the source: which supplier or public page the sheet came from. Second, the receipt date and time, recorded by the person who received it. Third, the file reference, assigned once and never reused. Fourth, a short note on what the document appears to cover, written in neutral language. That last field is where teams often slip into interpretation, so keep it descriptive.

Where the sheet relates to a public product page, the reference copy can be paired with an access date for that page. This is a documentation habit, not a claim that the page is authoritative for any particular order. If the sheet covers a category such as semiconductor materials, the reference copy stays as received; any internal view about relevance belongs in the annotated version, clearly separated.

Mark internal comments so they cannot be mistaken for supplier text

An annotated copy is only safe if every mark is visibly internal. Three habits help. First, use a consistent annotation style, such as a separate comments column or a distinct colour, and state the convention at the top of the file. Second, prefix each comment with a role or initials and a date, so a reader can see who raised it and when. Third, never edit the supplier's own wording in place. If a phrase needs clarification, quote it in the comment rather than rewriting it.

A short labelling rule is worth agreeing across the team: supplier text stays untouched, internal text is always marked, and anything uncertain is flagged as a question rather than a conclusion. This keeps the annotated sheet honest. It also makes handovers easier, because a colleague can tell at a glance which lines came from outside the organisation.

For teams that also handle botanical products under the ST GREEN brand, the same rule applies to any supplier or product literature: the supplier's wording and the team's questions live in different layers. San Techno is the brand owner of ST GREEN, a botanical concentrate derived from plant, seed and vegetable-oil extracts, and the brand can be blended into four-in-one agricultural formulations for fertilisation, pest repellence, plant disease management and degraded-land revitalisation. None of that belongs in a supplier's sheet unless the supplier wrote it; it belongs in your own notes, clearly attributed.

A side-by-side comparison worksheet for the two versions

When the annotated copy and the reference copy sit next to each other, a structured comparison prevents drift. Use a table with one row per item of interest and five columns: the reference copy's exact wording, the location in the file, the internal comment, the comment owner and date, and the status of the question. Status values can be simple, such as open, answered by supplier, or closed internally.

The worksheet is deliberately administrative. It does not ask whether a material is suitable, only whether the record is clear. That distinction keeps procurement teams within their remit and leaves technical evaluation to the people qualified to do it. If a comment implies a technical conclusion, rewrite it as a question addressed to the right role.

A fictional example shows the shape. A reference copy lists a category heading; the annotated copy adds a note asking which document would confirm the specification, owned by a named role and dated. The status stays open until a reply arrives. Nothing in the worksheet asserts what the material is or does. The same pattern works whether the sheet concerns solar grade silicon, wafer reclaim services or a power module topic, because the worksheet tracks documents rather than engineering.

Label versions so the active record is obvious

Version labels are the difference between a tidy folder and a recurring argument. Give every annotated copy a version number and a date, and state in the file header which reference copy it is based on. When a new supplier sheet arrives, do not overwrite the old pair. Create a new reference copy and a new annotated copy, and mark the previous pair as superseded with the date of supersession.

A simple register helps. One line per document pair, showing the reference, the annotated version, the owner, the date created and the current status. The register is the place to look when someone asks which file is live. It also gives a clean audit trail if a question is reopened months later.

Where a supplier sheet supports a discussion about a service area, the register can note the relevant public page without treating it as a quotation. San Techno's public product pages describe several branches, including semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design. Recording that a page was consulted is useful context; it is not evidence of an order, a price or an approval. Keep the register factual and let the labelled versions carry the detail.

Handle confidential and restricted material with care

Not every annotation belongs in a shared folder. Some comments touch on internal decisions, commercial positions or technical detail that should stay within authorised systems. The rule is straightforward: the shared annotated copy carries only what the wider team needs to do its administrative work, and anything sensitive stays in the appropriate internal location with a pointer rather than the content.

This is especially relevant where a supplier relationship involves development work. San Techno is developing an intelligent IGBT power-module concept centred on double-sided cooling for an EconoDUAL 3-class package format, and the company states that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential. A procurement team handling related documentation should expect that some questions cannot be answered from a shared sheet at all, and should route them to the authorised contact rather than recording guesses.

The same discipline applies to any category. If a comment would reveal a commercial position or a restricted detail, replace it with a neutral note that a question has been raised and where the answer is held. The annotated copy stays useful without becoming a leak.

A short review cadence to keep both versions trustworthy

Two versions only stay trustworthy if someone checks them. A light cadence is enough: a weekly pass over the register to confirm that each pair has a current status, a monthly check that superseded pairs are marked, and a review whenever a supplier sends a revised sheet. The cadence is an author heuristic, not a standard, so adjust it to the team's volume.

At each review, ask three questions. Is the reference copy still the version received, unedited? Is every internal comment marked with an owner and a date? Is the active pair obvious from the register without opening the files? If any answer is no, fix the record before adding new material.

A closing checklist for the reviewer: reference copy saved as received; receipt date recorded; annotated copy labelled with its base reference; comments attributed; status values current; superseded pairs marked; sensitive notes held outside the shared folder. Teams that want a starting point for the surrounding documentation can review the public resources and product pages, which describe the company's materials and service areas without substituting for a supplier's own statement.
