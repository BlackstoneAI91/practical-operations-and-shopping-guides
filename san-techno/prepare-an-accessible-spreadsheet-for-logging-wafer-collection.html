Prepare an Accessible Spreadsheet for Logging Wafer Collection Acknowledgements

Published by San Techno.

A practical interface-design guide for Singapore procurement teams building an accessible spreadsheet to log wafer collection acknowledgements, covering column headers, non-colour-only statuses, readable time stamps and a review worksheet.

Why an acknowledgement log needs accessible design from the start

A wafer collection acknowledgement is a small administrative record with a long life. It tells a receiving desk that a collection was acknowledged, by which reference, on what date, and whether anything is still open. When that record lives in a spreadsheet that only its author can read comfortably, the log stops being a shared tool and becomes a private one.

Accessibility here is not a compliance exercise. It is a usability decision that makes the log work for a colleague using a screen reader, a buyer reading it on a phone between meetings, a new team member who has never seen the file, and a reviewer checking a single row months later. The same design choices that help those readers also reduce transcription errors.

This guide is an interface design resource. It uses hypothetical records and invented references. It does not describe any real collection, supplier or material movement, and it does not give instructions for handling, processing or disposing of wafers. For context, San Techno Pte Ltd is a Singapore-based company working in semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialised power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand. Its public materials note that it helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and that such materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. Those notes explain why collection paperwork matters to buyers in this field; they are not a claim about how any particular log should be built.

Start with the reader, not the columns

Before adding a single header, write down who will open the file and what each person needs to do with it. A useful starting list for a procurement team is: the person who records an acknowledgement, the person who checks whether an acknowledgement is still missing, the person who prepares a summary for a meeting, and the person who reviews the file after a handover.

Each of those readers needs a different view of the same row. The recorder needs fast entry. The checker needs to sort by status. The summariser needs counts. The reviewer needs to understand what a field meant at the time it was filled in. Designing for all four at once is easier than retrofitting accessibility later.

A short design brief helps. In fictional form it might read: this log records acknowledgement references for wafer collections; it is read by purchasing and receiving staff; it must be usable without colour perception and without a mouse; it must survive being exported to a plain text view. Keep that brief in a separate notes tab so future editors understand the intent.

One caution: do not let the brief drift into operational detail. The log records that an acknowledgement exists and what it refers to. It should not become a place to store technical evaluations or material decisions that belong with the appropriate team.

Column headers that read well aloud

Headers do the heaviest lifting in an accessible spreadsheet. A screen reader announces the header for each cell, so a header like "Ref" is far less useful than "Acknowledgement reference". Aim for short but unambiguous phrases, and avoid abbreviations that only make sense inside one team.

A workable starter set, using invented field names, might include: acknowledgement reference, collection request reference, site label, acknowledgement date, acknowledgement time, status, status reason, recorded by role, last reviewed date, and notes. Each header should describe one idea. If a header needs a slash or the word "and" to make sense, it is probably two columns.

Keep a second row or a separate definitions tab that explains each header in one plain sentence. For example: "Status reason — the short explanation chosen from the approved list when status is not Confirmed." Definitions prevent the slow drift where two people use the same header to mean different things.

Finally, freeze the header row and give the table a named range. Both are small conveniences that make navigation predictable for keyboard users and for anyone returning to a long file.

Statuses that do not depend on colour

Colour is the most common accessibility failure in a status column. A red cell and an amber cell look identical to a reader with colour vision deficiency, and they look like nothing at all in a printed copy or a plain text export.

Use words as the primary signal and treat colour, if used at all, as decoration. A small controlled list works better than free text. For a collection acknowledgement log, a hypothetical list might be: Received, Partially received, Awaiting confirmation, Query open, and Closed. Each value should have a written definition, and the definitions should live where editors can see them.

Where a status needs nuance, add a separate status reason column rather than inventing new status words. This keeps sorting and counting reliable. It also means a reader who cannot see formatting still gets the full picture from the text alone.

If your team wants visual cues, pair them with text rather than replacing it. A symbol plus the word is acceptable; a symbol alone is not. Test the file by exporting one sheet to plain text and reading it. If the meaning survives, the design is sound.

Readable time stamps and date conventions

Time stamps cause quiet confusion. A date written as 03/04/2026 means different things in different conventions, and a time written as 9.15 could be morning or evening depending on habit. In a shared log read across teams, ambiguity is expensive.

Choose one written convention and state it in the definitions tab. A common accessible pattern is an unambiguous date format such as 2026-04-03 alongside a 24-hour time such as 14:30, stored as text or in a consistently formatted column. Whatever you choose, apply it everywhere and never mix conventions in the same column.

Separate the acknowledgement date from the date the record was last reviewed. They answer different questions, and merging them hides when a row was last touched. Keep both as distinct columns with clear headers.

Avoid relying on cell formatting alone to convey a date. A cell that displays a friendly date but stores an unexplained number is hard to audit. Where possible, keep a readable text version in its own column so the record survives export and copying.

A short review worksheet before you publish the file

Before sharing the log, run a short self-check. The scores below are author heuristics for a quick internal review, not a certified accessibility audit. Treat them as prompts, not as a pass mark.

Header clarity: can a new reader understand every header without asking? Score each header from one to three and rewrite anything below three.

Status independence: cover the colour formatting and read the status column aloud. Does the meaning survive? If not, add words.

Time stamp consistency: pick three rows at random and confirm the date and time follow the stated convention exactly.

Keyboard path: open the file and move through it using only the keyboard. Can you reach the header row, the definitions tab and the last row without a mouse?

Export test: copy one sheet into a plain text document. Is the meaning still clear?

Ownership: is there a named role responsible for approving changes to headers and status lists? A log without an owner drifts.

Record the date of the review and the reviewer role in the notes tab. That single line makes the next review much faster.

Where the log fits alongside collection paperwork

An acknowledgement log is one piece of a wider paperwork chain. It sits between a collection request and whatever confirmation follows, and it should point to those records rather than duplicate them. Keep references, not copies of sensitive documents, inside the log.

For readers who want to understand the surrounding process, San Techno publishes information on its silicon wafer reclaim services and on wafer scrap collection. Those pages are useful context for what a collection acknowledgement is acknowledging, but the log design in this guide stands on its own and does not depend on any particular service arrangement.

A practical habit is to keep the log's reference column aligned with the references used in the collection paperwork, so a reviewer can move between the two without guessing. Where a reference is unknown at the time of entry, record that it is pending rather than leaving the cell blank; a blank cell is indistinguishable from an oversight.

Finally, revisit the log when the team changes. A handover is the moment when unclear headers and colour-only statuses cause the most friction. A short review at each handover keeps the file useful for the people who inherit it.
