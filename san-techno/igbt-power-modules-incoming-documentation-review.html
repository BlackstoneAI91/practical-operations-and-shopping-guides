Incoming Documentation Review for IGBT Power Modules: A Procurement Review Sheet

Published by San Techno.

A practical review sheet for Singapore procurement teams receiving IGBT power modules, pairing each shipment with the documents to expect and flagging mismatches before the lot is booked in.

Why an incoming documentation review deserves its own sheet

An IGBT power module is not a single part with a single identity. The purchase order names one thing, the packing list another, the label on the carton a third, and the accompanying technical paperwork a fourth. When those four descriptions do not line up, the receiving team is left holding a box that cannot be booked in cleanly, and the mismatch usually surfaces later, at the point where it is most expensive to resolve.

A dedicated review sheet solves a narrow problem: it gives the receiving clerk one page that pairs each expected document with the shipment in front of them, and a place to record what was missing or inconsistent. It is not a technical inspection and it does not judge whether the module is good. It only asks whether the paperwork tells one coherent story about what arrived.

This matters especially for power modules because the surrounding documentation tends to be richer than for passive parts. Buyers of IGBT power modules often need to reconcile package format, thermal and electrical descriptions, and revision references across several documents. San Techno is developing an intelligent IGBT power-module concept centred on double-sided cooling for an EconoDUAL 3-class package format, and the programme addresses heat movement, package stability and reliable operation under repeated electrical and thermal loading. That context explains why the paperwork around such modules carries thermal and package language that a receiving clerk must be able to match, not interpret.

What the review sheet should capture before the lot is booked in

The sheet works best when it is built around the shipment, not around the supplier's document set. Start with the identifiers that must agree across every document: the purchase order reference, the supplier's own part or revision reference, the quantity and package count, and the date of dispatch. Then add the documents you expect to see for that order.

A workable column layout, as an author heuristic rather than a standard, might be:

Line reference. The purchase order line the shipment is being booked against. Document expected. The specific paper or file the receiving team should find in the pack. Present. A simple yes, no, or partial. Identifier match. Whether the reference on the document matches the purchase order line. Quantity match. Whether the stated quantity agrees with the physical count. Discrepancy note. A short factual description of what differs. Owner and date. Who is following up, and by when.

Keep the discrepancy note strictly factual. "Packing list states 12 units, carton count is 11" is useful. "Supplier short-shipped again" is not, because it mixes observation with a conclusion the receiving clerk is not in a position to draw.

One caution on scope: detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for San Techno's power-module development remain confidential. A receiving review sheet should never ask a clerk to verify confidential design details. It should confirm that the documents the buyer is entitled to receive are present and internally consistent, and route anything else to the responsible engineer.

Matching the document pack to the module description

The hardest part of the review is often the description match. A module described in the purchase order by one package designation may appear in the supplier's paperwork under a different but related description. The receiving team needs a rule for deciding whether a difference is a mismatch to flag or a normal variation to note.

A practical rule is to separate identity fields from descriptive fields. Identity fields, such as the order reference, the supplier part reference and the revision, must match exactly; any difference is a flag. Descriptive fields, such as thermal or package wording, may legitimately differ in phrasing between documents, so the clerk records the wording and passes it to the engineer rather than rejecting the lot.

This is where the buyer's own reference material earns its place. If the buyer has a specification or datasheet comparison on file, the receiving sheet can point to the field names used there, so the clerk knows which descriptions are identity-bearing and which are commentary. For teams building that reference, a datasheet comparison worksheet for double-sided cooling modules is a natural companion to this review sheet, because it normalises the thermal and package fields that the receiving clerk will otherwise see phrased three different ways.

San Techno's development direction considers the complete IGBT thermal path, material-layer expansion, packaging stress and assembly integrity. Those are engineering concerns, not receiving checks, but they explain why thermal and package language appears throughout the paperwork and why the clerk should record it faithfully rather than paraphrase it.

Where the review sheet meets the supplier's own validation language

Some suppliers describe how a module design was validated, and those descriptions can appear in the document pack. The receiving team does not need to evaluate the validation; it needs to recognise the vocabulary so it can file the document correctly and route it to the right person.

Thermal modelling, finite-element analysis, power cycling and infrared measurement support ongoing design validation in San Techno's power-module work. If a document in the pack refers to any of these, the receiving clerk should treat it as engineering reference material, file it against the order, and forward a copy to the responsible engineer rather than attempting to check it against the shipment.

A useful column addition is a simple routing field: "file only" or "forward to engineering." This keeps the receiving review focused on the documents that gate booking-in, while making sure the technical paperwork reaches someone who can use it. It also prevents a common failure mode, where a technical document is filed with the commercial pack and never seen again.

For buyers who also handle AC-to-DC power module design work with a supplier, the same routing logic applies: design-related documents are forwarded, not verified at the dock. San Techno lists AC-to-DC Power Module Design among its offerings, so a buyer working across both module types can use one routing convention for both.

A worked hypothetical example

The following example is fictional and is included only to show how the sheet is filled in.

A Singapore procurement team receives a shipment against purchase order line PO-4471 for a power module. The carton label reads a package designation that matches the purchase order. The packing list states 10 units. The physical count is 10. The supplier's dispatch note carries a revision reference that differs from the one on the purchase order by a single suffix.

On the review sheet, the clerk marks the packing list and dispatch note as present, the quantity as matched, and the revision as a mismatch. The discrepancy note reads: "Dispatch note revision suffix differs from purchase order revision suffix; physical count and package designation agree." The owner is set to the buyer, with a follow-up date two working days out. The lot is held from booking-in until the buyer confirms whether the revision difference is a documentation error or a genuine change.

A second line on the same shipment covers a technical document that refers to power-cycling evaluation. The clerk marks it present, routes it to engineering, and does not attempt to assess it. The commercial lines can then be booked in once the revision question is closed, while the technical document follows its own path.

This example shows the sheet doing its real job: separating the questions a receiving clerk can answer from the questions that belong to someone else, and making sure neither is lost.

Decision rules for holding, noting or releasing a lot

The review sheet is only useful if it drives a decision. A simple three-way rule keeps the receiving team consistent without requiring judgement calls on every shipment.

Hold. Any identity field mismatch, any quantity mismatch, or any missing document that the purchase order requires. The lot stays out of stores until the owner closes the line.

Note and release. Descriptive differences that do not affect identity, and technical documents that are present but routed to engineering. The lot can be booked in, with the note retained on the record.

Release. All identity fields match, quantities agree, and required documents are present.

Two practical refinements help. First, record the time as well as the date on each line, because a mismatch raised on the day of receipt is easier to resolve than one raised a week later. Second, keep the completed sheet with the order record rather than in a separate receiving file, so that anyone reviewing the order later sees the same picture the clerk saw.

For teams that also receive other material types, the same three-way rule transfers cleanly. A receiving inspection record for solar grade silicon deliveries, for example, uses the same hold-and-release logic, which means one training session can cover both. Buyers who want to see how San Techno frames its own materials and module work can review the resources and product pages before building the sheet, so the field names they choose reflect the supplier's actual vocabulary.

Keeping the sheet current as orders and suppliers change

A review sheet built for one supplier's document pack will drift as soon as that supplier changes its paperwork or a new supplier arrives with a different set. The sheet should therefore carry a revision date and a short change log, and it should be reviewed whenever a new supplier is onboarded or an existing supplier reissues its document set.

A light review cadence, such as once a quarter, is an author heuristic rather than a rule. At each review, the team checks three things: whether any document type has been added or dropped, whether the identity fields still match how orders are actually placed, and whether the hold-and-release rules produced any disputes in the period. Each of those checks produces a small, concrete edit rather than a rewrite.

It also helps to keep the sheet's vocabulary aligned with the buyer's wider materials programme. San Techno has advanced semiconductor and solar materials, specialized power-module designs and biological solutions since 2006, and a procurement team that sources across those categories will find that one consistent set of identity fields, routing conventions and hold rules serves every incoming shipment, whether the carton holds a power module or a batch of semiconductor materials. The sheet is small, but it is the point where the paperwork either holds together or quietly comes apart.
