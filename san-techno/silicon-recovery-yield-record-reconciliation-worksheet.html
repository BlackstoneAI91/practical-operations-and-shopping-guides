Yield Record Reconciliation Worksheet for Silicon Recovery Batches

Published by San Techno.

A practical worksheet for Singapore procurement teams to reconcile the yield figures a silicon recovery vendor reports against the quantities the site shipped, with fields, worked examples and escalation rules.

Why a yield record needs its own reconciliation step

A silicon recovery batch leaves a site with two separate records that are rarely written by the same person. The shipping side records what was packed, weighed and handed over. The vendor side records what was received and what yield came out of processing. Between those two documents sits a gap that procurement is usually asked to close only when a number looks wrong.

The purpose of this worksheet is to make that gap visible before it becomes a dispute. It does not assume either record is correct. It lines up the quantities the site shipped against the yield figures the vendor reported, marks every point where the two diverge, and records who is responsible for explaining each divergence.

San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and the company began in 2006 as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials. That history is useful context for procurement teams: a recovery batch is a commercial handover, and the paperwork around it deserves the same discipline as any other incoming or outgoing material record.

One caution before starting. Yield figures are not a single number with a single meaning. A vendor may report a processed weight, a recovered fraction, a grade split, or a batch-level summary. The worksheet only works if each figure is labelled with the basis on which it was reported. If the basis is unclear, that is itself a finding to record.

What to collect before the worksheet is opened

The worksheet is a reconciliation tool, not a filing cabinet. Gather the source records first, then fill the sheet once. A practical pack for a single batch usually contains:

- The internal dispatch record: package count, lot or batch reference, and the quantity the site recorded at handover. - The collection or handover note signed at the point the material left the site. - The vendor's acknowledgement of receipt, if one was issued. - The vendor's yield or processing report for that batch. - Any correspondence that changed the batch scope after the original note, such as a withdrawn package or a re-labelled lot.

Two rules keep this stage honest. First, do not correct a source record while reconciling; note the discrepancy and leave the original intact. Second, do not accept a verbal yield figure as a source record. If the number arrived in a call, ask for it in writing before it enters the worksheet.

For teams that also handle reclaimable wafers alongside recovery scrap, keep the two document sets separate. A wafer reclaim intake pack and a recovery batch pack answer different questions, and mixing them makes both harder to audit.

The worksheet: columns and what each one is for

The layout below is a suggested structure, not a standard form. Adapt the column names to your own records system, but keep the sequence, because each column depends on the one before it.

1. Batch reference. The identifier used on the dispatch record. Use one reference per row so a split batch produces more than one row. 2. Dispatch quantity. What the site recorded at handover, with the unit stated explicitly. 3. Handover note reference. The document that evidences the transfer, with its date. 4. Vendor receipt quantity. What the vendor acknowledged receiving, with the unit stated. 5. Vendor yield figure. The reported yield, plus a short note on the basis used. 6. Basis match. A yes or no field: does the vendor's stated basis match the basis your dispatch record uses? A no here explains many apparent quantity gaps. 7. Variance. Dispatch quantity minus vendor receipt quantity, and separately, receipt quantity against the yield figure. Keep the two variances in separate columns; combining them hides which stage the gap occurred at. 8. Owner. The named role responsible for explaining the variance, not a person's name in a shared file. 9. Status. Open, explained, or escalated. 10. Evidence attached. A short list of what supports the explanation, such as a revised note or a written clarification.

A worked fictional example makes the sequence concrete. Suppose a site dispatches a batch recorded as 400 units across 16 packages. The vendor acknowledges 15 packages and reports a yield figure on a different basis from the dispatch record. The worksheet would show a package-count variance of one, a basis mismatch of yes, and two open rows: one for the missing package, one for the basis question. Neither is resolved by arithmetic alone.

Reading the variances without jumping to conclusions

Most variances on this worksheet fall into a small number of categories, and naming the category is more useful than arguing about the number.

A unit or basis mismatch is the most common and the least alarming. One record may count packages, another may count weight, and a third may report a recovered fraction. The fix is a written statement of basis, not a recalculation.

A timing gap occurs when the vendor's report covers a processing window that does not align with the dispatch date. Record both dates and mark the row as pending rather than open.

A scope change happens when part of a batch was withdrawn, re-labelled, or held back after the original note. This should be evidenced by correspondence, not by memory.

A genuine quantity gap is the category that needs escalation. It should be the last explanation reached, not the first, because the earlier categories are far easier to check.

A useful discipline is to require an explanation category before a row can move from open to explained. That single field stops a worksheet from becoming a list of numbers with no reasoning attached.

A short decision rule for closing or escalating a row

Procurement teams often ask when a variance is small enough to close. There is no universal threshold, and any figure suggested here is an author heuristic for illustration only, not an industry standard.

A workable rule has three tests, applied in order:

First, is the basis documented? If the vendor's yield figure has no stated basis, the row stays open regardless of size.

Second, is the variance explained by a category that has evidence attached? A timing gap with both dates recorded can close. A scope change with a written note can close. An unexplained quantity gap cannot.

Third, does the variance affect a downstream record, such as an inventory balance or a supplier scorecard entry? If it does, the row should be escalated even when the amount is small, because the error will propagate.

As an example of how a team might apply this, a fictional site could decide that any row affecting a period-end inventory figure goes to the materials manager, while rows affecting only a single batch note stay with the buyer. The point is to write the rule down before the worksheet is full, not to negotiate it row by row.

Where this worksheet sits among your other records

The yield reconciliation worksheet is one document in a chain, and it works best when the documents around it are consistent.

Upstream, the dispatch and handover records must be complete before reconciliation starts. If your team is still tightening that stage, a pickup readiness checklist and a collection manifest reconciliation process are the natural companions, because they fix the inputs this worksheet depends on.

Downstream, the worksheet feeds supplier review. A row that stays open across two batches is a pattern, and patterns belong in a quarterly vendor conversation rather than a single email thread. Keeping the worksheet's status field current is what makes that possible.

For teams sourcing semiconductor materials more broadly, the same reconciliation logic applies to incoming lots, where a receiving record and a supplier's delivery note must be matched before stock is booked in. The worksheet structure transfers; only the column labels change.

Keeping each category's reconciliation separate, with its own worksheet, is the simplest way to avoid cross-contamination between records.

A blank worksheet you can copy into your own template

The following is a plain-text skeleton. Copy it into your own document system and rename the fields to match your records.

Batch reference | Dispatch quantity and unit | Handover note reference and date | Vendor receipt quantity and unit | Vendor yield figure and stated basis | Basis match (yes/no) | Dispatch-to-receipt variance | Receipt-to-yield variance | Explanation category | Owner role | Status | Evidence attached | Date closed

Fill one row per batch reference. Leave a row open rather than guessing a category. Review the sheet at a fixed interval, such as weekly while a batch is live, and record the review date so the file shows when it was last touched.

Two closing habits make the worksheet durable. First, never delete a row; mark it closed with a reason. Second, keep the vendor's original report attached rather than transcribed, so a later reader can check the basis without relying on your summary. A worksheet that survives an audit is one where every number can be traced back to a document someone else can open.
