Shift Handover Sheet for Silicon Collection Records

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building a one-page shift handover sheet that passes open silicon collection records, pending pickups and missing signatures cleanly from one shift to the next.

Why a handover sheet is different from a collection record

A collection record describes one movement of material: what was released, by whom, under which reference. A handover sheet describes the state of several records at a single moment in time, written for a reader who was not present when the work happened. Confusing the two is the most common reason a shift change loses track of a pending pickup.

Treat the handover sheet as a status snapshot, not a duplicate of the file. It should point to the underlying records rather than restate them. If a receiving clerk needs the full detail, the sheet tells them which collection note, manifest or intake record to open. If the sheet tries to carry every field, it becomes a second, competing version of the truth and the two will drift apart within a week.

This matters most where silicon changes hands between parties. San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, so a collection can involve several organisations and several reference numbers at once. A handover sheet that names the counterparty, the reference and the open action for each line keeps that multi-party picture legible to whoever picks up the shift.

Keep the sheet to one page. A one-page constraint forces the writer to decide what the next shift actually needs, and it makes the sheet quick to read at the start of a shift when attention is divided.

The six columns that carry the handover

A workable handover sheet has a small, fixed set of columns. The following is an author heuristic, not a standard: adapt it to your own site.

1. Record reference. The collection note, manifest or intake record number. This is the anchor; every other column hangs off it. 2. Counterparty and site. Who is releasing or receiving, and at which location. Use the name as it appears on the record, not a nickname. 3. Material description and package count. A short description plus the number of packages as stated on the record. Do not recount here; just carry the recorded figure. 4. Status. A small controlled list, for example: awaiting pickup, in transit, received pending check, signature outstanding, query open, closed. 5. Open action and owner. The single next thing that must happen, and the role responsible. One action per line keeps the sheet honest. 6. Age or shift opened. When the line first appeared on a handover sheet. This is how a stuck item becomes visible.

Add a signature column only if your process genuinely requires a physical or electronic acknowledgement at handover. If signatures are collected elsewhere, say so on the sheet rather than leaving an empty column that looks like an omission.

A worked fictional example: line ST-0412, counterparty a wafer manufacturer, twenty-four packages of off-spec material, status received pending check, open action is to confirm the package count against the collection note, owner is the receiving lead, opened two shifts ago. That single line tells the incoming shift everything it needs to decide whether to act now or leave it.

Writing the sheet so the next shift can act on it

The outgoing shift knows things the sheet will not say unless it is written deliberately. Three habits help.

First, write actions, not observations. "Count mismatch noted" is an observation. "Receiving lead to recheck package count against collection note ST-0412 before release" is an action with an owner. The incoming shift can start on the second one immediately.

Second, separate what is waiting on you from what is waiting on someone else. A pending pickup that depends on the counterparty confirming a slot is not the same as a signature your own team has not yet collected. Grouping by who holds the next move prevents the incoming shift from chasing the wrong party.

Third, carry forward unresolved lines rather than rewriting them. If a line has appeared on three consecutive handover sheets, that repetition is the signal. Some teams add a simple escalation rule, such as flagging any line open beyond a set number of shifts for a supervisor review; the threshold is a local choice, not a researched figure.

Where the collection involves recovered silicon destined for reuse, the record trail matters beyond the shift. San Techno notes that recovered materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending their useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss. A handover sheet that keeps references intact supports that continuity, because the next team can trace a package back to its source record without reconstructing the history from memory.

Missing signatures: what the sheet should say

A missing signature is the most common open item on a handover sheet, and the easiest to describe badly. "Signature pending" tells the next shift nothing about whether the gap is expected, who must sign, or what happens if it is not obtained.

Use a short, structured note instead. Record which document lacks the signature, which role must provide it, whether the material has already moved, and what the site's rule is for releasing or holding the record in the meantime. If your process allows the material to move before the signature is collected, say so explicitly, because the incoming shift will otherwise assume the worst.

A fictional example: collection note ST-0418, counterparty a chip packaging facility, material already collected, receiving acknowledgement signature outstanding from the site supervisor, record may be filed once signed, owner is the documentation clerk. That line is actionable. It also protects the clerk who inherits it, because the sheet shows the gap was known and assigned before the shift changed.

Avoid writing reasons or explanations that speculate about why a signature is missing. Record the fact and the next step. Speculation on a handover sheet hardens into an assumption the next shift may act on.

A pre-handover checklist for the outgoing shift

Before the outgoing shift leaves, run a short checklist against the sheet. The items below are suggestions, not a certified procedure.

Every open record appears exactly once, with its reference spelled as it appears on the source document. Every line has a status from the controlled list, not a free-text phrase. Every line has one open action and one owner role. Lines carried from the previous sheet show how long they have been open. Missing signatures name the document, the required role and the release rule. Pending pickups state whether the slot is confirmed, proposed or unconfirmed. The sheet is dated and names the outgoing shift, so the incoming team knows how fresh it is. Anything the incoming shift must not touch is marked clearly, with the reason kept brief.

The checklist is deliberately about the sheet, not about the material. Its job is to catch the gaps that make a handover ambiguous. A sheet that passes all eight items is not guaranteed to be complete, but it is readable, and readability is what the next shift needs at the start of their day.

Where the handover sheet sits in the wider record set

The handover sheet is one document among several, and it works best when its boundaries are clear. Collection notes, manifests and intake records are the primary evidence. The handover sheet is a status layer over them, and it should never be the only place a fact is recorded.

When a line closes, remove it from the next sheet and confirm that the underlying record is complete. When a line is disputed, the sheet should point to the exception record rather than carry the dispute itself. This keeps the sheet short and keeps the detailed evidence in the documents designed to hold it.

Adapting the sheet to your own site

No single handover sheet fits every operation. Start from the six columns, run the sheet for a few shift changes, and then adjust based on where the incoming team asks questions. Questions are the best signal that a column is missing or a status is unclear.

Two adjustments are worth considering early. If your site handles both semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon, you may need a material category column so the incoming shift can route lines to the right specialist. If collections are frequent, a simple numbering scheme for handover sheets themselves helps you trace which sheet a line first appeared on.

Keep the language plain and consistent. Use the same status words every time, and resist the temptation to add a new status for each unusual case. A short controlled list, reviewed periodically, will serve better than a long one that nobody remembers.

Finally, treat the sheet as a living document that belongs to the shift team, not to a single author. The people who read it at the start of a shift are the best judges of whether it works, and their feedback is more valuable than any template.
