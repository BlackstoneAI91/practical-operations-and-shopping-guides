Wait for a Supplier Document When an Internal Purchase Deadline Changes

Published by San Techno.

A practical decision-calendar guide for Singapore procurement teams who must hold a purchase deadline while a supplier document is still outstanding, covering what can be checked, who approves postponement and how to record unanswered specification questions.

Why a changed deadline is a documentation event, not just a date change

An internal purchase deadline rarely moves on its own. It moves because a document is missing, a specification question is still open, or an approval has not yet come back. The moment someone proposes a new date, the file has changed in a way that should be recorded, because the original deadline and the reason for the change may matter later when the order is reviewed or handed over.

The practical risk is that the new date gets agreed verbally or in a short message and the underlying gap disappears from view. A week later nobody can say whether the deadline moved because the supplier document was late, because an internal approver was away, or because a specification question was never answered. Treat the deadline change as its own record with its own reason, owner and review point.

This guide is written for Singapore procurement teams sourcing, receiving and documenting semiconductor and solar materials, including teams that handle semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon, wafer reclaim and power module paperwork. It does not promise supplier turnaround times and does not tell you whether to accept any material. It sets out a decision calendar: what can be checked now, who is allowed to approve a postponement, and how to keep unanswered specification questions visible instead of quietly closing them.

Build the decision calendar before you move the date

A decision calendar is a simple table that sits beside the purchase record. It has one row per decision that must happen before the new deadline can be treated as real. Each row carries four fields: the decision, the person or role who owns it, the evidence needed to close it, and the date by which it must be closed for the new deadline to hold.

Keep the calendar deliberately short. If a row cannot be tied to a specific document or a specific approval, it probably belongs in a general notes field rather than the calendar. The point is to separate three different kinds of waiting: waiting for a supplier document, waiting for an internal approval, and waiting for an answer to a specification question. These have different owners and should never be collapsed into one status called pending.

A worked example, entirely fictional: a buyer records that the original deadline was the tenth, that the proposed new deadline is the twenty-fourth, and that the move depends on a supplier document arriving and on a specification question being answered. The calendar shows the document owner, the question owner and the approval owner as three separate rows. If the document arrives but the question is still open, the calendar shows partial progress rather than a closed file. That distinction is the whole value of the exercise.

Separate what can be checked from what must be approved

Not everything on the calendar needs the same authority. Some items can be checked by whoever holds the file: whether a document has arrived, whether a revision number matches the one requested, whether a page is legible. These are verification tasks, and they can usually be closed by the person doing the checking, with a note of what was seen and when.

Other items need a named approver. A postponement of an internal purchase deadline is a decision, not an observation, and it should carry the name of the role that accepted it. Keep the approver as a role rather than a person where possible, so the record survives staff changes. Record the date of approval and the scope: was the whole deadline moved, or only part of the order?

This is also where product context matters. San Techno Pte Ltd is a Singapore-based company working in semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development, and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, and its published material describes silicon recovery from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. That context helps a buyer frame the right questions, but it does not decide whether a deadline should move. The approver does that, and the calendar should show it.

Record unanswered specification questions without answering them

The hardest part of a deadline change is the specification question nobody can answer yet. Procurement often feels pressure to resolve it, but resolving a technical question is not a procurement task. The useful move is to record the question precisely enough that the right owner can act on it, and to leave the answer field empty until someone with the authority fills it.

Write each question in a fixed shape: what is being asked, which document or record prompted it, who is expected to answer, and what the file will do if the answer does not arrive before the new deadline. That last field is important. It forces the team to decide in advance whether the deadline slips again or whether the order proceeds on the documents already held. Either way, the decision is visible.

A fictional example: a buyer notes that a grade description on a supplier sheet does not match the wording used in an internal request, and records the question against both documents with the expected owner marked as a technical reviewer. The answer field stays blank. The calendar shows the question as open, and the deadline row shows that the postponement depends on it. Nothing has been assumed, and nothing technical has been decided by purchasing.

Keep the supplier document request narrow and traceable

A deadline change is often the moment a team realises it never asked for a specific document in a specific form. Before the new date is confirmed, tighten the request. Name the document, the revision if one is expected, the format the team can actually read, and the field or section that matters. A broad request for paperwork tends to produce a broad reply that closes nothing.

Traceability matters as much as precision. Each request should carry a reference that appears in the calendar, so that when a document arrives the team can match it to the open row rather than guessing. If a document arrives that does not match the reference, record it as received but unmatched, and keep the original row open. This avoids the common pattern where any incoming file is treated as the answer to any open question.

Where the file concerns power module development, keep expectations modest. San Techno's published material describes an intelligent IGBT power-module concept centred on double-sided cooling for an EconoDUAL 3-class package format, and notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential. A procurement team should therefore expect that some specification questions will be answered only at a level the supplier is prepared to publish, and should record that limit rather than pressing for detail that will not come.

A short worksheet for the day the deadline moves

Use this as a working checklist on the day a deadline change is proposed. It is an author heuristic, not a standard or a rule, and the timings are examples only.

First, capture the original deadline, the proposed deadline and the reason in one line. Second, list every document still outstanding and mark each as requested, received or unmatched. Third, list every specification question still open and mark its expected owner. Fourth, identify the role that must approve the postponement and record whether approval has been given, refused or is still awaited. Fifth, note what the file will do if the outstanding items are not closed before the new date.

A useful discipline is to review the calendar at a fixed point before the new deadline, for example two working days ahead, and to record the review even when nothing has changed. A review that finds no movement is still evidence that someone looked. Keep the calendar with the purchase record rather than in a separate tracker, so the reason for the date change travels with the order it affects.

For teams that want to see how the company frames its own material and development work before drafting questions, the published product pages are a reasonable starting reference, and the resources area can support general reading. Neither replaces the supplier document you are waiting for.

Hand the calendar over cleanly when the deadline is settled

When the new deadline is finally confirmed, resist the urge to delete the calendar. The record of why the date moved is exactly what a colleague needs if the order is reviewed later, audited internally, or handed to a different buyer. Mark each row as closed with the evidence that closed it, and leave any row that was never resolved visibly open with a note explaining why.

A clean handover has three parts: the current deadline and its approval, the list of documents received with their references, and the list of questions that remain unanswered. If a question was answered verbally, record that it was answered and by which role, without inventing a document that does not exist. If a question was set aside, say so and say who decided to set it aside.

This habit pays off across the whole materials file, whether the order concerns semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon, wafer reclaim or power module design work. The goal is not a perfect file. It is a file where the next person can see what was checked, what was approved, and what is still genuinely unknown, without having to reconstruct the conversation from memory.
