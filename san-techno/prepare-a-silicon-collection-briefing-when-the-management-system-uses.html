Preparing a Silicon Collection Briefing When Your Management System Still Runs on Paper Forms

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on writing a silicon collection briefing that keeps paper forms, receipt references and later digitisation records aligned, without touching physical handling or transport steps.

Why a paper-based management system needs its own briefing

Many procurement teams in Singapore work with a mix of systems: an electronic ledger for purchase orders, a shared drive for specifications, and a stack of paper forms for anything that crosses a physical boundary. When a collection request moves through that stack, the briefing is the only document that explains how the paper trail connects to the digital record. Without it, a form can be signed, filed and forgotten while the electronic file shows nothing.

The purpose of a collection briefing is administrative. It tells the reader which paper form starts the process, which reference number travels with it, who signs each field, and where the scanned copy will eventually sit. It does not describe how material is handled, packed or moved. Keep that boundary explicit in the first paragraph so nobody mistakes the briefing for an operational instruction.

San Techno Pte Ltd, a Singapore-based company working in semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, is one example of a counterparty a briefing might name. Naming the counterparty is fine; describing what happens to the material is not part of this document. Treat the briefing as a filing map, and the rest of the guide follows from that single decision.

Start with the receipt reference, not the form number

The most common failure in a paper-based process is that the form number and the receipt reference drift apart. A receiving clerk writes the internal form number on the top sheet, a colleague writes a supplier reference on the carbon copy, and by the time both reach the procurement folder nobody can prove they describe the same event.

Fix this at the briefing stage by declaring one reference as the anchor. A workable rule is that the receipt reference is the anchor because it is the number that will later appear on invoices, acknowledgements and any correspondence about the same request. The paper form number becomes a secondary field, recorded beside the anchor rather than instead of it.

Write the rule as a short instruction: every paper form must carry the receipt reference in a designated box, written by the person who opens the form, before any other field is completed. Then state what happens if the box is blank: the form is returned to the originator rather than completed retrospectively. This is an author heuristic, not a standard, but it gives a paper process a single spine. Where the briefing names a counterparty such as San Techno, the same anchor rule applies to correspondence about a wafer reclaim enquiry, so the reference survives the handoff between paper and email.

Map each paper field to a digital field before anyone signs

A briefing is most useful when it includes a small mapping table. On the left, list every field printed on the paper form. On the right, name the digital field or folder where that value will later be recorded. Where no digital field exists yet, write 'to be created' rather than leaving the cell blank, because a blank cell hides a decision.

A fictional example keeps this concrete. Suppose the paper form has fields for date received, material description, quantity, originator, approver and remarks. The briefing might map date received to the intake log date, material description to the catalogue caption field, and remarks to a free-text note that must begin with the receipt reference. Quantity and approver map to fields that already exist in the purchasing ledger.

The mapping table does double duty. It shows the reader what to write, and it shows the reviewer what is still missing from the digital system. Keep the table to one page. If it grows beyond that, the paper form is probably carrying fields that belong in a separate document. For context on how recovered silicon moves back into the value chain, the silicon scrap recycling page describes the broader picture without prescribing any handling step.

Write the digitisation step as a dated event, not an assumption

Paper forms are often described as 'scanned later', which is not a record. A briefing should turn that phrase into a dated event with an owner. Write a line such as: the completed form is scanned within a stated number of working days by the named role, and the scan is saved to the folder named in the mapping table with the receipt reference as the filename prefix.

Choose the timing as an example and label it as such. A common internal convention is two to five working days, but the briefing should say 'example timing' so readers do not treat it as a rule imposed from outside. The important part is that the date of digitisation is recorded, because that date is what later proves the paper and digital records describe the same event.

Add a second line for exceptions. If a form cannot be scanned because a signature is missing, the briefing should say who is notified and where the pending item is tracked. This keeps the paper process honest: an unscanned form is a visible open item, not a silent gap. The wafer scrap collection page is a useful reference point for readers who want to see how a collection enquiry is framed publicly, while the briefing itself stays focused on the internal record.

Separate the briefing from the approval decision

A collection briefing describes how records travel. It does not decide whether a request should proceed. Mixing the two is how paper processes acquire implied approvals that nobody intended to give.

Keep a short section in the briefing headed 'Decisions not covered here'. Under it, list the decisions that belong to other documents: whether the request is approved, who holds budget authority, and what happens if the counterparty changes the scope. Each line should name the document or role that owns the decision, not the answer. This is a routing record, not an approval record.

A fictional illustration: a briefing might state that approval sits with the purchasing manager and is recorded on a separate approval sheet, while the briefing only tracks that the approval sheet exists and has been referenced. If the approval sheet is missing, the briefing records 'approval reference outstanding' and the request stays pending. That single convention prevents a paper form from being read as consent. San Techno's public materials describe silicon recovery as channeling non-usable silicon from fabs, wafer makers, cell producers and packaging facilities back into the value chain, which is context for why such requests arise, not a statement about any individual approval.

A one-page briefing template you can adapt

The template below is a starting point, not a standard. Adapt the field names to your own forms and keep the structure.

Purpose and scope: one sentence stating that the briefing covers records only. Counterparty and contact role: the organisation name and the internal role that liaises, with no personal details. Anchor reference: the receipt reference and the rule for where it is written. Field mapping: the table described earlier. Digitisation event: who scans, by when as an example, and where the file is saved. Exceptions: who is notified and where pending items are tracked. Decisions not covered: a short list of decisions owned elsewhere. Review date: when the briefing itself will be checked against the current forms.

Keep the whole document to one page. A briefing that runs longer tends to absorb operational detail it should not contain. If a reader needs more, point them to the relevant destination rather than expanding the template. The wafer scrap collection page and the silicon scrap recycling page are the natural places to send readers who want the public framing of a collection or recovery enquiry, while the briefing remains an internal filing aid.

Review the briefing when forms or systems change

A paper-based briefing ages quickly. A form is revised, a digital field is renamed, or a folder is reorganised, and the mapping table quietly becomes wrong. Build a review trigger into the document itself.

Write a review line that names the events which force a check: a new version of the paper form, a change to the digital ledger, or a change in the counterparty's public contact route. On each trigger, the owner compares the briefing against the current form and records the comparison date. If nothing has changed, the date still gets recorded, because an unchanged briefing with a recent check date is more trustworthy than one with no date at all.

When a change is found, do not overwrite the old mapping silently. Add a revision note at the foot of the briefing with the date, the field affected and the new value, and keep the previous note visible. This mirrors the way a procurement file should treat superseded documents: the old record stays, clearly marked, so a later reader can see what changed and when. That habit is what makes a paper-based management system auditable without pretending it is something it is not.
