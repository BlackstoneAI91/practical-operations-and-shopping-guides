Separating Confidentiality Questions from a Semiconductor Materials Purchase Request

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on splitting open and restricted questions out of a semiconductor materials purchase request, assigning a contact owner, and confirming what each party may disclose before the request moves forward.

Why the purchase request is the wrong place for confidentiality questions

A purchase request is a working document. It carries quantities, delivery expectations, document references and the approvals that let a buyer commit. When a confidentiality question is buried inside it, two things happen at once. The commercial request slows down because someone has to decide whether the question blocks the order, and the confidentiality question gets answered informally, often by whoever happens to reply first.

The cleaner habit is to treat the two as separate tracks that share a reference number. The purchase request stays commercial and factual. The confidentiality question becomes its own short record with an owner, a scope and a status. Neither track needs to wait for the other unless a specific decision genuinely depends on the answer.

This matters most where a supplier's public material is deliberately limited. San Techno, a Singapore-based company working across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, states on its product pages that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential. That is a normal and legitimate boundary. A procurement team that recognises the boundary early can ask better questions about what is open rather than pressing for what is not.

A useful test before you write anything: would this question change the price, the quantity, the delivery date or the acceptance decision? If yes, it belongs in the purchase request. If it is about what a supplier will reveal, to whom, or under what handling conditions, it belongs on the confidentiality track.

Sort every question into three disclosure categories

Before assigning owners, sort the questions themselves. Three categories are usually enough for a procurement file, and they keep the conversation honest about what can actually be answered.

Open questions can be answered from public or already-shared material. Examples include which product family a request refers to, what documentation accompanies a first delivery, or how a supplier describes its general areas of work. San Techno's public pages, for instance, describe three branches of activity: semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design. A buyer can confirm which branch a request touches without asking for anything restricted.

Restricted questions touch detail the supplier has chosen not to publish. The power-module confidentiality note is the clearest example. A restricted question is not rude, but it should be framed as a request for a disclosure decision rather than an assumption of an answer.

Held questions are ones your own organisation cannot yet release: internal cost targets, end-customer identities, or the commercial reason a specification was chosen. These are often the questions that leak into a purchase request by accident.

A simple worksheet works here. Give each question a row with four columns: the question in one sentence, its category, the person who owns the answer, and the date it was last reviewed. Keep the worksheet beside the purchase request rather than inside it. When a restricted question is answered, record only that a disclosure decision was made and by which role, not the restricted content itself.

Assign one contact owner per question, not per department

The most common failure in this split is ownership by committee. A question is copied to purchasing, engineering and quality, and then sits unanswered because each group assumes another is handling it. Assigning a single named role to each question, even when several people contribute, removes that ambiguity.

A practical pattern for a Singapore procurement team:

Commercial questions about quantities, delivery windows and order references go to the buyer who raised the request.

Documentation questions about what accompanies a delivery go to the receiving or quality role that will check it on arrival.

Disclosure questions about what a supplier will share go to the commercial owner of the supplier relationship, who then decides whether to escalate.

Internal release questions about what your own organisation may share go to the person who approved the purchase request.

Each row in the worksheet should name a role, not a person, so the record survives staff changes. Add a second column for a deputy role. If the owner is unavailable for more than a working week, the deputy answers or explicitly defers with a new date.

One caution: an owner is accountable for getting an answer, not entitled to give one. A buyer who owns a disclosure question is responsible for routing it and recording the outcome. They are not authorised to promise that a supplier will reveal restricted detail, and they should not imply that a purchase order buys access to it.

Write the disclosure confirmation before the request goes out

A short written confirmation, agreed before the purchase request is issued, prevents most later confusion. It does not need to be a contract clause and it is not legal advice. It is an internal record of who may say what.

A workable confirmation covers four points:

What each party may disclose publicly. For example, that a supplier's general areas of activity can be referenced, while detailed design information cannot.

What each party may share privately with named roles, and whether that sharing needs to be logged.

Who on each side is the single point of contact for disclosure questions.

What happens when a question cannot be answered, including who records the refusal and how it is communicated internally.

The fourth point is the one teams skip, and it is the one that causes friction. A recorded "not disclosed" is a useful outcome. It tells the buyer to plan around the gap rather than wait for information that is not coming.

Where a supplier publishes a confidentiality boundary, quote it accurately in the confirmation rather than paraphrasing it into something broader or narrower. San Techno's published note that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential is a good example of a boundary that should be copied as written, because a loose paraphrase could either overstate the restriction or accidentally suggest more openness than the supplier intended.

A worked example using fictional parties

The following example is invented and is intended only to show the mechanics.

A fictional Singapore buyer, Harbourline Components, is preparing a purchase request for reclaimed silicon wafers from a fictional supplier, Eastwind Materials. The draft request contains eleven questions. The buyer sorts them.

Six are open: delivery quantities, packaging references, the documents that will accompany the first lot, the named receiving contact, the collection window and the invoice reference format. These stay in the purchase request.

Three are restricted: whether the supplier will share the internal inspection method used on returned wafers, whether it will name its own upstream sources, and whether it will disclose the composition of a surface treatment. These move to the confidentiality track. The commercial owner routes them and records that two were declined and one was answered at a general level.

Two are held: Harbourline's internal cost ceiling and the identity of its end customer. These are removed from the request entirely and kept in the internal approval file.

The purchase request now contains six answerable questions and moves forward. The confidentiality track contains five rows, each with an owner and a status. Nothing is lost, and nothing restricted has been written into a document that will be circulated widely.

A second fictional example shows the same split for a power-module enquiry. A buyer asks which module family a quotation refers to, which is open, and separately asks for internal layer dimensions, which is restricted. The first question can be answered from published material. The second is routed as a disclosure request and, if declined, recorded as declined.

Keep the two tracks linked without merging them

Separate tracks still need a shared reference. Use the purchase request number as the common key, and add a single line to the request noting that related confidentiality questions are held on a separate record. That line is enough. Do not attach the confidentiality worksheet to the request, and do not paste restricted answers into it.

Review the two tracks on different rhythms. The purchase request moves at the speed of the order. The confidentiality worksheet can be reviewed less often, perhaps at a fixed interval you choose for your team, with each open row either answered, declined or given a new review date. Treat any interval you pick as an internal convention rather than a rule drawn from research.

When a request closes, close the confidentiality rows with it. An answered row becomes a note in the supplier file. A declined row becomes a known gap that the next buyer should not re-ask without a reason. A held row returns to the internal approval file.

For teams that want to see how a supplier describes its own areas of work before drafting questions, the published product and resource pages are the right starting point, and the company background page helps a buyer understand which branch of activity a request touches. Reading those pages first often converts several would-be restricted questions into open ones, simply because the answer is already public.

A short pre-send checklist

Run this checklist before any purchase request leaves the team. It is a set of author conventions, not a validated instrument, and the scoring below is only an example of how a team might use it.

Every question in the request can be answered from open or already-shared material.

No question asks for restricted design detail, and any such question has been moved to the confidentiality track.

No internal cost, customer or approval information appears in the request.

Each confidentiality row names one owner role and one deputy role.

Each confidentiality row has a status: open, answered, declined or held.

A disclosure confirmation has been agreed and stored with the supplier file.

A single line in the request points to the separate confidentiality record.

A closing step exists for each row when the request closes.

As an example of use, a team might score each line as met, partly met or not met, and hold the request if any line about restricted detail is not met. That scoring is a local habit, not a standard.

The underlying principle is simple. A purchase request should be a document anyone in the chain can read without breaching a confidence. Confidentiality questions deserve their own record, their own owner and their own honest status, including when the answer is no.
