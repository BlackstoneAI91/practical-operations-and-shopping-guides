Comparing Semiconductor Materials Quotes When Samples Use Different Units

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement and operations teams on building a quote comparison that stays honest when suppliers quote semiconductor materials in different sample units, packaging units and reference specifications.

Why a quote comparison breaks when sample units differ

You ask three suppliers for a sample of the same semiconductor materials. One replies in grams, one in wafers, one in a per-piece price with a minimum pack. The numbers land in the same spreadsheet and look comparable. They are not. A gram price and a wafer price describe different things, and a per-piece price attached to a sealed pack of twenty is not a per-piece price for one piece.

The fix is not a cleverer formula. It is a comparison sheet that records what each number actually refers to before anyone totals anything. Procurement staff in Singapore often work across suppliers who quote in different conventions, and the sheet has to survive that without pretending the units are the same.

This guide sets out the columns to keep, the bookkeeping that keeps mixed units honest, and the questions to send back to suppliers. It is written for procurement and operations readers, not for process engineers, and it deliberately avoids process instructions. The core columns: reference specification, packaging unit, sample quantity

Three columns do most of the work. Build them before you build any price column.

Reference specification. Record the exact document, revision and date that the supplier's quote is priced against. Two quotes for what looks like the same line item can be priced against different revisions, and the sheet should make that visible rather than hide it in a note. If a supplier has not stated a reference, write "not stated" rather than filling the gap yourself.

Packaging unit. Record how the material is supplied: the pack, the cassette, the bottle, the box, and how many units sit inside it. A price per pack is not a price per unit until you divide, and the division belongs in a separate column so the original figure stays visible.

Sample quantity. Record the quantity offered for evaluation and the unit it is expressed in. A sample of 25 grams and a sample of 25 wafers are not the same request, and a sheet that lists both under "qty 25" will mislead whoever reads it next.

Add two more columns that cost nothing and save arguments: the date the quote was received, and the named response owner on your side. A quote without a date is hard to compare with a later revision, and a quote without an owner tends to sit unanswered.

A worked example with fictional figures

The figures below are invented for illustration only. They are not prices, and they do not describe any real supplier.

Suppose Supplier A quotes solar grade silicon at 40 units per kilogram, supplied in 5-kilogram packs, with a 200-gram evaluation sample. Supplier B quotes the same category at 55 units per wafer, supplied in cassettes of 25 wafers, with a 5-wafer sample. Supplier C quotes 30 units per piece, supplied in boxes of 20 pieces, with a 2-piece sample.

On a naive sheet, the three prices sit in one column and Supplier C looks cheapest. On a proper sheet, the columns read: reference specification (A: rev 3, dated; B: not stated; C: rev 1, dated), packaging unit (5 kg pack / 25-wafer cassette / 20-piece box), sample quantity (200 g / 5 wafers / 2 pieces), and a derived column headed "price per stated base unit, as quoted". The derived column is labelled with its unit in the header, so nobody can total it by accident.

A second derived column, "normalised figure", is where you convert to a common basis, and it must carry a note saying which basis you chose and why. If no honest common basis exists, leave the column blank and say so. A blank cell with a reason is more useful than a number with a hidden assumption.

Keep the original quoted figure in its own column throughout. When a later reader asks why the normalised figure differs from the quote, the original is still there to explain it.

Handling mixed categories without forcing a single unit

Not every category in a comparison sheet can share a unit, and trying to force one creates false precision. Semiconductor materials, reclaimed wafers and power-module design work are different kinds of purchase, and a sheet that ranks them on one number is ranking things that were never comparable.

A workable approach is to group rows by category and give each group its own small summary block, rather than one grand total at the bottom. Within a group, normalise where the units allow it. Across groups, compare only the things that share a basis, and state the basis in the group heading.

Where a supplier's offering is a development programme rather than a catalogue item, the comparison sheet should record what is public and mark the rest as pending. San Techno's public pages note that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module programme remain confidential, so a sheet comparing that kind of work should expect gaps and record them as gaps rather than estimating around them.

Dates, revisions and response owners

A comparison sheet is a record of a conversation over time, not a snapshot. Three fields keep that record usable.

Quote date. Every row carries the date the figure was received. When a supplier reissues, add a new row rather than overwriting the old one, and mark the old row superseded. Overwriting destroys the audit trail that a later review will ask for.

Reference revision. Where the supplier's quote is tied to a specification revision, record it. Where it is not, record "not stated". A revision label is not the material itself, and a sheet that treats the two as interchangeable will eventually compare a price against the wrong document.

Response owner. Name the person on your side who asked the question and the person at the supplier who answered. This is not bureaucracy; it is how you know who to ask when a figure needs clarifying three weeks later.

A short "open questions" column, one line per row, keeps the unresolved items visible instead of buried in email threads.

Questions to send back before you compare

Before any normalisation, send each supplier the same short list. It is faster than guessing and it makes the answers comparable.

What reference specification and revision is this quote priced against, and what is its date? What is the packaging unit, and how many base units are inside it? What sample quantity is offered, in what unit, and is the sample charged, loaned or consumed in testing? Is the quoted figure valid to a stated date? Who should we contact if a figure needs clarifying?

Ask the same questions in the same order to every supplier. When the answers come back, they drop into the columns already built, and the comparison becomes a matter of reading rather than reconstructing.

For categories where the public information is deliberately limited, ask what can be shared and accept a partial answer. A supplier conversation about that kind of work should be scoped to what is public, not pushed toward figures that are not.

A reusable blank checklist

Copy this into your sheet as a header row. It is a starting structure, not a standard, and you should adapt it to your own categories.

Row fields: category; supplier; reference specification; reference revision; reference date; packaging unit; base units per pack; sample quantity; sample unit; sample terms (charged, loaned or consumed); quoted figure; quoted figure unit; quote date; valid-to date; normalised figure; normalisation basis note; open questions; response owner; status.

Group headings: one per material category, each stating the basis used within the group.

Footer fields: prepared by; date prepared; next review date; list of superseded rows.

Two habits make the checklist work. First, never leave a unit cell blank; write "not stated" instead. Second, never delete a row; mark it superseded and keep it. A comparison sheet that only shows the current state cannot answer the question "why did we choose this?" six months later.

Where this fits in a wider procurement record

A quote comparison is one document in a chain. It sits between the specification you issued and the purchase order you will raise, and it should be readable by someone who joins the team later without a briefing.

Keep it alongside the specification revisions it references, the supplier correspondence it summarises, and any sample records. When a later audit asks how a figure was reached, the chain answers the question without anyone reconstructing it from memory.

San Techno has operated since 2006 across semiconductor and solar materials, specialized power-module designs and biological solutions, and its public pages are a reasonable place to check category descriptions before you draft a request. They are not a price list, and this guide does not present them as one. The comparison sheet remains your record, built from what each supplier actually wrote, with units stated, dates recorded and owners named.
