A Silicon Recovery Collection Manifest for Nonmatching Package Counts

Published by San Techno.

A receiving-desk guide for Singapore procurement and logistics teams who find that the package count on a silicon recovery collection manifest does not match what actually arrived, covering source-record checks, discrepancy logging and escalation.

Why package counts drift on a silicon recovery collection

A collection manifest is written before the material moves. It records what the releasing site believes it is handing over: a number of packages, a description of each, and a reference back to the source record. The receiving count is written after the material arrives. Between those two moments, several ordinary things happen. A package is re-banded, a carton is split across two pallets, a cassette is counted as one unit at dispatch and as a stack at receipt, or a label is transcribed by hand from a source sheet.

None of these is necessarily a loss. The problem is that a mismatch looks identical whether it is a labelling difference, a packing difference or a genuine shortfall. The receiving team's job is not to decide which one it is on the spot. It is to record enough that someone with the source records can decide later.

This matters in silicon recovery because the material is often non-usable silicon collected from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from those sources, so a collection can legitimately contain mixed package types from more than one origin. A manifest built for a single-origin shipment will not describe that well.

The three counts you should keep separate

Most count disputes come from collapsing three different numbers into one column. Keep them apart on the receiving sheet.

Declared count: the number of packages stated on the collection manifest, as issued.

Observed count: the number of packages physically present at the receiving point, counted once, by one named person, at one time.

Reconciled count: the number the releasing and receiving parties agree on after the source records are checked. This number is usually produced days later, not at the dock.

A useful habit is to write the observed count before reading the declared count. If the receiver reads the manifest first, the count tends to drift toward the expected figure. Counting first and comparing second gives you a cleaner record, and it costs nothing.

For wafer-related collections, the unit question is sharper than for boxed goods, because wafers may be counted by cassette, by stack or by individual wafer. Wafer reclaim intake work usually needs the unit named explicitly on every line. If your collection includes wafers rather than packaged scrap, check the relevant resources on the San Techno website for the document pack that applies.

A worked example with fictional quantities

The following is a fictional illustration only. No real shipment, supplier or site is described.

Suppose a collection manifest lists four lines:

Line 1: 12 cartons, packaged scrap, reference SR-001. Line 2: 6 cassettes, wafer material, reference SR-002. Line 3: 3 pallets, mixed packaging material, reference SR-003. Line 4: 1 sealed tote, reference SR-004.

Declared total: 22 packages.

At receipt, the team counts 12 cartons, 6 cassettes, 4 pallets and no sealed tote. Observed total: 22 packages. The totals match, but the composition does not. One pallet has appeared and one tote has not.

A second fictional case: declared 22, observed 20, with two cartons short and everything else present.

These two cases need different handling. In the first, the total is right and the line detail is wrong, which usually points to a packing or labelling change made after the manifest was issued. In the second, the total is wrong, which points to something that did not load, did not arrive, or was counted differently at dispatch.

Writing the case type on the record, composition mismatch or total mismatch, saves the reviewer a step. It also stops the two cases being merged into one vague note that says the count was off.

Checking the source record before you escalate

Before a mismatch goes to a supplier or a counterparty, the receiving team should be able to answer four questions from documents already on file.

First, which source record does each manifest line point to? A line without a reference is a line nobody can check.

Second, was the manifest issued before or after the material was packed? A manifest issued early will often describe an intended packing, not the actual one.

Third, is there a collection note or handover acknowledgement signed at dispatch? If so, its count is the releasing party's position and should be quoted, not paraphrased.

Fourth, do the package labels on arrival match the labels named on the manifest? Label mismatch is the most common benign explanation and the easiest to confirm.

Where the collection involves wafers, the source record may sit with a reclaim programme rather than a scrap programme. A discrepancy log that survives a review

A discrepancy note written at the dock is often unusable a week later because it records the conclusion and not the observation. Use fixed fields instead.

Record the date and time of receipt, the receiving location, and the name of the person who counted. Record the manifest reference and its issue date. Record the declared count and the observed count as separate numbers, with the unit named for each line. Record the case type, composition mismatch or total mismatch. Record which packages are affected by line reference. Record what was physically seen, in plain description, without interpretation. Record which source documents were checked and which were missing. Record who was notified and when.

Leave the cause field blank until someone with the source records fills it in. A blank cause is honest. A guessed cause becomes a fact in the next meeting.

One further field is worth adding: whether the material has been moved from the receiving area since the count. Once material is relocated, the observed count can no longer be re-verified, and the record becomes the only evidence. Note the relocation time even if nothing else changes.

When to escalate, and to whom

Not every mismatch needs the same response. A short set of thresholds, agreed in advance, keeps the receiving team from either over-reporting or sitting on a problem.

A composition mismatch with a matching total can usually be resolved by the receiving supervisor against the packing documents, without involving the counterparty.

A total mismatch of one or two packages should be notified to the counterparty the same working day, with the discrepancy log attached, while the loading area can still be checked.

A total mismatch that also involves a missing sealed package, or any case where labels do not match the manifest at all, should go to procurement as well as the counterparty, because it may affect the commercial record rather than only the physical one.

A mismatch that cannot be explained after the source records are checked should be escalated with the full document set rather than a summary, so the reviewer sees the same evidence the receiver saw.

These thresholds are an author heuristic for structuring a response, not an industry standard. Set your own with your counterparty and write them into the collection procedure so the receiving team is not improvising at the dock.

Where this fits in the wider materials relationship

Count reconciliation is administrative work, but it sits inside a materials relationship that has a longer horizon. Material that is non-usable in one process may still have a route back into the value chain, and the collection record is the first document in that route. San Techno's silicon recovery work channels non-usable silicon from fabs, wafer makers, cell producers and packaging facilities back into the value chain, and recovered material can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. A clean collection manifest is what makes that chain auditable at the front end.

San Techno Pte Ltd is a Singapore-based company incorporated in 2006, and it began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials. That history is useful context for procurement teams comparing a collection counterparty: the same organisation may appear on both the supply side and the recovery side of a materials file, and the document trail should be consistent across both.

For receiving teams, the practical takeaway is narrow. Count before you read the manifest, keep declared and observed counts separate, name the unit on every line, and write the case type before you write the cause. Those four habits will not eliminate mismatches, but they will make each one resolvable from documents rather than from memory.
