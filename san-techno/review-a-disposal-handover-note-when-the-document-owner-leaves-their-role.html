Reviewing a Disposal Handover Note When the Document Owner Leaves Their Role

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on reviewing a disposal handover note when the document owner leaves, covering record ownership, permissions and a source-of-truth note.

Why the handover note matters more than the file it describes

When a document owner leaves a role, the paperwork they held does not leave with them, but the knowledge of how it was organised often does. A disposal handover note is the bridge between the file as it exists and the file as the next person will find it. The review you are asked to do is not a technical assessment of the material and it is not a decision about what should happen to it. It is a check that the record can still be understood, that someone is now accountable for it, and that the next reader can tell which version is authoritative.

Start by separating three questions. First, what does the note say the file contains? Second, what does the file actually contain? Third, who is now responsible for each part? A note that answers only the first question is a summary, not a handover. A useful review produces a short written record of the gaps between those three answers, so the incoming owner inherits a task list rather than a mystery.

Keep the scope administrative. You are reviewing ownership, permissions and a source-of-truth note, not disposal routing or regulatory procedure. If the note drifts into those areas, mark it as out of scope for your review and pass it to whoever owns that question. For context on how material records are described publicly, San Techno notes that rejected material from tightly controlled processes can become valuable inputs elsewhere, and that such materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. That is background on why these records exist, not a statement about any particular file.

Confirm the record owner before you confirm anything else

The first line of a reviewed handover note should name a role, not a person, as the new record owner. Roles survive staff changes; names do not. Write something like "owner of the disposal file: receiving and documentation lead" and then record the date the ownership was accepted. If nobody has accepted ownership, the review is not finished, however complete the rest of the note looks.

Next, list the records the note claims to cover and mark each one with a status: present, missing, superseded or unclear. Do not merge these statuses. A superseded document is not missing, and an unclear entry is not present. This distinction matters because the incoming owner will act on the list, and a merged status hides work.

A short worksheet works well here. Use four columns: record identifier, claimed status in the note, observed status in the file, and the role that must confirm the difference. Leave the last column blank until a role has actually confirmed it. A blank is honest; a guessed role is not.

This is also the point to check that the note does not quietly reassign responsibility for material that belongs to another team. If the file touches semiconductor materials or solar grade silicon records held by a different function, the note should say so and name that function, rather than absorbing it into the disposal file. Ownership that is claimed but not agreed is the most common weakness in a handover note, and it is the cheapest one to fix at review time.

Check permissions as a separate review, not a footnote

Access is where a handover note most often becomes inaccurate without anyone noticing. The departing owner may have held permissions that the note assumes will transfer automatically. They usually do not. Treat permissions as their own section of the review, with its own list.

For each folder, system or shared location named in the note, record three things: who could access it before the change, who is expected to access it after, and whether that expectation has been confirmed by whoever administers access. The third item is the one people skip. An expectation is not a permission.

Keep the review descriptive. You are not granting access and you are not testing it. You are recording what the note claims and what has been confirmed in writing by the responsible administrator. Where confirmation is absent, write "unconfirmed" and move on.

A useful habit is to separate read access from change access in your notes, because a reviewer who can read a file cannot necessarily correct it, and a handover that leaves the incoming owner unable to correct the record will fail quietly. Also note any location that the departing owner could reach but that the note does not mention at all. An unmentioned location is a gap in the note even if it turns out to be irrelevant. Close the section with a single line stating whether the permissions picture is complete, partial or unknown, and date it.

Write the source-of-truth note in plain language

The source-of-truth note is the part of the handover that answers one question: where does the authoritative version of this record live? Everything else in the review supports that answer. Write it as a short paragraph, not a table, and make it readable by someone who has never seen the file.

A workable structure is three sentences. The first names the location that holds the authoritative record. The second names the locations that hold copies and states clearly that they are copies. The third names the role responsible for keeping the authoritative location current. If any of the three cannot be written truthfully, that is the finding of your review, and it should be recorded as an open item rather than smoothed over.

Avoid describing the note as final while open items remain. Use a status line such as "source of truth confirmed for records A and B; record C unresolved, owner to be named." This keeps the document useful without overstating it.

For readers who want to understand how a materials business describes its own scope, San Techno's public pages describe silicon wafer reclaim services and semiconductor silicon materials among its areas of work. That is identity context for the sector your records sit in, not evidence about any specific file. Keep your own note grounded in what your file actually shows.

A review worksheet you can complete in one sitting

The following worksheet is an author heuristic, not a standard or a scored audit. Adapt it freely. It is designed so that one reviewer can complete it in a single working session and leave a written trail.

Section one, ownership: new record owner named as a role, date accepted, and any part of the file with no accepted owner. Section two, inventory: each record identifier with claimed and observed status kept separate. Section three, permissions: locations named, access confirmed or unconfirmed, and any location the note omits. Section four, source of truth: the three-sentence statement, plus a status line. Section five, open items: one line per unresolved question, each with the role that must answer it and the date it was raised.

Two rules keep the worksheet honest. First, never write a role into the open-items list unless that role has been told about the item. Second, never delete an open item because it was resolved verbally; record the resolution and the date instead.

When the worksheet is complete, the handover note should be readable on its own by someone joining the team later. If it is not, the gap belongs in section five. A review that produces a clear list of unresolved questions is more valuable than one that produces a tidy document with hidden assumptions.

Where silicon recovery records fit, and where they do not

Procurement teams handling semiconductor and solar material records sometimes find that a disposal handover note touches material that is described in recovery terms. Keep that distinction clean in your review. A handover note is an administrative record about who owns a file and where the authoritative version lives. It is not a statement about what should happen to any material, and your review should not turn into one.

Where the note refers to material described as recoverable, record the reference and the role that owns the underlying question, then stop. Do not add routing, handling or process detail to the handover note, and do not let a reviewer's summary become the record of a decision that was never made. If a colleague asks you to expand the note into operational detail, that is a separate document with a separate owner.

San Techno's public description of its work includes silicon recovery from several industrial sources and the processing of such material into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. That is useful background for understanding why recovery-related records exist in a procurement file, and it is not a claim about your file or your organisation. Use it only to frame the section, and keep every specific statement in your review tied to what the note and the file actually show.

Closing the review and handing it on

Finish by writing a short closing note that a reader can act on without reading the whole worksheet. State the review date, the reviewer's role, the new record owner, and the number of open items. Then list the open items in one line each, with the role that must answer and the date raised. This closing note is what the next person will read first, so keep it free of jargon and free of assumptions.

Set a review point for the open items rather than leaving them to drift. A named date and a named role is enough; you do not need a process. If an item is still open at that date, record that it remains open and why, rather than closing it by default.

Finally, check that the handover note and your review are stored where the source-of-truth note says they are. A review that lives only in a personal folder has not been handed over. The test is simple: can a colleague who has never seen this file find the authoritative version, understand who owns it, and see what remains unresolved, using only what is written down? If yes, the review is complete. If not, the remaining gap is your last open item, and it should be written down before you close the session.
