Organize Procurement Questions Before an Electronic Approval Meeting

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams sourcing semiconductor and solar materials: build a neutral agenda that separates confirmed specifications, commercial assumptions, the decision owner and unresolved information before an electronic approval meeting.

Why the agenda matters more than the meeting

An electronic approval meeting is a decision event, not a discovery session. By the time the approvers join the call or open the shared file, the questions they are being asked to answer should already be written down, grouped and assigned. When they are not, the meeting drifts into a live reading of documents, and the decision either slips to a later date or gets made on incomplete information.

The practical problem is that a procurement file for semiconductor materials rarely has one author. Engineering contributes specification language, the buyer contributes commercial terms, the receiving desk contributes document expectations, and the supplier contributes its own paperwork. Each of these arrives in a different format and at a different time. The agenda is the one place where they are forced into a common shape before anyone is asked to approve anything.

This guide is about building that agenda. It does not tell you what to buy or which supplier to choose. It gives you a structure for separating what is confirmed from what is assumed, naming who decides, and listing what is still open, so that the approval meeting can do its actual job. San Techno Pte Ltd, a Singapore-based company working in semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development, and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, is one example of a supplier whose product range spans several of these categories, which is exactly why a single file can contain several kinds of question at once.

The four-column agenda: confirmed, assumed, decided, open

The core of the method is a four-column table. Every question that would otherwise be asked aloud in the meeting is written into one of the four columns first.

Confirmed specifications. These are statements backed by a document you can point to: a supplier specification with a revision number, a drawing, a certificate, a signed acknowledgement. The test is simple. If you cannot name the document and its revision, the item is not confirmed. It belongs in another column.

Commercial assumptions. These are the things the team believes but has not yet had confirmed in writing: that a quoted validity period still applies, that a delivery window is still achievable, that a quantity band still holds. Assumptions are legitimate working material, but they must be labelled as assumptions so that an approver is not misled into treating them as settled.

Decision owner. Every question needs one named role, not a department. "Procurement" is not an owner; "the buyer who raised the request" is. If two roles share a question, split it into two questions.

Unresolved information. This is the column that protects the meeting. Anything that is genuinely unknown, contradictory or awaiting a reply goes here, with the date it was raised and the date a response is expected. An approval meeting that ends with a clear list of unresolved items is more useful than one that ends with a decision resting on a guess.

A worked fictional example: a buyer is preparing a file for a lot of solar grade silicon. The confirmed column holds the specification revision and the supplier's written acknowledgement. The assumed column holds the belief that the quoted validity period covers the planned order date. The owner column names the buyer for the commercial question and the quality lead for the specification question. The open column records that a revised certificate has been requested and not yet received. Nothing in that table claims anything about the supplier's capability; it only records what the file can and cannot support.

Turning product categories into separate question sets

A common failure is to write one agenda for a mixed basket of items. Silicon materials, reclaimed wafers and power modules raise different questions, and mixing them lets a strong answer on one item create a false sense of comfort about another.

For semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon, the questions tend to cluster around grade description, quantity basis and document identity. The agenda should ask which document defines the grade, whether the quantity is stated on the same basis throughout the file, and which revision is current.

For silicon recovery and wafer reclaim, the questions tend to cluster around what is being collected, what record accompanies it, and who confirms the record on the receiving side. These are documentation and handover questions rather than technical ones, and they belong in the agenda as such.

For IGBT power modules and double-sided cooling work, the questions are more likely to be about which documents the supplier has actually issued and which remain internal. It is worth noting that San Techno describes detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes of its power-module programme as confidential, so an agenda that asks for those details in an approval meeting is asking for something that will not be provided. The better question is what public or issued documentation exists and what the approver is expected to rely on.

For AC-to-DC power module design changes, the agenda question is narrower: what changed, which documents were reissued, and what purchasing must confirm before the next order. That is a documentation question, not a design review.

Grouping the agenda by product category, rather than by supplier, keeps each question attached to the item it actually concerns.

Writing questions an approver can actually answer

Approvers are asked to decide, not to investigate. A question phrased as "is the supplier acceptable?" invites either a rubber stamp or an indefinite deferral. A question phrased as "does the file contain a current specification revision and a written acknowledgement for this lot?" can be answered yes or no from the documents in front of the approver.

Three habits make questions answerable.

First, make each question binary or bounded. "Which revision is current?" is bounded. "Are we comfortable with the quality?" is not.

Second, attach the evidence reference to the question. The approver should be able to see which document the question rests on without leaving the agenda. If the evidence is missing, the question moves to the unresolved column.

Third, separate the question from the recommendation. A buyer may recommend proceeding, but the recommendation should sit beside the question, not replace it. When the two are merged, the approver cannot tell whether they are approving a fact or an opinion.

A short format helps here. Each line can read: question, evidence reference, owner, status. Four fields, one line, no narrative. The narrative belongs in the covering note, not in the table the approver is scanning.

This is also where a controlled vocabulary pays off. If the file uses "grade" in three different senses across three documents, the agenda should say which sense it means, or the question is unanswerable.

A pre-meeting checklist with a simple readiness score

Before the electronic approval meeting is opened, run a short checklist. The scoring below is an author heuristic for internal use, not a validated instrument, and the thresholds are examples that a team should adjust to its own risk appetite.

One point each: every confirmed specification names a document and revision; every commercial assumption is labelled as an assumption; every question has exactly one named role as owner; every unresolved item has a date raised and a date expected; the agenda is grouped by product category rather than by supplier; no question asks for information the supplier has already marked confidential; the covering note states what decision is being requested.

A file scoring seven out of seven is ready to schedule. A file scoring five or six can proceed if the missing points are all in the unresolved column and the approvers have been told. A file scoring four or below is usually better served by a short working session to close the gaps than by an approval meeting that will end without a decision.

Two checklist items deserve emphasis because they are commonly skipped. The first is the covering note: an agenda without a stated decision request leaves approvers guessing what they are being asked to do. The second is the confidentiality check. Where a supplier has drawn a boundary around certain information, the agenda should record that boundary rather than route around it.

Where the agenda hands off to other records

The approval agenda is one document in a chain, and it works best when it points to the others rather than duplicating them.

When the decision concerns a first delivery, the agenda should reference the document checklist for that delivery rather than restate it. When the decision concerns a collection, the agenda should reference the pickup readiness record. When the decision concerns a specification revision, the agenda should reference the comparison record that shows what changed. The agenda's job is to ask the decision question and point at the evidence; the evidence lives in its own controlled record.

This separation matters for audit. If the agenda restates a specification, and the specification is later revised, the agenda becomes a stale copy of a document it was never meant to own. A reference to a document identifier and revision does not go stale in the same way, because it points at the controlled source.

It also matters for the meeting itself. Approvers who are handed a short agenda with references can read the underlying documents if they choose. Approvers who are handed a long agenda that paraphrases everything tend to read only the agenda, which is the weaker position.

For teams that want to see how a supplier presents its own product categories before drafting questions, the published product pages are a reasonable starting point for vocabulary, though they are supplier statements rather than independent verification.

After the meeting: closing the loop on open items

The meeting produces a decision and a list of open items. The open items are the part that most often gets lost, so give them an owner and a review date before the meeting closes.

A simple closing routine works. Re-read the unresolved column aloud and confirm each item still has an owner and an expected date. Record the decision against the specific questions it answers, not against the file as a whole. Note any assumption that the decision depended on, so that if the assumption later fails, the decision can be revisited rather than silently invalidated.

Then file the agenda with its evidence references intact. A future reader should be able to reconstruct why the decision was made without needing to have been in the meeting. That is the real test of a well-organized approval file: not whether the meeting was short, but whether the reasoning survives the meeting.

San Techno's own materials describe a business that has operated since 2006 across semiconductor and solar materials, power-module design and biological solutions, which is a reminder that a single supplier relationship can generate several distinct documentation streams. Keeping each stream's questions in its own agenda, with its own evidence references, is what keeps the approval record readable months later.
