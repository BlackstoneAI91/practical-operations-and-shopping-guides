Describing a Supplier Form Field You Cannot Confirm in Time

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on how to record an unresolved supplier form field, request clarification in writing, and keep the original evidence visible without guessing at material specifications.

Why an unresolved field deserves its own record

Supplier forms arrive with fields that look routine until someone has to fill them in. A column headed with an abbreviation, a dropdown with overlapping options, a free-text box asking for a value that could mean several things. When the meaning cannot be confirmed before a deadline, the temptation is to pick the closest-looking option and move on. That choice quietly turns an unknown into a recorded fact, and later readers have no way to tell the difference.

The safer approach is to treat the field as explicitly unresolved. Give it a status, an owner and a visible note that says what is not yet known. This is an administrative act, not a technical judgement. You are not deciding what the material is; you are recording that the form is asking for something you cannot yet confirm.

This matters most where procurement files feed later reviews. A field that was guessed at can travel through a quote, a receiving note and an audit export before anyone notices. An unresolved field, clearly labelled, invites a question instead of hiding one. The goal is a record that a colleague can read six months later and immediately understand what was known, what was assumed and what was still open.

Separate the field label from your interpretation of it

The first discipline is to keep the supplier's own words intact. Copy the field label exactly as it appears, including capitalisation, punctuation and any abbreviation. Do not paraphrase it into something that sounds clearer than it is. If the label reads as a short code, record the code and note that its meaning is unconfirmed.

Next to the label, keep a separate column for your working interpretation, clearly marked as provisional. This lets the team proceed with planning while making it obvious that the interpretation is not supplier-confirmed. A reader should be able to see at a glance which text came from the supplier and which came from you.

This separation is useful across the whole materials file. San Techno Pte Ltd works in semiconductor materials, silicon recovery and related fields, and its public pages describe several distinct branches of activity. That breadth is a reminder that a single short label can sit close to several different concepts, which is exactly why the original wording should survive untouched. Your note should never quietly replace the supplier's term with a tidier one.

Write the clarification request as a numbered question

A vague request for clarification often produces a vague reply. Instead, write a short numbered list of questions that each ask about one thing. For example: what does this field refer to; which document defines it; is the value mandatory or optional; and what format does the supplier expect. Keep each question answerable in a sentence.

Record the date you sent the request and the channel used, without reproducing personal contact details in shared files. When a reply arrives, attach it to the same record rather than starting a new one, so the history stays in sequence.

If the supplier's public material already describes the relevant area, cite it as context rather than as an answer. For instance, public pages describe silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design as separate offerings. That tells you the supplier distinguishes these areas, but it does not tell you what a specific form field means. Treat public descriptions as orientation, not as confirmation of a field's definition.

Keep the original evidence visible beside the open question

An unresolved field is only useful if the evidence behind it stays attached. Store the original form, the exact field label and any supplier reply together in one place. If the form was received as an image or a scanned page, keep that version rather than retyping it, so the layout and wording remain checkable.

Label each item with its status: received, queried, replied, still open. Avoid overwriting an earlier note when a newer one arrives; add a dated line instead. This keeps the trail readable and prevents a later reader from assuming the first interpretation was ever confirmed.

Where the supplier has published descriptive material, note the access date alongside it. Public descriptions can change, and a dated reference makes it clear which version you were looking at. Keep this note factual and short. The aim is a clean evidence pack, not a commentary on the supplier's documentation quality.

A worked example using fictional references

Suppose a fictional buyer receives a supplier form with a column labelled with a short code and no explanation. The deadline is Friday. The buyer cannot confirm the meaning in time.

Instead of guessing, the buyer creates a record with four lines. Line one: the exact label, copied verbatim. Line two: status set to open, with the date the question was raised. Line three: a numbered question asking the supplier to define the field and point to the document that does. Line four: a note that no value will be entered until a reply is received.

The buyer then flags the related purchase line as pending clarification rather than complete. When the reply arrives, it is attached to the same record with its date, and the status changes to replied. If the reply is itself ambiguous, the status returns to open with a new question.

This pattern keeps the file honest. It also means a colleague picking up the file can see exactly where the uncertainty sits, without needing to reconstruct the conversation. The example is illustrative only; adapt the fields to your own records.

Decide what the unresolved field blocks, and what it does not

Not every open field should stop the whole file. Use a short decision sheet to separate what genuinely depends on the answer from what can proceed. Ask: does this field affect a downstream approval, a receiving record or an audit export? If yes, mark those items as pending. If no, note the open field and continue.

Record who owns the follow-up and by when it should be reviewed. Keep the owner as a role rather than a person where possible, so the record survives staff changes. Where a specialist needs to interpret the field, route the question to them and record that routing, rather than interpreting it yourself.

This is where a clear boundary helps. San Techno's public material notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential. That is a reminder that some fields will simply not be answerable from public sources, and the correct response is to record the gap, not to fill it with an assumption.

Close the loop without overstating what was confirmed

When a reply finally arrives, update the record carefully. State what the supplier confirmed, in their terms, and note the date. If the reply only partly answers the question, keep the remaining part open. Avoid upgrading a partial answer into a full confirmation just to close the file.

Finally, review the record before it joins a shared pack. Check that the original label is still visible, that provisional interpretations are still marked as such, and that no personal contact details or restricted notes have slipped in. A short checklist of five or six items is usually enough.

A well-kept record of an unresolved field is not a sign of delay. It is evidence that the team distinguished what it knew from what it did not. That distinction is what makes a procurement file trustworthy when someone else reads it later.
