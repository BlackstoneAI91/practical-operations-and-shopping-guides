Wafer Reclaim Intake Document Checklist for a Procurement Audit

Published by San Techno.

A practical six-section reference for Singapore procurement and operations teams assembling an intake document pack for a wafer reclaim audit, with a worked hypothetical example, a reusable blank checklist and guidance on where each related destination fits.

What a reclaim intake pack is for, and what it is not

A wafer reclaim intake document pack exists to answer a narrow question at audit time: when material left your site for reclaim, what did you agree it was, who took custody, and what evidence supports the outcome recorded in your books? It is not a technical qualification dossier for the reclaim process itself, and it is not a substitute for your own quality or finance sign-off. Treat it as the procurement-side evidence trail that sits alongside those records.

Three principles keep the pack useful. First, every document should be traceable to a specific lot, date and counterparty. Second, the pack should distinguish what you asserted (declared material description, quantities, condition) from what the counterparty confirmed (receipt, acceptance, disposition). Third, the pack should be assembled before material moves, not reconstructed afterwards, because recollection is the weakest form of audit evidence.

For context on who does this work in Singapore, San Techno Pte Ltd is a Singapore-based company working in semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development, and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand. Its silicon recovery activity covers non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. That describes the service context; it does not tell you what your own pack must contain, which depends on your internal controls and your auditor's expectations.

Section 1: Identity and scope documents

Start with the documents that establish who is involved and what is in scope. A typical set includes the purchase or service order referencing reclaim, the signed scope note listing material categories and quantities, and the counterparty's business identity details as held in your vendor master. If the arrangement sits under a framework agreement, cite the clause that covers material handling rather than attaching the whole contract.

A worked hypothetical, used only to illustrate structure: a Singapore operations team prepares a reclaim intake for 1,200 wafers drawn from two process areas. Their scope note lists wafer diameter, approximate thickness range, cassette counts and the declared condition category for each group. The note is signed by an operations lead and countersigned by the counterparty before pickup. Nothing in that example is a real customer case, and your own scope note will reflect your material and your controls.

Keep scope documents short and specific. A one-page scope note that names quantities and condition categories is easier to audit than a long narrative that mixes scope with expectations about outcomes.

Section 2: Material description and condition records

This is where most audit questions land. The pack should record what the material was declared to be, in terms your own systems already use, and what condition it was in at handover. Useful items include a lot-level inventory extract, a condition classification consistent with your internal definitions, and any non-conformance notes raised before the material was set aside for reclaim.

Be careful to separate description from expectation. A record that says "declared as non-usable wafers from process area B" is a description. A record that says "expected to yield reusable material" is an expectation, and it belongs in a different section with its own evidence. Mixing the two is a common reason an auditor asks for clarification.

Where material is destined for silicon recovery rather than direct reuse, the description should still stand on its own. San Techno's recovery work takes in non-usable silicon, and recovered materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending their useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss. That is a statement about the service's purpose, not a guarantee about any particular lot's yield, which your own records must establish.

Section 3: Custody, collection and chain-of-custody records

Custody documents answer who held the material and when. At minimum, the pack should contain a pickup or collection record with date, location, quantity and the names of the people handing over and receiving, plus any transport reference your logistics provider issues. If collection is split across more than one date, each movement needs its own record rather than a single summary line.

A practical habit is to number custody events sequentially and reference that number in every later document, including the receipt confirmation and the disposition record. This makes the trail readable months later without needing to reconstruct the sequence from emails.

For teams arranging the physical movement of material, the collection step is where paperwork most often lags the truck. Build the collection record into the same workflow as the scope note so the two cannot drift apart.

Section 4: Receipt, acceptance and disposition evidence

The pack is incomplete without the counterparty's side of the story. Ask for a receipt confirmation that references your lot identifiers and quantities, an acceptance or rejection note with reasons, and a disposition record stating what happened to the material. If the disposition involves recovery, the record should say so in terms that match your own reporting categories.

Where material is routed toward disposal rather than recovery, the disposition evidence should reflect that route explicitly, with the same lot-level traceability. The distinction matters at audit because recovery and disposal usually sit under different internal approvals and different reporting lines.

Keep acceptance criteria out of this section and in your quality records. The intake pack's job is to show that acceptance happened and what was decided, not to re-argue the technical standard.

Section 5: A reusable blank checklist

The following is an author's checklist structure, offered as a starting point rather than an established standard. Adapt the items to your own controls and confirm them with your auditor.

Identity and scope: service order or purchase order reference; signed scope note with material categories and quantities; vendor master extract; contract clause reference if applicable.

Material description: lot-level inventory extract; condition classification; pre-existing non-conformance notes; clear separation of description from expectation.

Custody: sequential custody event numbers; pickup records with names and dates; transport references; split-collection records where applicable.

Receipt and disposition: receipt confirmation referencing lot identifiers; acceptance or rejection note with reasons; disposition record; route stated as recovery or disposal.

Reconciliation: quantity reconciliation between declared, collected and received; variance explanation where figures differ; sign-off by the responsible owner.

A simple scoring approach, again an author heuristic rather than research: mark each line as present, present but incomplete, or absent, and treat any absent line in the custody and reconciliation groups as a blocker before the pack is filed.

Section 6: Common gaps and how to close them

Three gaps recur in practice, and each has a straightforward fix. The first is quantity drift: declared, collected and received figures differ without explanation. Close it by requiring a variance note whenever figures do not match, signed by the person who owns the reconciliation.

The second is identifier mismatch: your lot numbers and the counterparty's references do not map to each other. Close it with a simple cross-reference table attached to the scope note, agreed before pickup.

The third is timing: documents are assembled after the material has moved. Close it by making the scope note and custody record prerequisites for collection rather than follow-ups.

Use the checklist to identify which intake records are still missing for the proposed trial. Keep the conversation about your material and your documentation needs; the pack itself remains your organisation's record, and its adequacy depends on your controls and your auditor's requirements rather than on any single supplier's involvement.
