Planning a Documentation Update When a Supplier Adds a New Catalogue Category

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on comparing public catalogue headings, recording eligibility for future review and keeping old records distinct when a supplier adds a new category.

Start with the public heading, not the assumption

A new catalogue category is a filing event before it is a buying event. The supplier has chosen a new public heading, and your job is to record that heading accurately without deciding what it means for your existing files. Begin by capturing the exact wording of the new category as published, the date you saw it and the page or document where it appeared. Keep that capture separate from any internal material name you already use.

The temptation is to map the new heading straight onto an existing folder. Resist that for the first pass. A heading is a label chosen by the seller; it does not confirm that the items inside match anything you have bought before, and it does not confirm availability in Singapore. Write a short note that says what the supplier published and nothing more.

This is where a materials distributor's public structure can help as a reference point. San Techno Pte Ltd, incorporated in 2006, began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, and its public pages group offerings under headings such as semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design. Those headings show how one supplier organises its public catalogue; they are not a template for how your file should be arranged, and they say nothing about whether a new category overlaps with them. Use them only to see the shape of a public taxonomy, then return to the supplier's own wording for the record you are building.

Build a comparison sheet for old and new headings

The core working document is a two-column comparison sheet. On the left, list the headings already in your procurement file, with the date each was first recorded and the document that introduced it. On the right, place the new category exactly as published. Between them, add three narrow columns: wording overlap, open questions and status.

Wording overlap is a plain observation, not a judgement. If the new heading shares a word with an old one, note it. If it shares none, note that too. Open questions are the things you cannot answer from the public page: does the supplier intend this category to replace an older one, does it apply to the Singapore market, and which document would confirm the contents. Status should stay at pending until someone with the right authority reviews it.

A worked example, entirely fictional: a buyer sees a new heading called reclaimed substrate blanks. The existing file has a heading called wafer reclaim services. The overlap column records the shared word reclaim. The open questions column asks whether the new heading covers the same activity or a different one. The status stays pending. Nothing in the sheet claims the two are equivalent, and nothing suggests the supplier offers either in Singapore. That restraint is the point of the sheet: it holds the comparison open long enough for a proper review rather than closing it with a guess.

Note eligibility for a future review without deciding it

A new category often arrives with an implicit question: should this go into our next review cycle? Recording eligibility is different from granting it. Eligibility means you have written down the conditions under which the category would be considered, and who would consider it. It does not mean the category has passed anything.

Use a short eligibility note with four lines. First, the public source and the date accessed. Second, the reason the category might be relevant to your team, phrased as a question rather than a conclusion. Third, the role that would need to look at it, described by function rather than by name. Fourth, the trigger that would move it from pending to reviewed, such as a published specification document or a written confirmation from the supplier.

Keep this note in the same folder as the comparison sheet so the two travel together. If your team later asks why a category was or was not reviewed, the note answers the question without anyone reconstructing the reasoning from memory. Avoid words like approved, qualified or accepted in this note; they belong to a different stage with different owners. The eligibility note is an administrative marker, and it should read like one.

Keep the old record visibly distinct

The most common documentation failure in this situation is quiet merging. Someone adds the new category to an existing folder, the old heading disappears into a subfolder, and six months later nobody can tell which record came first. Prevent that by giving the old record a visible status line at the top of its own file: current, superseded or under review, with the date the status was set.

Do not delete or rename the old record when the new category appears. The old record documents what you held and when. If the supplier's public pages have changed, note the change alongside the old record rather than overwriting it. A simple line such as public heading observed on a given date, previous wording retained below does the job.

Where the supplier publishes a broader description of its work, keep that separate from your category record. San Techno, for example, describes itself as working across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialised power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand. That breadth is useful context for understanding a supplier's public identity, but it is not evidence that a specific new category belongs to any of those areas. Your old record stays about your old record; the supplier's public description stays where it was published.

Assign owners and a review date, not a verdict

Every documentation update needs a named owner and a review date, and neither should imply a decision has been made. The owner is the person who keeps the comparison sheet current and chases the open questions. The review date is when the team looks again, whether or not the supplier has published anything new.

Set the review date by working backwards from your own procurement calendar rather than from the supplier's announcements. If your next category review is in a set month, place this item on that agenda with its status clearly marked as pending. If no review is scheduled, create a placeholder date and label it as provisional.

A short owner log helps here. Record the role, the date the item was assigned, the last action taken and the next action due. Keep names out of shared documents where your organisation's practice is to use roles. When the review date arrives, the team either closes the item with a recorded reason or moves it forward with a new date. Either outcome is fine; what matters is that the file shows a decision was taken deliberately rather than inherited by default.

Write the handover note for the next person

Documentation updates outlive the person who started them. A handover note of a few paragraphs saves the next owner from re-reading everything. It should say what the new category is, where the public source sits, what the comparison sheet concluded so far, which questions remain open and who currently owns the item.

Keep the note factual and short. Avoid describing the supplier's technical capabilities unless a public document states them and you have cited it. Where a supplier's public material does make a specific claim, quote the claim's scope rather than extending it. San Techno's public pages, for instance, describe an intelligent IGBT power-module concept centred on double-sided cooling for an EconoDUAL 3-class package format, and note that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential. A handover note can mention that such a public description exists; it should not treat it as a specification for anything you are buying.

Finish the note with a single line stating the current status and the next review date. That line is what most readers will look for first, and it keeps the file honest about where the update actually stands.

A short worksheet to run the update

Use this worksheet as a working checklist. It is an author's suggested structure, not an established method, and you should adapt the fields to your own filing practice.

One: capture the new public heading verbatim, with source and date accessed. Two: list the existing headings in the affected folder, with the date each was recorded. Three: fill the comparison sheet columns for wording overlap, open questions and status. Four: write the eligibility note with source, relevance question, reviewing role and trigger. Five: set the old record's status line and confirm nothing was deleted. Six: assign an owner and a provisional review date. Seven: draft the handover note and confirm the closing status line.

Score nothing and promise nothing. The worksheet is complete when every field has an entry, even if the entry says not yet known. A file that records its own gaps is more useful than one that looks finished because the gaps were left blank. When the review date arrives, the team can read the worksheet, see exactly what was and was not established, and decide the next step with the evidence in front of them.
