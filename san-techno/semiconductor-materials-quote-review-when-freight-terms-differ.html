Quote Review Worksheet for Semiconductor Materials When Freight Terms Differ

Published by San Techno.

A line-by-line worksheet that converts each semiconductor materials quote to a landed cost basis so Singapore procurement teams can compare offers with different freight and delivery terms before selecting a supplier.

Why freight terms break a simple quote comparison

When you collect quotes for semiconductor materials, the headline number on each page rarely describes the same thing. One supplier may quote material only, with freight billed later. Another may bundle delivery to your Singapore site. A third may quote a delivered price but leave duties, handling or insurance to be confirmed. Put those three numbers side by side and the cheapest line may not be the cheapest outcome.

The fix is not a cleverer spreadsheet formula. It is a worksheet that forces every quote onto one common basis before anyone ranks it. That basis is landed cost: the amount you would actually commit to receive the material at your named location, under a stated set of responsibilities.

This guide is written for procurement teams sourcing, receiving and documenting semiconductor and solar materials in Singapore. It assumes you already have quotes in hand and need a repeatable way to normalise them. It does not tell you what freight should cost, and it does not rank suppliers. It gives you columns, questions and a decision rule so the comparison you present to approvers is defensible.

One useful piece of context: San Techno has worked in this space since its incorporation in 2006, when it began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials. That history is background for who you may be quoting, not evidence about any particular offer.

Set the worksheet header before you open any quote

Every comparison fails or succeeds on the header. Before you read a single quote, fix the following on one row at the top of the worksheet:

Reference specification. The exact material, grade and form you asked for. If two quotes describe different grades, they are not comparable and should sit in separate worksheets.

Named delivery location. Your receiving dock, warehouse or store, written in full. A quote delivered to a port is not the same as a quote delivered to your site.

Required delivery window. The date range you can actually accept. A quote that cannot meet the window is a different decision, not a cheaper one.

Currency and exchange reference date. State the date you will use to convert any foreign-currency line, and use the same date for every quote.

Responsibility basis. The single set of terms you will convert every quote to. Most teams pick delivered-to-site, because that is the point at which the material is in their control.

Quote validity date. The date each offer expires. A quote that lapses before your approval meeting is a risk line, not a price line.

Write these once and lock them. If a supplier's quote cannot be expressed against this header, mark it incomplete rather than guessing.

The landed cost worksheet, line by line

Build the worksheet with one column per quote and the following rows. Each row is a question you answer from the quote text, not an assumption.

Row 1, material price. The base price for the stated quantity and unit. Record the unit exactly as quoted, because units differ between offers.

Row 2, freight and carriage. Any charge to move the material to your named location. If the quote says freight is excluded, leave this row blank and flag it, do not estimate silently.

Row 3, insurance. Whether transit insurance is included, excluded or optional. Record which.

Row 4, duties and taxes. What the quote says about import charges and any tax treatment. Record the wording, not your interpretation of it.

Row 5, handling and documentation fees. Any charge for paperwork, customs brokerage or terminal handling that the quote names.

Row 6, packaging and pallet charges. Any charge tied to how the material is packed or returned.

Row 7, minimum order or surcharge lines. Any condition that changes the price at your quantity.

Row 8, exclusions. A free-text row listing everything the quote explicitly leaves out. This row is often the most valuable on the page.

Row 9, landed cost subtotal. The sum of rows 1 to 7, with row 8 carried forward as a caveat rather than a number.

Row 10, confidence flag. Mark each quote as complete, partially complete or incomplete based on how many rows you could fill from the quote text alone.

Converting each quote to one responsibility basis

The worksheet only works if every quote is expressed against the same responsibility basis. Take the basis you locked in the header and adjust each quote toward it using only what the quote states.

If a quote excludes freight, the landed cost is unknown until you have a freight figure. Record it as unknown and request the figure in writing. Do not borrow a freight number from another supplier's quote, because the material, weight and route may differ.

If a quote includes delivery but excludes duties, the landed cost is partially known. Mark the duties row as open and note who is expected to bear it.

If a quote is silent on a cost category, treat silence as unknown, not as zero. A blank row that everyone reads as zero is how a comparison quietly becomes wrong.

Where a supplier offers more than one delivery option, record each as a separate column so the options are visible side by side rather than buried in an email thread.

Keep a short note column for the source of each figure: quote page, line or email reference. When an approver asks where a number came from, the note column answers in seconds.

A worked hypothetical: three quotes, three freight positions

The following example is fictional and exists only to show how the worksheet behaves. No figures are real, and nothing here describes any actual supplier.

Suppose you request the same reference specification from three suppliers. Supplier A quotes material only and states freight is excluded. Supplier B quotes a delivered price to your site. Supplier C quotes material plus freight but leaves duties and handling open.

On the worksheet, Supplier A's landed cost subtotal is incomplete because row 2 is blank. Supplier B's subtotal is complete against your basis. Supplier C's subtotal is partial, with rows 4 and 5 flagged open.

Ranking now changes shape. Supplier A cannot be ranked until freight is confirmed, so it moves to a follow-up list rather than a shortlist. Supplier B can be ranked immediately. Supplier C sits in a middle state: comparable once two figures arrive.

The decision rule is simple. Only quotes with a complete landed cost subtotal enter the ranking. Partially complete quotes get a named owner and a follow-up date. Incomplete quotes are returned to the supplier with a specific request, not a general query.

Handling quotes that mix material categories

Some quotes will not fit a single worksheet because they cover more than one category. A supplier may quote silicon material alongside a reclaim service, or a power module line alongside a materials line. Mixing them in one column hides the comparison.

Split the worksheet by category. Give each category its own header, its own reference specification and its own responsibility basis. Then compare within category, not across it.

This matters most when the categories carry different freight profiles. A reclaim collection and a materials delivery are different movements with different documentation, so a single landed cost line would be misleading.

If you are unsure which category a line belongs to, check the supplier's own description of its service lines before you assign it. The decision meeting: what approvers should see

Bring three things to the approval meeting, and nothing else.

First, the locked header, so everyone agrees on the basis. Second, the worksheet with confidence flags, so the gaps are visible rather than hidden. Third, a short follow-up list naming who owns each open figure and when it is due.

Do not present a single ranked number if any shortlisted quote is incomplete. Present the complete quotes as a ranked set and the incomplete ones as a separate track with dates.

Record the decision itself: which quote was selected, on what basis, which exclusions were accepted, and which open items remain. That record is what makes the next comparison faster and what protects the team if a cost appears later.

Finally, keep the worksheet with the purchase file. When the material arrives, the receiving team can check the delivery against the basis you agreed, and any mismatch becomes a documented query rather than a surprise.

A reusable blank checklist

Copy this list into your worksheet template and tick each item before a quote enters the ranking.

Header locked: reference specification, delivery location, delivery window, currency date, responsibility basis, validity date.

Rows 1 to 7 filled from quote text, or explicitly marked unknown.

Row 8 exclusions listed in the supplier's own wording.

Row 9 subtotal calculated only where rows 1 to 7 are complete.

Row 10 confidence flag assigned.

Source note recorded for every figure.

Category split applied where the quote spans more than one service line.

Follow-up owner and date named for every open item.

Decision record drafted for the approval meeting.

Treat the checklist as an author heuristic, not a standard. Adjust the rows to your own approval process, and keep the principle: no quote is ranked until it sits on the same basis as the others.
