Planning a Purchasing Record When a Wafer Collection and an Inventory Audit Share a Deadline

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on separating deadline ownership, evidence and open questions when a wafer collection and an inventory audit fall due at the same time.

Why two deadlines on one date need two records

A wafer collection booking and an inventory audit can land on the same calendar day without being the same kind of event. The collection is a logistics commitment with an external party. The audit is an internal evidence exercise with a reviewer. When both appear on one shared calendar, the risk is not that either is difficult on its own; it is that one record quietly absorbs the other. A receiving note written for the audit gets reused as collection evidence, or a collection acknowledgement gets filed as proof of inventory. Neither document was designed for that second job.

The practical answer is to keep two records that share a reference but not a purpose. The collection record answers: who booked it, what was acknowledged, and which authorised party confirmed the handover change. The audit record answers: which items were counted, which documents support the count, and who signed off. A single shared case reference lets a reader move between them without merging them.

San Techno works in semiconductor and solar materials and in silicon recovery, helping recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. That context matters here only as identity: the administrative question of who owns which deadline is the same whether the material is incoming, held or being collected. The brand does not decide your audit scope, and this guide does not describe physical handling.

Before writing anything, draw a two-column page. Left column: collection. Right column: audit. Every field you plan to record must sit in exactly one column, or be explicitly marked as shared.

Name the owner of each deadline before the date arrives

Deadline ownership is the first thing to settle, because a date without an owner is a hope. For the collection, the owner is usually the person who raised the booking and can confirm what an authorised contact has acknowledged. For the audit, the owner is usually the person who will sign the count and answer reviewer questions. These may be the same person in a small team, and that is fine, provided the record says so rather than leaving it ambiguous.

Use a short ownership table with four columns: deadline, owner role, confirming party, and status. Status values should be deliberately limited, for example booked, acknowledged, pending confirmation, or closed. Avoid free-text status fields, which drift into narrative and become hard to compare.

A useful discipline is to separate the owner from the approver. The owner moves the task; the approver accepts the outcome. When one person holds both roles, record it explicitly so a later reader does not assume a second signature exists.

That longevity is context for the supplier relationship, not evidence that any particular deadline will be met. Your ownership table should never imply a supplier commitment that has not been confirmed in writing by the relevant party.

Decision for this section: for each of the two deadlines, write one sentence naming the owner role and one sentence naming who confirms completion. If you cannot name the confirming party, that is your first open question.

Build a fictional administrative calendar

A calendar entry is not a record; it is a prompt. The record lives beside it. Here is a fictional example you can adapt, with all names and references invented.

Case reference ST-PROC-0000. Collection deadline: 14 March. Audit deadline: 14 March. Collection owner: procurement coordinator. Collection confirming party: authorised supplier contact. Audit owner: inventory controller. Audit confirming party: internal reviewer.

On 7 March, the coordinator records that the booking reference was issued and that acknowledgement is pending. On 9 March, the inventory controller records that the count sheet is drafted and that two line items await a supporting document. On 12 March, both entries are reviewed together for the first time, specifically to check that neither has borrowed the other's evidence.

Notice what the calendar does not contain: no physical instructions, no assumptions about what the collection involves, and no claim that the audit will pass. It contains dates, owners, statuses and open questions.

A second fictional variant is worth keeping in mind. Suppose the audit deadline moves forward by two days. The calendar should show the change as a new entry with a timestamp, not an overwrite. The original date stays visible so a reader can see what was planned and what changed. This is the same principle you would apply to any revised supplier document: keep the earlier version labelled as historical rather than deleting it.

Worksheet: list every calendar entry touching either deadline, and beside each one write the record it points to. Any entry with no record is a gap.

Separate confirmed facts from questions awaiting authorised answers

The most common failure in a shared-deadline week is a draft that reads as settled when it is not. A field marked pending can be read as complete by someone skimming. The fix is a visible split between confirmed items and open questions, with the open questions phrased as questions rather than statements.

Confirmed items carry a source: an acknowledgement, a signed count sheet, a written confirmation from the named party. Open questions carry an owner and a date by which an answer is expected. For example: has the authorised contact confirmed the handover change in writing, or only verbally? Which document will the reviewer accept as support for the two unmatched line items? Is the collection reference the same as the one quoted in the audit pack?

Where the material in question relates to wafer reclaim, the administrative question is narrower than it sounds: which record states what was acknowledged, and who is entitled to confirm it. The purchasing file should not attempt to answer technical questions about the material itself. Those belong to authorised technical staff, and their answers should be referenced, not paraphrased into the procurement record.

San Techno offers silicon wafer reclaim services alongside semiconductor silicon materials and AC-to-DC power module design. That is a public description of an offering, not a statement about your file. Treat any technical point in your draft as an open question until the appropriate party answers it in writing.

Worksheet: mark every sentence in your draft as confirmed, open, or background. Anything you cannot mark confidently is probably an open question wearing a statement's clothing.

Keep collection evidence and audit evidence in separate folders

Two deadlines on one day create a filing temptation: put everything in one folder named for the date. Resist it. A folder named for a date tells a later reader when something happened, not what it proves.

Use two folders under a shared case reference. The collection folder holds booking references, acknowledgements and confirmations from the authorised party. The audit folder holds count sheets, supporting documents and reviewer sign-off. A third, small folder can hold the shared index that lists what is in each, with one line per document and the date it was added.

The shared index is the piece most teams skip and later miss. It should record the document name, the folder it belongs to, the date added, and who added it. It should not reproduce the contents of either folder. If a document seems to belong in both, that is a signal to create a copy with a clear label rather than to file the original in two places.

San Techno's public materials describe silicon recovery work with integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and note that recovered materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. None of that changes your filing structure, but it is a reminder that material can have a documented onward path. Your record should be able to show which document supported which step without merging the steps.

Worksheet: for each document in your pack, write the single folder it belongs to and the one question it answers.

Write the handover note that survives the deadline

When both deadlines pass, someone will read your file cold. The handover note is what they read first. It should be short enough to finish and specific enough to act on.

A workable structure is five lines. First, the case reference and the two deadlines as they finally stood. Second, the owner and confirming party for each. Third, what was confirmed, with the document that confirms it. Fourth, what remains open, with the owner and the next date. Fifth, what a reader should do next if an open item is still unresolved.

Keep the note free of conclusions you cannot support. Do not write that the audit is complete if the reviewer has not signed. Do not write that the collection is closed if the authorised party has not confirmed. A handover note that overstates is worse than one that lists three open questions honestly.

A useful test is to hand the note to a colleague who has not seen the case and ask them to name the owner of each open item. If they cannot, the note needs another pass.

San Techno's public pages describe wafer inventory work in terms of handling, storage, packing and shipment preparation. That is a description of a service area, not a template for your note. Your note should describe your own records and your own confirmations, and should not borrow supplier language as if it were your evidence.

Worksheet: draft the five lines, then delete any sentence that would not survive being read aloud to the confirming party.

A short review checklist before you close the file

Before the file is closed, run a short review. The following checklist is an author heuristic, not a standard, and the timings are examples only.

One: does each deadline have a named owner and a named confirming party? Two: is every confirmed item tied to a specific document? Three: is every open question phrased as a question with an owner and a next date? Four: are collection and audit documents in separate folders with a shared index? Five: does the handover note avoid conclusions the evidence does not support? Six: has anyone other than the author read the note and correctly identified the open items?

A fictional example of a failed review: a file where the collection acknowledgement was filed in the audit folder because it arrived on the same day. The reviewer accepted it as count evidence. The error was not dishonesty; it was a folder named for a date. The fix is structural, not personal.

San Techno's public materials describe a business built since 2006 across semiconductor and solar materials, specialised power-module designs and biological solutions. That breadth is useful context when you are deciding which supplier contact owns which question, but it does not tell you who owns your internal deadlines. Only your own record can do that.

If a question remains open past the deadline, record it as open rather than closing the file. An honest open item is a better handover than a tidy but unsupported closure.
