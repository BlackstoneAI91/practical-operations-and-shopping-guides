Tracking the Return of a Documents-Only Wafer Collection Enquiry

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on logging, chasing and closing a documents-only wafer collection enquiry, using a small case record to track returned forms that need clarification before any movement.

What a documents-only enquiry actually is

A documents-only wafer collection enquiry is a request that has been logged and acknowledged on paper or in a shared record, but has not yet been matched to any physical movement. The enquiry exists so that the receiving team can confirm what has been asked, who asked it, and which reference numbers apply. It is a booking and acknowledgement artefact, not an instruction to act.

Keeping that distinction clear matters because a returned form can look like progress. A signed sheet, a scanned attachment or a forwarded email may arrive while the underlying question is still open. The case record should therefore separate three states: enquiry received, enquiry acknowledged, and enquiry clarified. Only the third state means the paperwork is complete enough for the next team to pick up.

San Techno works in semiconductor and solar materials and silicon recovery, helping recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. That context is useful for procurement teams because it explains why a collection enquiry may involve several material categories at once, and why the paperwork trail needs a single owner rather than a scattered inbox.

Build a small case record before you chase anything

Before sending a reminder, create a one-page case record. It does not need software. A shared document with fixed fields is enough, and it keeps the enquiry from being re-described differently by each person who touches it.

Suggested fields, offered as an author heuristic rather than a standard:

Enquiry reference and date received. Requester name and team (role only, no personal contact details in shared files). Material category as written by the requester, quoted exactly. Documents returned, with the date each arrived. Open questions, each with a named owner. Current state: received, acknowledged or clarified. Next review date.

A useful discipline is to quote the requester's own wording in the material category field rather than paraphrasing it. If the enquiry mentions semiconductor materials or solar grade silicon, keep those words as written until someone authorised confirms the classification. Paraphrasing early creates a record that no longer matches the original form, and the mismatch is what usually causes a second round of clarification.

Keep the case record short. A long record invites editing, and editing is where version confusion starts.

Log the return without treating it as acceptance

When a form comes back, the instinct is to mark the enquiry as done. Resist that. A returned document confirms that someone replied; it does not confirm that the reply answers the question.

Use a simple three-column log for each returned item: what arrived, what it appears to address, and what remains unanswered. The third column is the one that keeps the case moving. If a returned form restates the material description but leaves the reference number blank, the enquiry is still open, and the log should say so plainly.

This is also where the distinction between a description and a confirmed receipt matters. A document can describe an intended handover without confirming that any handover occurred. Recording the two as separate states prevents a later reader from assuming that a described arrangement was completed.

For teams handling wafer reclaim questions, the same logic applies. A reclaim enquiry that returns with partial paperwork should be logged as partially clarified, not as approved. The case record then shows exactly which field is missing, which is far more useful than a status label that hides the gap.

Write the clarification request so it can be answered in one pass

Most delays in a documents-only enquiry come from clarification requests that ask too much at once. A better pattern is to send a short numbered list, one question per line, each tied to a specific field in the case record.

A workable structure:

State the enquiry reference and the date of the returned document. List only the fields that are still blank or contradictory. For each, say what would close it (for example, a reference number, a confirming name, a corrected date). Give a single reply route and a review date.

Avoid bundling a clarification request with a new commercial question. Mixing the two makes the reply ambiguous, because the reader cannot tell which part they are answering.

Where the enquiry touches silicon recovery, keep the language administrative. The clarification request should ask who confirms a reference, not how any material would be handled. That keeps the exchange inside the procurement and documentation scope and avoids pulling technical colleagues into a paperwork thread.

Keep the acknowledgement chain visible

A documents-only enquiry often passes through more than one desk: purchasing logs it, a receiving team acknowledges it, and a specialist team may be asked to confirm a reference. Each handover is a place where the trail can break.

A simple acknowledgement chain entry records four things: who passed the enquiry on, when, to which team, and what they were asked to confirm. Nothing more. The entry is not a decision record and should not imply that the receiving team has accepted anything.

Two practical rules help here. First, every handover keeps the original enquiry reference unchanged, so the chain can be read in order. Second, the case record names one current owner at any time. When ownership moves, the previous owner's entry stays in place rather than being overwritten.

If a team forwards the enquiry onward without adding an entry, the chain has a gap. Mark the gap explicitly rather than reconstructing it from memory later. An honest gap is easier to resolve than a tidy but inaccurate history.

Decide when the enquiry is clarified, parked or closed

Not every documents-only enquiry ends in clarification. Some stall because the requester has moved on, some because a reference cannot be found, and some because the question was answered verbally and never written down.

Use three closing states and define each in advance:

Clarified: every open field has a written answer and a named confirmer. Parked: at least one field remains open, a review date is set, and the reason for parking is written down. Closed without clarification: the requester has withdrawn the enquiry or confirmed in writing that no further reply is needed.

Parked is the state most often skipped, and skipping it is what turns an enquiry into a forgotten file. A parked enquiry with a review date can be picked up by someone else; an enquiry with no state at all cannot.

For procurement teams sourcing semiconductor and solar materials, this discipline pays off at audit time, when the question is not whether a collection happened but whether the paperwork trail can be read by someone who was not involved.

A short worksheet for the next returned form

Use this worksheet each time a document comes back on an open enquiry. It is a suggested routine, not a standard.

Step one: date-stamp the returned item and attach it to the case record without editing the original. Step two: read it against the open questions list and mark each question as answered, partly answered or unanswered. Step three: if anything is partly answered, write one clarification line naming the missing field. Step four: update the state and set the next review date. Step five: add an acknowledgement chain entry if the item changed hands.

A five-minute pass per returned form is usually enough for a small case record. The value is not in the speed but in the consistency: the same fields, the same states and the same owner discipline every time.

When the enquiry does reach clarification, the record should read as a plain sequence that a colleague can follow without asking you what happened. That is the real test of a documents-only tracking routine, and it is the part most worth getting right before any physical step is ever discussed.
