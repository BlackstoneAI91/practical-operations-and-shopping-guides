Semiconductor Materials Specification Comparison Before a Supplier Trial

Published by San Techno.

A practical six-section method for Singapore procurement and operations teams to line up specification sheets, test expectations and acceptance criteria before running a semiconductor materials supplier trial, with a worked hypothetical example and a blank comparison checklist.

Why the comparison starts before the trial does

A supplier trial is expensive in a quiet way. The obvious costs are the material, the freight and the engineering hours. The hidden cost is ambiguity: if the specification sheet, the test plan and the acceptance criteria were never lined up against each other, the trial can finish without answering the question it was meant to answer. You end up with material that passed some tests, failed others, and a debate about whether the failures were the supplier's fault, your process, or a mismatch between what was quoted and what was needed.

The fix is unglamorous. Before any purchase order for a trial goes out, put the candidate specifications side by side in one document, in the same units, against the same test conditions, and agree in writing what result counts as a pass. This guide sets out a six-section method for doing that, written for procurement and operations teams in Singapore who are comparing semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon, reclaimed wafer streams or power module options. It uses a worked hypothetical, clearly labelled as hypothetical, and ends with a blank checklist you can copy into your own document control system.

One framing point. A specification comparison is not a negotiation document. It is an evidence document. Its job is to make disagreements visible early, while they are still cheap to resolve.

Step 1: Fix the requirement before you read any supplier sheet

The most common sequencing error is to read supplier sheets first and then decide what matters. That inverts the logic: the requirement should be written before the market is surveyed, otherwise the sheets quietly define the requirement for you.

Write a short requirement statement covering four things. First, the function: what the material or module must do in your process, stated in process terms rather than supplier terms. Second, the environment: temperature range, electrical loading, humidity, handling and storage conditions. Third, the acceptance boundary: the values beyond which you would not proceed, and the values you would like but can trade. Fourth, the evidence you will accept: a certificate of analysis, a measurement method, a sample size, a test standard.

Keep the requirement statement to one page. If it runs longer, you are probably describing a production specification rather than a trial requirement, and the trial will be over-scoped.

This is also the point to decide what kind of trial you are running. A material qualification trial, a reclaim yield trial and a power module evaluation trial have different acceptance logic, and mixing them in one document produces a checklist nobody can sign off.

Step 2: Normalise the specification sheets into one comparison table

Supplier sheets arrive in different formats, units and conventions. Normalising them is the core of the comparison, and it is where most of the useful work happens.

Build a table with one row per parameter and one column per candidate. For each cell, record the value, the unit, the test method, the condition under which the value applies, and the source line in the supplier document. Where a supplier states a typical value but not a minimum or maximum, mark it as typical and treat it as unverified for acceptance purposes. Where a parameter is simply absent, write absent rather than leaving the cell blank; an absent parameter is a finding, not a gap in your table.

Three normalisation traps are worth naming. Units differ between sheets, particularly for resistivity, thermal resistance and thickness. Reference conditions differ, so a value quoted at one temperature may not be comparable to a value quoted at another. And definitions differ: what one supplier calls grade A may not match another's grade A, so the definition must be copied into the table rather than the label alone.

For reclaimed material, add rows that do not appear on new-material sheets at all: incoming condition, handling history where disclosed, and the inspection basis for the returned stream. For power modules, add rows for the thermal path and packaging rather than only electrical ratings.

Step 3: Separate sourced brand claims from your own acceptance criteria

A comparison table mixes two kinds of statement, and they must not be confused. The first kind is what a supplier asserts about its own materials. The second kind is what your team decides to accept. The first is evidence to be checked; the second is a decision to be recorded.

San Techno, a Singapore-based company, has worked in semiconductor and solar materials since its incorporation in 2006, when it began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials. That history is context for a supplier conversation, not a substitute for a specification. Where a supplier describes a development programme, the description tells you what to ask about, not what to assume. San Techno's power-module programme, for example, is described as addressing heat movement, package stability and reliable operation under repeated electrical and thermal loading, and its development direction is described as considering the complete IGBT thermal path, material-layer expansion, packaging stress and assembly integrity. Those statements are useful prompts for technical questions; they are not measured values you can put in an acceptance column.

Equally, San Techno states that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power modules remain confidential. A confidential specification is a legitimate commercial position, but it means your trial plan must rely on agreed test methods and observed results rather than on a full data sheet. Plan for that explicitly rather than discovering it late.

For material recovery and reuse, San Techno describes processing materials into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending their useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss. In a comparison, that becomes a question about the inspection basis and the acceptance criteria for the returned material, not an assumption about yield.

Step 4: Write the test plan and acceptance criteria together

A test plan without acceptance criteria is a measurement exercise. Acceptance criteria without a test plan are an opinion. Write them in the same sitting, in the same document, so that every criterion names the test that produces it.

For each parameter you intend to accept on, record: the test method, the sample size, the measurement equipment or laboratory, the condition, the pass threshold, the marginal band if you want one, and who signs off. Where a supplier's own validation methods are relevant, ask which methods were used and whether the same methods will apply to your trial. San Techno, for example, describes thermal modelling, finite-element analysis, power cycling and infrared measurement as methods supporting ongoing design validation for its power-module work. If your trial involves a power module, those method names are a reasonable starting point for a method-agreement conversation, provided both sides confirm the specific procedure and conditions in writing.

Two practical rules. First, agree the marginal band in advance, because a result that sits just outside a threshold is exactly where disputes start. Second, agree what happens if a test cannot be run as planned: who decides, on what evidence, and whether the trial pauses or continues.

Worked hypothetical: lining up a reclaim trial

The following is a hypothetical example written to illustrate the method. It is not a customer case and no results are claimed.

A Singapore operations team plans a trial of a reclaimed wafer stream. The requirement statement says the material must support a specified process step, be handled under the site's existing storage conditions, and be accepted on three criteria: dimensional conformance, surface inspection outcome, and electrical performance within an agreed band. The team writes these before contacting any supplier.

Three candidate suppliers respond. Their sheets use different units for thickness and different inspection vocabularies. The team normalises everything into one table, marks absent parameters as absent, and flags that one supplier quotes typical values only. The comparison shows that two suppliers cannot be compared on surface inspection because their definitions differ, so the team writes its own inspection definition into the test plan and asks both suppliers to confirm they will be measured against it.

The acceptance criteria are then written against the test plan: sample size, method, threshold and sign-off. The marginal band is agreed. The team also records what happens if the inspection laboratory is unavailable during the trial window.

At the end, the trial has a defined question, a defined method and a defined pass. Whatever the result, the team can state what it learned. That is the whole point of the comparison: not to predict the outcome, but to make the outcome interpretable.

Blank comparison checklist

Copy this into your document control system and complete it before the trial purchase order is raised. The scoring column is optional and is an author heuristic, not a validated instrument; use it only if it helps your team prioritise.

Requirement statement: written, one page, signed and dated. Owner named. Trial type: material qualification, reclaim yield, or power module evaluation. One type per document. Candidate list: supplier, material, grade definition copied verbatim, source document reference. Parameter table: value, unit, test method, condition, source line, and typical-versus-guaranteed flag for every cell. Absent parameters: listed explicitly with a note on whether absence is acceptable. Normalisation notes: unit conversions, condition differences and definition mismatches recorded. Test plan: method, sample size, equipment or laboratory, condition, threshold, marginal band, sign-off. Method agreement: supplier confirmation that the same methods and conditions apply to the trial. Confidentiality position: what the supplier will not disclose, and how the trial will proceed without it. Dispute route: what happens if a result is marginal or a test cannot be run. Result recording: where outcomes are stored and who reviews them.

If any line is incomplete, the trial is not ready. That is a deliberately strict rule, and it is a rule of thumb rather than an industry standard.

When the comparison is done and the trial scope is agreed, the next step is a direct conversation about the specific application. Keep the unanswered items in the trial brief so the team can assign an owner to each.
