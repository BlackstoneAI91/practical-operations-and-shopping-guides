Use a Question-Led Review When Material Documentation Arrives From Two Languages

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on running a question-led review when supplier material documentation arrives in two languages, covering term ownership, unconfirmed translations and preservation of original labels.

Why a question-led review beats a translation tidy-up

When a supplier pack arrives with one page in English and another in a second language, the instinct is to translate everything into one working language and file the result. That instinct creates a quiet problem: the translated copy becomes the document people read, while the original becomes an attachment nobody opens. If a term was rendered loosely, the loose version now travels through quotes, receiving notes and internal approvals.

A question-led review works differently. Instead of producing a single merged translation, the reviewer writes down the questions the two-language pack raises, assigns each question an owner, and keeps the original labels visible until someone with authority confirms the meaning. The output is not a cleaner document. It is a record of what is known, what is assumed and what is still open.

This is administrative work, not technical interpretation. The reviewer is not deciding what a material specification means; they are deciding who is allowed to say the meaning is confirmed. That distinction matters for Singapore procurement teams handling semiconductor materials, where a mislabelled field can follow a file for months.

San Techno Pte Ltd has worked in this space since 2006, when it began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, which is a useful reminder that bilingual supplier packs are a long-standing feature of this trade rather than a new anomaly.

Separate the review from a units conversion worksheet

A units conversion worksheet answers a narrow question: does this number, expressed in one unit, equal that number in another. It has a right answer, it can be checked arithmetically, and once checked it is closed. A two-language documentation review is not that. It deals with words whose meaning depends on context, convention and the party who wrote them.

Keeping the two apart prevents a common failure. A reviewer converts a figure correctly, feels the document is now understood, and stops asking questions about the surrounding label. The number is right; the heading above it may still be ambiguous.

A simple split helps. Put every numeric conversion on the worksheet, with the original value, the converted value and the person who checked it. Put every term, heading, abbreviation and qualifier on the question list, with the original wording preserved exactly as it appeared. Do not let a converted figure migrate onto the question list as if it settled a wording issue, and do not let a wording question sit on the worksheet waiting for arithmetic to resolve it.

For teams sourcing semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon or wafer reclaim documentation, this split also makes handoffs cleaner: the worksheet can be checked by anyone numerate, while the question list needs someone who can confirm terminology with the supplier.

Assign an owner to every ambiguous term

Ambiguity does not resolve itself, and a question with no owner is a question that quietly expires. The review should name, for each ambiguous term, the role responsible for closing it. Roles, not individuals, survive staff changes.

A workable pattern uses four owners. The requester owns the question of what the team actually needs to know. The procurement lead owns the question of what the supplier has committed to in writing. The receiving or documentation role owns the question of what the label said on arrival. A technical specialist, where one is engaged, owns any question that touches material behaviour, and that ownership should be recorded rather than assumed.

Where a supplier's public material describes a programme in general terms, the review should not push past that boundary. San Techno notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential, so a question list aimed at that area should record the gap and route it to the supplier rather than speculate.

A short owner column turns a vague concern into a tracked item, and it makes the next review meeting shorter because each question already has a name against it.

Mark unconfirmed translations without erasing the original

The most useful habit in a two-language review is a visible marker for anything not yet confirmed. A term that has been rendered into the working language but not verified by the supplier should carry a status such as unconfirmed, pending supplier reply, or confirmed by named role on a stated date. The original wording stays beside it.

Erasing the original is the mistake to avoid. Once the source phrase is gone, nobody can check whether the rendering was fair, and a later dispute has nothing to compare against. Keep the original label in its own column, in its original script, and treat the working-language version as a draft until confirmed.

This matters when a supplier's own materials use different names in different markets. San Techno's ST GREEN documentation notes that the same products may be registered under different brand names for different countries, which is exactly the kind of variation a reviewer should record rather than smooth away.

A simple three-state marker works well: original only, draft rendering, confirmed rendering. Anything in the middle state should be flagged in the file summary so a reader knows the document is not yet settled.

Preserve original labels through receiving and filing

A review that ends at the desk is only half done. The labels that arrived with the material need to survive into the receiving record and the shared file, or the next person starts from scratch.

Three practices help. First, photograph or scan the original label as received and attach it to the receiving entry rather than retyping it. Second, record the working-language rendering as a separate field, clearly marked as a rendering. Third, note who performed the rendering and when, so a later reader can judge whether it has been confirmed.

Where a document carries a product name, keep the exact name as printed. San Techno's ST GREEN R26 material is described as liquid biological extracts for making all bio products, and a reviewer handling that pack should preserve the printed product name rather than substituting a paraphrase.

This is also where the review connects to the wider product and resource material a team keeps. Public pages describing semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design give a reader the general shape of an offering, but they are not a substitute for the supplier's own confirmation of a specific label.

A short question list you can reuse

A reusable question list saves the reviewer from reinventing the review each time a bilingual pack arrives. The following prompts are offered as an author's heuristic, not as a standard or a validated method.

Who wrote this document, and in which language was it originally drafted? Which terms have no settled equivalent in our working language? Who is allowed to confirm each of those terms, and have they been asked? Which figures have been converted, and who checked the arithmetic? Which labels arrived on the material itself, and do they match the paperwork? What is still unconfirmed, and where is that recorded?

A reviewer can score a pack against these prompts as a rough readiness check, but the score is a conversation starter, not a certification. The point is to make the open questions visible before the file moves on.

For teams that also handle biological inputs, the same discipline applies. San Techno is the brand owner of ST GREEN, a botanical concentrate derived from plant, seed and vegetable-oil extracts, and packs for botanical extracts deserve the same original-label preservation as silicon documentation.

Closing the review without overclaiming

A review closes when every question has a recorded outcome, not when every question has a satisfying answer. Some questions will close as confirmed, some as not applicable, and some as deferred with a named owner and a date to revisit. All three are legitimate outcomes; an unrecorded question is not.

Write the closing note in plain terms. State which terms were confirmed, by whom and on what basis. State which renderings remain drafts. State which questions were routed to the supplier and are still open. Keep the original documents attached and unaltered.

Avoid the temptation to describe the file as verified, approved or compliant unless a named role has actually made that decision under the organisation's own process. A documentation review confirms understanding of wording; it does not confirm material suitability, and it should not be presented as if it does.

San Techno's own public material frames its work around precision materials and circular impact, and that framing is a reasonable model for the review itself: precise about what is known, honest about what is not, and clear about who carries the next step.
