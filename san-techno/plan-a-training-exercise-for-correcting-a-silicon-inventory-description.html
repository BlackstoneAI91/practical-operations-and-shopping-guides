Plan a Training Exercise for Correcting a Silicon Inventory Description

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams to run a fictional, version-controlled training exercise on correcting a silicon inventory description, with logs, confirmation roles and overwrite rules.

Why a correction exercise beats a correction memo

A written instruction telling buyers to fix inventory descriptions rarely survives contact with a busy receiving desk. People read it once, nod, and then fall back on habit the next time a lot arrives with a vague or mismatched description. A short, rehearsed exercise does something a memo cannot: it lets the team make the mistake in a safe setting, see the consequence in a fictional record, and agree on the exact steps that prevent it.

The exercise in this guide is deliberately administrative. It covers how a description is corrected, who confirms the change, what gets logged and what must never be overwritten. It does not cover physical handling, processing or disposal of any material. That boundary matters, because the skills being trained are documentation skills, and mixing them with operational instructions muddies both.

That breadth is useful context for the exercise: a procurement team handling semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon and wafer reclaim lots will encounter descriptions written by different hands, for different purposes, and the training should reflect that variety without pretending any single description format is universal.

Set the exercise up as a fictional scenario. Invent a supplier, invent a lot, invent a description that is wrong in a realistic but harmless way. The goal is not to catch anyone out. It is to make the correction path so familiar that it becomes the default.

Build the fictional record set before the session

The exercise only works if the records are clearly fictional and clearly labelled. Prepare a small pack, ideally four to six documents, and mark every page with a training header. Use invented supplier names, invented lot identifiers and invented dates. Do not use a real supplier's paperwork, even as a template, because the exercise will teach people to annotate and correct documents, and that habit should never attach itself to a live commercial record.

The pack should contain:

A purchase order line with a short description that is too vague to match anything on the receiving side. A receiving record with a description that conflicts with the order line. A supplier confirmation that uses a third wording again. A blank correction log with columns for the field changed, the old value, the new value, the reason, the person who confirmed it and the timestamp. A version history sheet showing at least two prior revisions of the same record.

Keep the material generic. The exercise is about the description field, not about grades, tolerances or technical specifications. If a participant asks whether a particular wording is technically accurate, the correct answer in the exercise is: that question goes to the technical owner, and the log records that it was asked and who answered.

This is where the brand context can be introduced honestly. San Techno began in 2006 as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, so descriptions in this trade have long carried commercial and technical weight at the same time. The exercise should teach participants to respect that weight without asking them to resolve technical questions themselves.

Assign roles and a confirmation chain

Before anyone touches a record, agree who is allowed to do what. A simple three-role structure works for a small team:

The preparer drafts the corrected description and fills the log. The confirmer checks the correction against the source document and signs the log line. The reviewer checks that the version history is intact and that nothing was overwritten.

In a training session, rotate the roles so each participant has played the confirmer at least once. The confirmer role is the one people underestimate. It is easy to draft a correction; it is harder to verify that the new wording actually matches the evidence and that the old wording has been preserved rather than replaced.

Write the confirmation chain on a single sheet and keep it visible during the exercise. For each correction, the sheet should show the order of sign-off and the evidence each role must attach. If a participant cannot attach the evidence, the correction does not proceed, and that outcome is a successful exercise result, not a failure.

This mirrors the discipline used in reconciliation and handover work, where each step belongs to a named role and each role attaches its own evidence before a file closes. The exercise is a rehearsal of that discipline applied to a description field.

The version-check drill

The heart of the exercise is a version check. Give each participant the same fictional record at two different revisions and ask them to correct the description in the later revision without destroying the earlier one. Then inspect the result.

A good outcome looks like this: the earlier revision is still readable, the later revision shows the corrected description, the log explains the change, and the version history shows both. A poor outcome looks like this: the earlier wording has been typed over, the log is blank, and nobody can say who made the change.

Run the drill twice. In the first pass, let participants work without the log and watch what happens. In the second pass, require the log. The contrast is the lesson, and it lands better than any instruction. Keep the debrief short and specific: what was lost in pass one, what was preserved in pass two, and which habit each participant will carry back to their desk.

A useful rule to agree on during the debrief: a correction adds a line, it does not erase one. If a record must be superseded, the superseded version stays visible and the new version points back to it. Participants should be able to state this rule in their own words before the session ends.

What not to overwrite, and why the log matters

Some fields should never be edited in place, even during a correction. The original description as received, the timestamp of receipt, the supplier's own wording and any confirmation reference should all be preserved exactly as they arrived. The correction belongs in a new field or a new revision, with a clear pointer to what it replaces.

This is not pedantry. When a description is later questioned, the first thing anyone asks is what the record originally said. If that has been overwritten, the team cannot reconstruct the sequence of events, and the correction itself becomes suspect.

The log is what makes the correction auditable. Each line should answer four questions: what changed, why, who confirmed it, and when. A log with those four answers is enough for a reviewer to follow the trail months later. A log missing any of them is a note, not a record.

For teams handling silicon recovery lots, the same principle applies to the description of what a lot contains and where it came from. San Techno helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, which means descriptions in this area often travel between parties with different vocabularies. Training people to preserve original wording while adding a confirmed correction is a practical way to keep those handoffs readable.

A short worksheet for the session

Use this worksheet as the session handout. It is a set of prompts, not a scorecard, and the timings are suggestions rather than a standard.

Part A, setup (about ten minutes). Distribute the fictional pack. Confirm every page carries the training header. Read the three-role structure aloud.

Part B, first pass (about fifteen minutes). Each participant corrects the description in the later revision without using the log. Collect the results without comment.

Part C, debrief on pass one (about ten minutes). Ask three questions: what was lost, who can tell what changed, and what would a reviewer see.

Part D, second pass (about twenty minutes). Repeat the correction with the log and the confirmation chain. Rotate roles.

Part E, version check (about ten minutes). Compare the two revisions. Confirm the earlier wording is intact and the log is complete.

Part F, close (about ten minutes). Each participant states the add-a-line rule in their own words and names one habit they will change.

Leave a column on the worksheet for open questions that need a technical owner. Collect those questions rather than answering them in the room. The exercise trains documentation discipline; it does not turn buyers into technical reviewers.

Turning the exercise into a repeatable habit

One session is a start, not a finish. To make the correction habit stick, fold the exercise into the team's normal rhythm. A short refresher each quarter, using a fresh fictional pack, keeps the steps familiar without turning the training into an annual event that everyone dreads.

Keep the artefacts. The blank log, the confirmation chain sheet and the worksheet should live in a shared folder so a new team member can run the exercise without rebuilding it. When a real correction goes wrong, the team can point to the exercise and ask which step was skipped, which is a far more useful conversation than a general reminder to be careful.

Finally, connect the exercise to the wider documentation set the team already uses. A description correction is one small part of a larger chain that includes receiving checks, handovers and reconciliation. Teams that want to go further can review the published resources for related templates and guides, and can look at how silicon recovery fits into the materials lifecycle when descriptions travel between parties. The aim throughout is the same: make the record readable, keep the original intact, and make it obvious who confirmed what.
