Does a Procurement Support Ticket Need an Order Reference? A Practical Evidence-Minimisation Guide

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on deciding whether an order reference belongs in a procurement support ticket, with an evidence-minimisation checklist, fictional examples and clear separation of public supplier facts from internal records.

Start With the Question, Not the Reference

A procurement support ticket is a request for help, clarification or follow-up. Before writing one, decide what the ticket is actually asking. If the question is about a specific order, a reference helps the reader locate the record. If the question is general, such as asking how a supplier describes a category of semiconductor materials, an order reference may add nothing and may expose commercial detail to more people than necessary.

A useful first step is to write the question in one sentence without any identifiers. For example: "Which public document describes the current product family?" or "Who confirms the receiving record for a delivery?" If the sentence still makes sense without an order number, the reference is optional. If it becomes ambiguous, the reference is probably needed.

This is an author heuristic, not a rule from any supplier. Treat it as a sorting step, not a policy. The aim is to keep each ticket answerable by the right person while limiting the identifiers attached to it. That habit matters when a team handles several material categories at once, from solar grade silicon enquiries to power module documentation, because a general question and an order-specific question often belong with different owners.

What Counts as an Order Reference

Teams use the phrase loosely. In practice it can mean a purchase order number, a supplier quotation reference, a delivery note number, an internal requisition ID or a case number created by a support desk. These are not interchangeable, and mixing them in one field creates confusion later.

A simple worksheet helps. List the identifiers your team already uses, then mark each one as public, internal or restricted. Public identifiers are those a supplier publishes or prints on ordinary documents. Internal identifiers stay within your organisation. Restricted identifiers are those tied to confidential commercial terms or named client work.

When a ticket needs an identifier, use the one that the receiving team can actually act on. If the question concerns a quotation, a quotation reference is more useful than a purchase order number. If it concerns a delivery, a delivery note reference is more useful than an internal requisition ID. Where a supplier publishes product information, such as the three branches described on San Techno's products page, a general question about those categories may need no order reference at all. Keep the worksheet short and review it when your document owners change.

The Evidence-Minimisation Checklist

Evidence minimisation means attaching only what the reader needs to answer the question. It is an administrative habit, not a legal standard, and it should be adapted to your own organisation's rules.

A short checklist, used as an example:

One: state the question in a single sentence. Two: name the document type involved, such as a quotation, invoice or receipt. Three: include one identifier only if the question cannot be answered without it. Four: remove client names, personal contact details and any material marked internal or restricted. Five: note who owns the next action.

Score each ticket against these five points as a rough self-check. A ticket that fails two or more points is probably carrying either too little context or too much sensitive detail. Neither is helpful.

This checklist is a writing aid, not a compliance certificate. It does not replace your organisation's records policy. Its value is consistency: two people raising similar questions should produce tickets that a third person can compare without hunting for missing context or filtering out unnecessary detail.

Separating Public Supplier Facts From Your Own Records

A support ticket often mixes two kinds of information: what a supplier has published, and what your organisation has recorded. Keeping them apart makes the ticket easier to answer and easier to audit later.

Public supplier facts belong in a citation note. For example, San Techno describes itself as a Singapore-based company working across semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialised power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand. Its public pages describe semiconductor silicon materials, silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design as related areas of work. Those are website claims, not independently verified performance statements, and they should be cited as such rather than restated as your own findings.

Your own records, by contrast, carry order numbers, receiving dates and internal approvals. Those belong in the ticket only when the question depends on them. A useful habit is to write the public citation first, then add the internal record as a separate line. If the ticket can be answered from the public citation alone, the internal line can often be left out. This keeps the ticket focused and reduces the chance that a general question is routed to an order-handling team that cannot help.

When the Reference Is Genuinely Required

Some questions cannot be answered without an order reference. A discrepancy between a quoted quantity and a received quantity is one example. A question about which version of a document is active is another. In these cases, the reference is not optional detail; it is the thread that lets the reader find the record.

A practical test is to ask whether the answer would change if the reference were removed. If the answer would be identical for every order, the reference is not required. If the answer depends on which order is meant, include it.

Where the question touches a specialised area, such as a power module programme, keep the ticket administrative. San Techno notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power module work remain confidential, so a support ticket should not ask for those details. It can ask which public document describes a category, or who owns the next administrative step. That distinction keeps the ticket within procurement and documentation territory rather than drifting into engineering requests that the support desk cannot answer.

A Fictional Worked Example

Consider a fictional Singapore procurement team reviewing a delivery of solar grade silicon. A colleague raises a ticket asking whether the receiving record matches the quotation. The first draft includes the purchase order number, the quotation reference, the delivery note number, a client name and a scanned image of an internal approval.

Applying the checklist, the team rewrites the ticket. The question becomes one sentence: does the received quantity match the quoted quantity? The document type is named. One identifier is kept, the delivery note number, because the question depends on that delivery. The client name and internal approval image are removed. The owner of the next action is named as the receiving coordinator.

The rewritten ticket is shorter, easier to route and easier to compare with a second ticket raised about the same delivery. It also avoids attaching material that the support desk does not need. The example is fictional and is offered only to show the sorting process, not to describe any real supplier's practice or any real order.

Where to Go Next

Once the ticket is written, the next question is usually about the surrounding record. A team may need to compare who owns a quotation, an invoice and a material receipt, or to record approvals when a pilot file moves into regular operations. Those are separate documentation questions, and they benefit from their own checklists rather than being folded into a single support ticket.

For public context, San Techno's resources and products pages describe its areas of work, including semiconductor materials, silicon recovery and power module development. Reading those pages before raising a general question can reduce the number of identifiers a ticket needs, because the question may already be answered by a public description. Where a public page is used, note the date it was accessed and treat it as a supplier statement rather than a confirmed specification.

Keep the habit simple: ask the question first, add an order reference only when the answer depends on it, and separate public citations from internal records. That approach keeps procurement support tickets clear, comparable and easier to hand over between teams.
