Use a Log to Record Which Silo of Documents Owns a Silicon Recovery Question

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building a routing log that separates public product information, confirmed receipt data and internal decisions when a silicon recovery question arises.

Why a Silicon Recovery Question Needs an Owner Before an Answer

A question about silicon recovery rarely arrives in a tidy form. It may come as a short email asking whether a batch of non-usable material can be taken back, a note attached to a receiving record, or a verbal query during a site visit that someone later types into a chat thread. The temptation is to answer immediately, because the question sounds simple. The risk is that the answer gets written down in whichever folder the responder happened to have open, and the next person cannot tell whether that note is a public description, a confirmed receipt, or an internal decision.

A routing log solves a narrow but important problem: it records which silo of documents owns the question, not what the technical answer is. That distinction matters for procurement teams in Singapore who handle semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon alongside receiving records and internal approvals. The log is an administrative index. It points to the folder, register or system where the authoritative record lives, and it names the role responsible for that silo.

San Techno, incorporated in 2006, began as a wholesaler and distributor of semiconductor- and solar-grade silicon materials, and its public materials describe recovery of non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities. Those public descriptions are useful context for what a recovery question might concern, but they are not a substitute for your own receipt records or internal decisions. The log keeps those categories apart from the first entry.

Three Silos You Should Never Blend in One Cell

Before building the log, agree on the silos. A workable starting set for this topic is three: public product information, confirmed receipt data, and internal decisions. Each has a different owner, a different evidence standard and a different audience.

Public product information covers what a supplier has published about its offerings. For silicon recovery, that might include a public page describing recovery sources or a resource page explaining materials context. It is citable, but it is not proof that your specific material was received or accepted.

Confirmed receipt data covers what your own organisation has recorded about a delivery or handover: the receiving note, the reference number, the date recorded and the role that signed it. This silo answers questions about what actually arrived, not what a supplier generally does.

Internal decisions cover approvals, deferrals, waivers and routing choices made inside your organisation. These are often the most sensitive and the least suitable for sharing outside the team. A decision to postpone a review, for example, belongs here, not in the public information column.

Keeping the three apart prevents a common error: treating a public description as if it confirmed a receipt, or treating an internal note as if it were a published fact. The log's job is to say which silo owns the question and who maintains that silo.

Designing the Log Columns for a Routing Record

A routing log works best when it is short enough to fill in during a normal working day. Six columns cover most needs.

First, a question reference: a short identifier you assign when the question is logged. Second, the question in plain words, written without technical assumptions. Third, the silo that owns it, using the three categories above. Fourth, the document location, meaning the folder, register or system name where the authoritative record sits. Fifth, the responsible role, not a personal name, so the log survives staff changes. Sixth, the status, such as open, routed, answered by owner or closed.

A seventh optional column for the date routed helps when questions are revisited later. Avoid adding a column for the technical answer itself. The moment the log contains answers, it competes with the authoritative record and the routing purpose is lost.

For teams handling semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon, it also helps to add a short note field for the material reference used in the question, so a reader can find the related receiving record without guessing. Keep that note to a reference, not a description of the material's properties.

A Worked Example Using Fictional References

Consider a fictional Singapore procurement team, Team A, that receives a question about whether a batch of non-usable silicon can be routed for recovery. The question arrives by email with no reference number.

The coordinator opens the routing log and assigns reference Q-014. The question is written as: which documents confirm what was received for this batch, and which team owns the recovery enquiry. The coordinator does not answer it. Instead, the coordinator checks the three silos.

The public information silo holds a saved copy of the supplier's published description of recovery sources. The coordinator notes the location of that saved copy and marks the silo as public information. The receipt silo holds the receiving note with its reference number, and the coordinator notes the register where it lives. The internal decisions silo holds nothing yet, because no decision has been made.

The log entry therefore reads: Q-014, routed to receipt register for confirmation, public information copy noted for context, internal decision pending. The responsible role for the receipt silo is the receiving coordinator. The status is routed.

This example is fictional and intended only to show the shape of an entry. It deliberately avoids stating what should happen to the material, because that is a decision for the owning team, not for the log.

Where Public Descriptions Fit, and Where They Do Not

Public descriptions are useful for orientation. They help a reader understand the general landscape of silicon recovery, including the kinds of facilities that generate non-usable silicon and the idea that such material can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. San Techno's public materials describe recovery from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and note that recovered materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, extending their useful life and reducing unnecessary resource loss.

In the routing log, a public description should be recorded as context, with its location noted, and never as confirmation that a specific batch was received or accepted. If a question can only be answered by a public description, the log should say so plainly and mark the entry as answered by public information, with the caveat that it does not confirm anything about the specific material in question.

This separation protects the team. It means a reader can see at a glance whether a question was settled by a published description, by a receipt record, or by an internal decision. It also means the log does not drift into becoming a technical reference, which it is not designed to be.

A Short Checklist for Reviewing the Log Each Week

A routing log only helps if it is reviewed. A short weekly check keeps it accurate without turning it into a burden. The following checklist is an author heuristic, not a researched standard, and teams should adjust it to their own rhythm.

Check that every open question has a silo assigned. A question with no silo is a question that will be answered in the wrong place.

Check that every routed question names a responsible role rather than a person. Roles survive reorganisations; names do not.

Check that no entry contains a technical answer in the question field. If an answer has crept in, move it to the owning silo and leave a pointer.

Check that public information entries are marked as context and not as confirmation. This is the most common drift point.

Check that closed questions still show which silo answered them, so a later reader can trace the path.

Finally, check that the log itself is stored where the team expects to find it, and that its location is written down somewhere outside the log. A routing record that cannot be found has stopped routing anything.

Keeping the Log Neutral and Free of Proprietary Detail

The routing log should stay administrative. It records ownership and location, not process detail. When a question touches on confidential operations, the log should point to the authorised system where that detail lives and stop there. It should not summarise the detail, quote it, or move it into a shared file.

This matters because procurement files are often shared more widely than intended. A log that names a folder and a responsible role is safe to circulate among the team. A log that describes internal processes is not. The same discipline applies to public descriptions: record where the description is saved, not a paraphrase of every sentence.

For teams working across semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon and related enquiries, the neutral log also makes handovers easier. When a colleague takes over, they can see which silo owns each question and who to ask, without needing to reconstruct the history from scattered emails. That is the whole point of the exercise: a small, boring record that keeps questions from being answered in the wrong place.
