Written Procurement Confirmation vs Meeting Note: Which Counts as Proof?

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams sourcing semiconductor materials: how to compare a written procurement confirmation with a meeting note, decide which record carries status, and ask the confirmation owner to clarify differences.

Why the two records are not interchangeable

A meeting note and a written procurement confirmation often describe the same conversation, but they do different jobs. A meeting note records what was discussed, who attended and what someone understood at the time. A written procurement confirmation is a document issued to state a position: a reference, a scope, a quantity basis, a date, or a next step that the issuing party is prepared to stand behind.

Treating them as equivalent creates a quiet problem. The meeting note may be the only place a question was raised, while the confirmation is the only place an answer was given. If the team files them together without labels, a later reader cannot tell which one governs.

A simple working rule helps: the meeting note is context, the confirmation is the record of status. Context explains why a decision was discussed; status tells you what is currently accepted. When the two disagree, the disagreement itself is the finding, and it belongs in the file rather than in someone's memory.

This matters in a Singapore procurement setting where teams source semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon and related items across several suppliers and internal reviewers. The same person rarely reads both documents, so the labels have to do the explaining. San Techno Pte Ltd works in semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand, so a supplier file may legitimately contain several document types at once.

Classify each record before you compare content

Before comparing wording, give each document a status label. This takes a few minutes and prevents most confusion later.

Use four labels. Draft means the document is still being shaped and no one has issued it. Issued means the document was sent by its owner and is the current version. Superseded means a later version exists and this one is kept only for history. Reference means the document is background, such as a meeting note or an internal briefing.

A short worksheet, applied to each file:

Who authored it, and in what role? Was it sent to anyone outside the author's team? Does it carry a version or reference that can be matched to a later document? Does it state a position, or only describe a discussion? What would make it out of date?

Score each question as clear, unclear or absent. Treat the score as an author heuristic, not a standard. A document with several absent answers is probably reference material, not a confirmation.

Keep the original file untouched and record the label in a separate index. If a meeting note later turns out to be the only written trace of a question, that is useful to know, but it does not promote the note to confirmation status. The label describes the document's role, not its importance to the reader.

Compare the five fields that usually diverge

Most differences between a meeting note and a confirmation fall into a small set of fields. Compare them side by side rather than reading each document in full.

Subject: does the note describe the same item or request as the confirmation? A note may say "silicon samples discussed" while the confirmation names a specific category.

Reference: does either document carry an identifier that the other can be matched to? If only one has a reference, note that.

Quantity or scope basis: does the note record an intention while the confirmation records a stated figure or range? Record both without deciding which is correct.

Date: separate the date of the meeting, the date the confirmation was issued, and the date your team received it. These are three different facts.

Owner: who is expected to act next, and who issued the confirmation?

For a supplier working across semiconductor materials and solar grade silicon, the subject field is where mismatches most often appear, because catalogue headings and internal shorthand rarely match word for word. Write the difference in neutral language: "note refers to category A; confirmation refers to category B; relationship not established." That sentence is more useful than a guess, and it gives the confirmation owner a precise question to answer.

Decide which record carries status, and say so in writing

Once the fields are compared, make an explicit status decision and write it down. A short entry is enough: which document is treated as the current confirmation, which is retained as context, and what remains unresolved.

Three outcomes are common. First, the confirmation is current and the note is context; file both, label the note as reference. Second, the confirmation is current but the note raises a question the confirmation does not answer; keep the question open and route it to the confirmation owner. Third, no confirmation exists yet; the note stays reference material and the file is marked as awaiting a written confirmation.

Avoid the fourth outcome, which is silent: filing both documents together and letting the next reader assume the note is authoritative because it is more detailed.

Where the file concerns a service area such as silicon recovery or wafer reclaim, the confirmation owner is usually the party who issued the written statement, not the person who took the meeting note. Naming that owner in the index is the single most useful field you can add, because it tells a later reader who can clarify the difference. Keep the entry factual and avoid characterising the supplier's position beyond what the document says.

Ask the confirmation owner to clarify, without renegotiating

A clarification request should be narrow. Its purpose is to resolve a documented difference, not to reopen the commercial discussion.

A workable structure for the request:

State the two documents by their references and dates. Quote the specific field that differs, in neutral terms. Ask one question: which statement should be treated as current? Ask what, if anything, the owner wants recorded as superseded. Give a date by which you will file the answer, as an internal target rather than a demand.

Keep the request short and avoid attaching the full meeting note if it contains internal commentary. Summarise the relevant line instead. This keeps the exchange focused and avoids circulating material that was never meant for the supplier.

If the topic touches a development programme, expect a limited answer. San Techno notes that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes for its power-module work remain confidential, so a clarification request on those points may reasonably return a scope statement rather than specifics. That is a valid answer and should be recorded as such.

Keep the file readable six months later

The test of a procurement file is whether a colleague who was not in the meeting can follow it. Three habits help.

First, keep a one-line index entry per document: label, author role, date, reference and status. Second, never overwrite an original; add a note beside it. Third, when a difference is resolved, record the resolution next to the original difference rather than deleting it, so the reasoning survives.

A short closing checklist, used as an example rather than a rule:

Both documents are labelled. The status decision is written in one sentence. The confirmation owner is named. Open questions are listed with the role expected to answer. Nothing confidential has been copied into a shared folder.

For teams that also handle botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand alongside silicon items, the same discipline applies: the meeting note explains, the confirmation states. Keeping those roles separate is what makes the file trustworthy when the next person opens it.
