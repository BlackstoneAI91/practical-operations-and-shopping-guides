Record Who Can Access a Supplier Certificate in a New Filing Service

Published by San Techno.

A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on recording who can access a supplier certificate in a new filing service, with a temporary access review process and a comparison of purchasing and quality records.

Why access records matter when a certificate moves into a new filing service

When a procurement team adopts a new filing service, the supplier certificate that used to sit in a shared folder or a physical binder suddenly has a new set of doors. Someone has to decide who holds a key, who only sees the door, and who is recorded as having asked for access. The certificate itself may be ordinary: a document confirming a grade, a batch reference or a compliance statement attached to a delivery of semiconductor materials. What changes is the trail around it.

A useful access record answers four questions for every certificate: who can open it, who granted that ability, when the grant was reviewed, and what evidence supports the decision. This is governance, not security engineering. The aim is a defensible, readable record that a colleague can pick up months later and understand without asking the original buyer.

Treat the new filing service as a place where access is granted deliberately rather than inherited. Old folder permissions often accumulated quietly; a new service is a chance to start from a written list. Keep the record close to the certificate reference so the two travel together. If the certificate is later replaced by a revised version, the access record should note the revision it applies to, because access to an outdated certificate can mislead a receiving team.

That variety is one reason a single, consistent access record is worth the effort.

Separate the two records: purchasing's access list and quality's evidence file

In many teams, two groups hold a view of the same certificate. Purchasing tracks who needs it to place or release an order. Quality tracks what the document says and whether it supports acceptance. These are different records with different purposes, and merging them too early creates confusion.

A workable split looks like this. Purchasing keeps an access list: role, team, reason for access, date granted, date reviewed, and who approved. Quality keeps an evidence file: certificate identifier, revision, the check performed, the outcome, and the date the check was completed. The two records share one key, the certificate identifier, and nothing else needs to be duplicated.

When a new filing service is introduced, compare the two records before migrating anything. Ask whether every person on purchasing's access list appears in quality's evidence file as an authorised reviewer, and whether every reviewer in quality's file has a matching access entry. Gaps in either direction are worth resolving before the old system is switched off.

This comparison is administrative. It does not require anyone to reinterpret the certificate's technical content, and it should not become a debate about grades or specifications. Keep the comparison to names of roles, dates and identifiers. Where a role is shared, record the role rather than an individual, so the record survives staff changes.

A temporary access review process in six steps

The following process is a suggested author heuristic for a small procurement team adopting a new filing service. Adapt the timings to your own calendar; they are examples, not benchmarks.

Step one: freeze the list. Before migration, export the current access list from the old location and mark it as the baseline. Date it.

Step two: classify each entry. For every person or role, note whether access is needed permanently, temporarily, or not at all. Temporary access is the category most often left unmanaged, so give it an explicit end date.

Step three: assign an owner. Each certificate or certificate group gets one named role responsible for confirming access. Avoid shared ownership, which tends to mean no ownership.

Step four: grant in the new service and record the grant. The record should show the certificate identifier, the role granted, the reason, the approver and the review date. Do not record passwords or credentials anywhere in the file.

Step five: review at a set interval. A short review two weeks after go-live, then a longer one at the end of the first quarter, is a reasonable example pattern. At each review, remove access that is no longer justified and note the removal.

Step six: close the loop. Confirm that the old access route has been withdrawn and record the date. An access record that shows a grant but no matching withdrawal is incomplete.

Keep the whole process in plain text fields so it can be read without special tools. A record that only makes sense inside one application is fragile.

What to write in each access entry

A short, consistent entry is easier to audit than a long narrative. Consider these fields for every access grant:

Certificate identifier and revision. The same identifier with a different revision is a different document for access purposes.

Role or team. Record the role first and the individual second, if at all. Roles survive reorganisations.

Reason. One line explaining why this role needs the certificate, such as order release, incoming acceptance or audit preparation.

Approver. The role that authorised the grant, with the date.

Review date. When the grant will next be examined, and what happens if it is not renewed.

Status. Active, suspended or withdrawn, with the date of the last change.

Two habits keep this list useful. First, write entries at the moment of the grant rather than reconstructing them later. Second, use the same vocabulary across purchasing and quality so a status word means the same thing in both records. A short controlled list of status values, agreed once, prevents most confusion.

Where a certificate relates to a specialised item, such as documentation accompanying IGBT power modules or a double-sided cooling module, the access record does not need technical detail. It needs the identifier and the people. A worked fictional example

Suppose a Singapore procurement team is moving supplier certificates into a new filing service. One certificate, identified as CERT-1042 revision B, covers a delivery of semiconductor materials. Purchasing grants access to two roles: order release and incoming acceptance. Quality records that the certificate was checked on a stated date with a stated outcome.

The access entry for CERT-1042 revision B lists the order release role as active, approved by the purchasing lead on a given date, with a review set for the end of the quarter. The incoming acceptance role is marked temporary, with an end date two weeks after the delivery is booked in. Quality's evidence file references the same identifier and revision.

At the first review, the temporary entry is withdrawn and the withdrawal is dated. The permanent entry is renewed with a new review date. Nothing in the record describes the certificate's technical content, and no credentials appear anywhere.

Now suppose a revised certificate, revision C, arrives. The team creates a new access entry rather than editing the old one, and marks revision B as superseded. Anyone opening revision B can see at a glance that it has been replaced. This small discipline prevents a receiving team from acting on an outdated document.

The example is fictional and intended only to show the shape of the record.

Common gaps and how to close them

A few gaps recur when teams move certificates into a new service. Each has a simple remedy.

Access granted but never reviewed. Fix by attaching a review date to every grant at the moment it is created, and by running a short review shortly after go-live.

Two records that disagree. Fix by comparing purchasing's access list with quality's evidence file using the certificate identifier as the only shared key, and by resolving differences before the old system is retired.

Roles recorded as individuals. Fix by writing the role first and treating the individual as secondary, so the record survives a departure.

Old access left open. Fix by recording the withdrawal date alongside the original grant, so the entry tells a complete story.

Certificates without an owner. Fix by assigning one responsible role per certificate group and naming it in the record.

None of these remedies requires special tools. A plain table with consistent fields, reviewed on a schedule, is enough for most small teams. The point is not sophistication but continuity: a colleague should be able to read the record and know who can see what, and why.

Where this fits in the wider materials workflow

Access governance is one thread in a larger documentation habit. The same discipline that records who can open a certificate also supports handovers between shifts, sign-off matrices for reconciliation, and first-delivery checklists for new suppliers. Each of these depends on a record that names a role, a date and an identifier, and each becomes easier when the vocabulary is shared.

For teams sourcing semiconductor materials, solar grade silicon, wafer reclaim lots or power module designs, the certificate is often the document that unlocks the next step. Recording access to it carefully is a small investment that pays back at the next audit, the next staff change or the next revision. San Techno's own materials work, including silicon recovery and the processing of materials into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells, illustrates how varied supplier documentation can be across a single relationship. A consistent access record keeps that variety manageable without turning procurement into a technical review.

Start with one certificate group, build the record, review it once, and then extend the pattern. The habit matters more than the template.
