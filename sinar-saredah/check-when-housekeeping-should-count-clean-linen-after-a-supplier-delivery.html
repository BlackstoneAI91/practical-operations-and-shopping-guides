When Housekeeping Should Count Clean Linen After a Supplier Delivery

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A receiving guide for Sarawak accommodation operators and venue managers: fix the count point, run a discrepancy path, keep a proof record and hand clean linen to housekeeping without double counting.

Why the count point decides everything

Clean linen arriving from a laundry service is easy to accept in a hurry. A van pulls up, bags or bundles come off, someone signs, and the linen disappears into a store room. Days later a shortfall appears and nobody can say whether it left the supplier short, was miscounted at the door, or was issued to a room and never logged.

The fix is not a better memory. It is a fixed count point: one named place and one named moment where the delivered quantity becomes the hotel's responsibility. Everything before that point belongs to the delivery paperwork. Everything after it belongs to housekeeping stock.

Choose the count point deliberately. Common options are the loading bay, the linen room doorway, or a marked table inside the store. The loading bay is fastest but exposed to weather and passing traffic. The linen room is calmer but may be cramped. A marked table inside the store is usually the most workable because it gives a flat, lit surface and keeps clean linen off the floor.

Write the count point into your receiving note, not just into someone's habit. If the count point moves, the record should say so. This matters because the count point is also the boundary for disputes: a bundle counted at the table is accepted, while a bundle still on the tailgate is not yet yours.

For operators in Kuching and Miri, where Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd provides laundry, dry cleaning and specialist cleaning alongside pickup and delivery, the same principle applies whether linen arrives by van or is dropped off. The count point is your internal decision, and it should be agreed before the first delivery, not argued over after a shortfall.

Build the receiving inspection in four steps

A receiving inspection does not need to be elaborate. Four steps cover most small and mid-sized properties.

Step one, match the paperwork. Before any bag is opened, compare the delivery note against your order: date, service type, and the number of bundles or bags. If the note lists piece counts, read them aloud with a second person present. If it lists weight only, note that the piece count will be established at the table.

Step two, count at the fixed point. Move each bundle to the count point, open it, and count by item type: sheets, pillowcases, bath towels, hand towels, bath mats, table linen. Count in the same order every time so a missed category is obvious. A tally sheet with pre-printed categories is faster than a blank page.

Step three, inspect condition as you count. Look for tears, heavy staining, colour transfer, missing buttons or hems, and items that are still damp. Damp linen is a condition issue, not a count issue, and should be separated immediately so it does not enter the clean shelf.

Step four, sign and transfer. Only after steps one to three should the receiving staff member sign the delivery note and move the linen into housekeeping stock. The signature is the transfer of responsibility, so it should come last.

A useful heuristic, not a rule from research: allow roughly one minute per ten pieces for counting and a quick visual check. Adjust after your first few deliveries and record the actual time so you can plan staffing around it.

The discrepancy path: what happens when the count does not match

A discrepancy is any difference between the delivery note and the count at the table. It might be a shortfall, a surplus, a wrong item type, or a condition problem. The important thing is that the path is written down before it happens.

Name one person who owns discrepancies. On a small property this may be the housekeeping supervisor. On a larger one it may be a linen room coordinator. The owner records the discrepancy on the receiving sheet, photographs the bundle as found, and notes the time. Photographs should show the bundle label and the counted items together, without guest details or staff faces.

Then decide the immediate action. Shortfalls usually mean the item stays on the discrepancy list and housekeeping plans around the gap. Surpluses are held separately until confirmed, because an unconfirmed surplus can become a phantom stock figure that hides a later shortfall. Condition problems are set aside for a separate conversation about whether the item returns to service.

Finally, close the loop. The discrepancy owner contacts the supplier through the agreed channel, quotes the delivery note reference, and records the response. A discrepancy that is logged but never closed is worse than no log, because it creates the impression of control without the substance.

Keep the discrepancy path short enough that a busy receiving clerk will actually follow it. Three fields, a photo and one contact step is usually enough for a small property.

Proof records that survive a staff change

Receiving records fail most often at handover: the person who counted goes off shift, and the next person cannot tell what was accepted. A proof record should be readable by someone who was not there.

At minimum, the record should carry the date and time of delivery, the delivery note reference, the count point used, the counted quantities by item type, the name of the person who counted, any discrepancy noted, and the signature that transferred responsibility. If a photo was taken, note that it exists and where it is stored, rather than relying on a phone camera roll.

Store the record where housekeeping can reach it, not only where finance can. A folder at the linen room and a digital copy in a shared drive is a workable combination for many small operators. The point is that the count is visible to the people who will feel the shortfall.

It also helps to keep the supplier's own paperwork alongside your receiving sheet. Sinar Saredah's customer terms and conditions apply when the company accepts items, linen and commercial fabrics from a customer, and they sit alongside quotations, invoices and written service confirmations. That means your delivery note and any written confirmation are part of the same record set, so file them together rather than in separate piles.

Transferring clean linen to housekeeping without double counting

The receiving count and the housekeeping issue count are two different events, and confusing them is a common source of phantom stock. Receiving answers the question: how much clean linen did we accept today? Issue answers: how much clean linen went to rooms today?

Keep them in separate columns or separate sheets. When linen moves from the store to a floor trolley, record the issue quantity against the room or floor, not against the delivery. The store balance is then the opening balance plus received minus issued, and it can be checked at any time.

A simple transfer slip helps. It names the store, the floor or area receiving the linen, the item types and quantities, and the person taking and giving. It does not need to be elaborate, but it should be filled in at the moment of transfer rather than reconstructed at the end of the shift.

For accommodation operators, this separation also makes it easier to spot patterns. If receiving counts are consistently correct but store balances drift, the issue step is the place to look. If receiving counts themselves vary, the count point or the counting method needs attention.

Venue managers can use the same split for table linen and event textiles, treating the store-to-event transfer as the issue step.

Supplier delivery versus pickup paperwork

Delivery and pickup are not mirror images, and the paperwork should not be treated as if they were. On a delivery, the supplier brings clean linen to you and your count point is the moment of acceptance. On a pickup, you hand soiled linen to the supplier and the count point is the moment it leaves your control.

That difference changes what you record. On pickup, the risk is that items are counted short at the supplier and you have no evidence of what left. So the pickup record should capture the count at handover, the bag or bundle labels, and the signature of the person who took the linen. Photographs of sealed bags with labels are useful here.

On delivery, the risk runs the other way: you accept a bundle without counting and discover the shortfall later. The delivery record therefore needs the opened count, not just the bag count.

Where a provider offers both pickup and delivery, as Sinar Saredah does as part of its laundry service, it is worth agreeing in writing which count each side performs and where. A short written confirmation avoids the assumption that the supplier counted the same thing you did. Keep that confirmation with your receiving records so the two counts can be compared later.

A one-page receiving worksheet you can adapt

The worksheet below is an example format, not a standard. Adapt the fields to your property and the item types you actually handle.

Header fields: date, time, delivery note reference, supplier, count point used, receiving staff name.

Count table: item type, quantity on note, quantity counted, difference, condition flag. Use one row per item type and leave the difference column blank until counted.

Discrepancy block: description, photo taken yes or no, action agreed, owner, date closed.

Transfer block: signature, time signed, store location, housekeeping recipient.

Pickup block, if applicable: bags out, items counted at handover, signature of collector.

A short note at the foot of the sheet can remind staff that the signature transfers responsibility and should be the last action. Keep the sheet to one page so it is used rather than filed away.

Review the worksheet after the first month. If a field is never filled in, either remove it or find out why. A worksheet that reflects real practice is more useful than a comprehensive one that is ignored.
