Comparing Laundry Cost per Room Night: A Worksheet for Sarawak Accommodation Operators

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical worksheet that converts laundry and commercial cleaning quotes into a cost per room night, so in-house and outsourced options can be compared on the same basis, with notes for households and venue managers in Kuching and Miri.

Why cost per room night beats a headline quote

A laundry quote usually arrives as a rate per kilogram, per piece, or per bag. None of those numbers can be compared directly with an in-house wage bill, and they cannot be compared with each other unless the underlying volume is the same. Cost per room night fixes that problem by dividing the total laundry cost over a defined period by the number of occupied room nights in the same period. The result is a single figure that survives changes in linen mix, occupancy and pickup frequency.

The method is simple but it needs discipline. Decide the period first, for example one calendar month or one four-week block. Count occupied room nights from your booking records, not from the number of rooms you own. Then total every laundry-related cost in that period: the invoice, any delivery charge, the labour hours your own staff spend sorting and counting, and the replacement cost of linen that was retired. Divide the total by the room nights. Write the figure down with the period beside it, because a cost per room night without a period is not comparable to anything.

This worksheet is a planning tool, not a benchmark. Your figure is only meaningful against your own past months or against another option measured the same way. Treat any suggested timings below as author heuristics for planning, not as established research.

Building the input sheet before you request quotes

Collect the inputs before you talk to any provider, because a quote is only as good as the volume description behind it. Build a simple sheet with these columns.

Room nights in the period. Taken from your occupancy record.

Linen units per room night. Count what a standard room turn actually consumes: sheets, pillowcases, towels, bath mats, duvet covers. Do this for each room type rather than averaging across the property, because a family room and a single room do not consume the same volume.

Weight or piece count per unit. If your provider quotes by weight, you need an approximate weight per room night. Weigh one full set of standard linen on a kitchen scale and multiply. If the provider quotes per piece, count pieces instead.

Turnaround requirement. How many hours pass between checkout and the next check-in? This determines whether you need same-day return, which may change the option you choose.

Your own handling hours. Sorting, counting, bagging and storing linen takes staff time. Record it honestly, even if it is absorbed into a housekeeping shift.

Replacement and retirement. Note how many items were retired in the period and what they cost to replace. Linen loss is part of the true cost and is easy to forget.

Once the sheet is filled, the same inputs can be sent to more than one provider, which makes the returned quotes comparable. Converting each option into the same unit

With the input sheet ready, convert each option using the same arithmetic so the comparison is fair.

Outsourced per-piece or per-kilogram quote. Multiply the quoted rate by the volume from your input sheet to get a period cost. Add any delivery charge and any minimum order charge. Add your own handling hours valued at your internal hourly cost. Add linen replacement. Divide by room nights.

In-house operation. Total the wages and hours of staff who handle laundry, the share of utilities attributable to laundry, detergent and consumables, machine maintenance and depreciation, and linen replacement. Divide by room nights. In-house figures are often understated because utilities and maintenance are shared across the building, so decide a defensible allocation and keep it consistent between periods.

Mixed option. Some operators send standard linen out and handle specialist items in-house, or the reverse. Split the volume between the two routes and calculate each separately, then add the two cost per room night figures.

A worked fictional example makes the arithmetic concrete. Suppose a small guesthouse records 300 room nights in a month and sends out 1,200 pieces of linen at a quoted per-piece rate, with a delivery charge and 20 staff hours of sorting. The period cost is the invoice plus delivery plus the valued sorting hours plus replacement of retired items. Dividing that total by 300 gives the cost per room night for the outsourced route. The same guesthouse then estimates its in-house alternative on the same 300 room nights. The two figures are now directly comparable, and the difference can be discussed in absolute terms rather than as a percentage of an unrelated quote.

Keep the working visible. If a provider later changes a rate or a minimum charge, you can update one cell and see the effect immediately.

Where specialist cleaning sits in the comparison

Standard linen usually dominates the volume, but it is not the only cleaning cost an accommodation operator carries. Curtains, carpets and soft furnishings are cleaned less often, and their cost does not divide neatly by room night. The practical approach is to keep them in a separate line and amortise them across the period you expect between cleans.

For example, if a set of guest room curtains is cleaned on a cycle you decide in advance, divide the quoted cost by the number of room nights the room is expected to earn before the next clean. That gives an amortised per-room-night figure you can add to the linen figure. The same logic applies to carpet cleaning in corridors and rooms. The curtain cleaning decision guide and the carpet cleaning preparation checklist on this site cover the operational side of those jobs; this page only handles the cost allocation.

Specialist items such as wedding gowns, shoes and soft toys are usually household or venue costs rather than room-night costs, so keep them out of the accommodation comparison entirely. Mixing them in distorts the figure. If you operate a venue that also hosts events, run a separate small sheet for event-related textile costs rather than folding them into room nights.

A scoring worksheet for the non-cost factors

Cost per room night is necessary but not sufficient. Two options can land on the same figure and still differ in ways that matter operationally. Add a short scoring block beside the cost columns, using a simple scale you define yourself, for example one to five, and record the reason for each score.

Turnaround reliability. How often did the option return linen within the window you need? Use your own records if you have them, or ask for a trial period before committing.

Count accuracy. Did the pieces returned match the pieces sent? A shortfall has a replacement cost that belongs back in the cost column.

Condition handling. How are stained or torn items flagged and separated? A clear rejection log saves arguments later.

Flexibility. Can volume rise for a peak weekend without renegotiating the whole arrangement?

Communication. Is there a named contact and a predictable way to raise a problem?

Access and scheduling. Does the pickup window fit your housekeeping shift and your building access?

Score each factor for each option, then look at the cost column and the score block together. An option that is slightly higher per room night but scores well on count accuracy and turnaround may be the better operational choice. An option that is cheapest on paper but scores poorly on condition handling carries hidden replacement cost. The scores are your judgement, not a published standard, so state that when you share the sheet with a partner or manager.

Running a short trial before you commit

A worksheet predicts; a trial confirms. Before committing to a full arrangement, run a defined trial period, for example two to four weeks, with the same measurement discipline as the main sheet. This is an author heuristic for planning, not a researched recommendation.

Set the trial scope in writing: which room types, which linen items, which pickup days and which return window. Record the volume sent and returned each day. Note any shortfall, any item returned in unacceptable condition, and any day the return window was missed. At the end of the trial, recalculate the cost per room night using actual invoices and actual handling hours rather than the quoted rate. Compare that figure with your in-house baseline and with any other option you trialled.

Decide the pass criteria before the trial starts, so the decision is not made on impressions. A simple rule works: the option must meet the return window on an agreed proportion of days, keep count variance within a tolerance you set, and land within an agreed range of your baseline cost per room night. If it fails one criterion, discuss whether the cause is fixable before ending the trial.

Turning the comparison into a decision record

The final step is to write a short decision record so the comparison can be revisited rather than rebuilt. Keep it to one page and include the period, the room nights, the volume assumptions, the cost per room night for each option, the score block and the trial result if one was run.

Record the assumptions explicitly, especially the weight per room night and the internal hourly cost used to value handling time. These are the two figures most likely to be challenged later, and stating them makes the comparison auditable. Note the date of each quote, because rates and minimum charges change.

Set a review date. A quarterly review is a reasonable starting point for a small property, adjusted to your own seasonality. At each review, update the volume and the rates, recalculate, and note what changed. Over a few cycles you will have a record of how your laundry service cost per room night moves with occupancy, which is more useful than a single snapshot.

Finally, keep the operational documents close to the cost sheet. The terms and conditions, the quotation and the trial notes together tell the full story of why an option was chosen. If a future manager asks why the property outsources rather than runs in-house, the decision record answers the question with figures rather than recollection.
