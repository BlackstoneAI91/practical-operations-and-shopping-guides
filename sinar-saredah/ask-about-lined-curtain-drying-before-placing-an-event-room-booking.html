Ask About Lined Curtain Drying Before Placing an Event-Room Booking

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical question set for venue managers and accommodation operators in Sarawak who need to know how lined curtains will be dried, what the provider will report, and which booking decisions should wait for answers.

Why the drying question belongs in the booking conversation

An event-room booking usually locks in a date, a layout and a set of access windows. Curtains are rarely part of that conversation until someone notices a mark near the stage or a musty smell in a corner room. By then the room is committed and the cleaning has to fit around rehearsals, setups and guest arrivals.

The drying stage is the part most likely to disturb that schedule, because it is the step that decides when the curtains can go back up. A lined curtain carries two layers of fabric plus the lining itself, and the way a provider plans to dry it affects how long the room stays out of use and what the venue needs to prepare. Asking early is not a sign of doubt about the provider. It is the same discipline as asking when the stage will be free or when the caterer needs the service corridor.

This guide gives you a question set rather than a technical answer. It assumes you are comparing what a provider agrees to report, what your own team can inspect, and which booking decisions should wait until you have information. It deliberately does not tell you which drying method to choose, because that depends on the fabric, the lining and the provider's own assessment.

Six questions to put to the provider in writing

Treat these as a starting set and adapt them to your room. Written answers are easier to compare across providers and easier to hand to whoever approves the booking.

First, ask how the provider plans to dry the curtains and whether that plan changes for lined panels. You are not asking for a guarantee, only for the intended approach and what would make them change it.

Second, ask what they will tell you when drying is complete. A useful answer names the person who reports, the form the report takes, and whether it is verbal, a note on the collection document or a separate written confirmation.

Third, ask what condition they will record before cleaning starts. Existing fading, water marks, seam strain and hem wear are the things you want noted while the curtains are still hanging, not discovered after rehang.

Fourth, ask what happens if drying takes longer than expected. Who contacts whom, and how much notice you should expect?

Fifth, ask which parts of the work sit with the provider and which sit with your team, such as taking down the curtains, labelling panels by window, and rehanging them.

Sixth, ask what the provider needs from you before the room can be released. Access times, a contact who can approve changes, and a decision on whether the room must stay closed are all reasonable items to settle in advance.

If you are also arranging broader cleaning for the same venue, keep the curtain questions separate from the general scope discussion so neither gets lost. A commercial cleaning scope conversation covers different ground from a textile handover.

What the venue can inspect before and after

The provider's report is only half the record. Your own inspection gives you something to compare it against, and it costs little to prepare.

Before the curtains come down, walk the room with a phone or a notebook and record each window in order. Note the panel count, the heading type, the tie-back positions and any marks you can already see. Photograph the top fixing and the hem at floor level, because those are the areas most likely to shift during handling. If the room has a blackout layer behind the main curtain, record that too, since it changes what the provider is handling.

After rehang, repeat the same walk in the same order. Check that panels hang to the same length, that the lining does not show below the face fabric, and that the curtains still close without strain. Note anything that looks different from your before record.

This is where a simple comparison beats a long report. Two photographs of the same window, taken from the same spot, tell you more than a paragraph of description. If something looks wrong, you have a dated record to raise with the provider rather than a memory of how the room used to look.

Keep the before and after records with the booking file. If the same room is booked again next season, the earlier record becomes your baseline.

Decisions that should wait for the provider's answer

Not every booking decision needs to wait. Dates, catering and seating plans can usually proceed. The decisions below are the ones where an early commitment can be expensive to unwind.

Hold the room-release date until you know the expected drying window. If the provider cannot give a date, ask for the point at which they will confirm one, and build that into your own calendar.

Hold any promise that the room will be available for a specific setup time on the day after collection. Drying is the variable, and a setup promise made before you have the answer is a promise you may have to break.

Hold approval of the final invoice until the written confirmation matches what you observed on rehang. This is not a dispute tactic; it is the normal sequence when terms, quotations and service confirmations are meant to work together.

Hold any decision to clean only part of the room's curtains. Mixing freshly cleaned panels with older ones in the same window can make a colour or length difference more obvious, and that is worth seeing before you commit.

Everything else can move ahead. The point of the question set is to protect a small number of decisions, not to stall the whole booking.

A one-page booking worksheet

Use a single sheet per room so the answers stay together. The fields below are an example format, not a required standard.

Room name and booking reference. Window count and panel count. Date curtains come down and date they are expected back. Named provider contact and named venue contact. Drying approach as described by the provider, in their words. Report format agreed, and who receives it. Condition notes recorded before collection, with photo references. Rehang responsibility. Room-release date confirmed yes or no. Open questions still outstanding, with an owner and a date to chase.

Score the sheet only if it helps you compare rooms. A simple three-column mark such as answered, partly answered and not answered is enough. Treat any scoring as your own working heuristic rather than an industry measure.

When the sheet is complete, attach the provider's written reply and your before photographs. That bundle is what you hand to whoever signs off the booking. It also gives the next person a starting point if the room is booked again.

If your venue also runs accommodation, the same worksheet can sit alongside your housekeeping records, so a curtain job does not disappear into a separate folder.

Where this fits alongside other venue cleaning work

Curtains are one textile in a room that also contains carpets, upholstery and loose furnishings. Keeping them in separate conversations helps, because each has its own handling and drying considerations.

If the event room shares corridors and stairwells with the rest of the building, the general cleaning scope is a different discussion from the curtain handover. Grouping them into one meeting tends to bury the curtain questions under a longer agenda.
