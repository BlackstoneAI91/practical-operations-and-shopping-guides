Curtain Cleaning While Guests Occupy the Building: A Room-by-Room Sequencing Sheet

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical sequencing sheet for accommodation operators and venue managers in Sarawak who need curtain cleaning while guests occupy the building, covering takedown windows, temporary covering and rehang checks.

Why sequencing matters more than speed

When a guest building stays open during curtain cleaning, the constraint is not the cleaning itself. It is the number of rooms that can be out of service at any one time, and the length of each window. A room with bare windows is not lettable, so every takedown creates a temporary hole in your inventory. The useful planning question is therefore: how many rooms can be without curtains at once, and for how long?

Start by writing down three numbers for each room: the earliest time housekeeping can enter after checkout, the latest time the room must be ready before the next arrival, and the number of spare rooms you can hold as a buffer. Those three numbers define your window. Everything else in this sheet fits inside it.

Treat the window as fixed and the sequence as flexible. If a window is too short for takedown, cleaning and rehang in one day, split it across two days with a temporary covering in between. That is usually better than compressing the work into a rushed evening. Build the room register before booking anything

A sequencing sheet only works if it describes real rooms. Walk the building and record, for each room, the curtain type, the number of panels, the fixing method, the track or rod condition, and whether the window faces a public area where a bare window would be visible from outside.

Add two practical fields: who holds the key, and where the removed curtains will be staged before collection. Staging space is often the bottleneck in small properties. A corridor corner that blocks a fire exit is not a staging area; a locked store room with a labelled rail is.

Group rooms by similarity rather than by floor number. Rooms with the same curtain type and fixing method can share a takedown method and a rehang check, which shortens the briefing for housekeeping. Rooms with unusual fixings, heavy linings or difficult access should be scheduled separately, ideally not on the same day as a full floor.

Finally, mark which rooms have back-to-back bookings in the planning period. Those rooms should either be scheduled first, while buffer rooms are still free, or deferred to a quieter week.

Choose a takedown window that housekeeping can actually meet

The takedown window is the period between a guest leaving and the room needing to look presentable again. In an occupied building, that window is usually short and shared with bed-making, bathroom cleaning and linen changes.

A workable pattern is to take down curtains only after the room has been stripped and cleaned, so the person removing them is not stepping around wet floors. Remove hooks or rings into a labelled bag that stays with the panel set, and note any bent hooks, missing rings or damaged track gliders on the room register. Those notes protect both sides of the handover conversation later.

If the property uses a pickup and delivery arrangement, align the takedown day with the collection day rather than letting bagged curtains sit in a corridor for two nights. Confirm the collection point and the person who will hand items over. Temporary covering: the part most plans forget

A room with no curtains is not simply unfinished. It is a privacy problem, a light problem and, in some rooms, a heat problem. Temporary covering needs to be planned before the first panel comes down, not improvised on the day.

Decide per room whether the temporary option is a spare set held in store, a tension rod with a plain panel, or a room closure. Room closure is the simplest and safest choice where the window is large, the fixing is unusual, or the room faces a neighbouring building. Where a temporary panel is used, check that it does not pull on the existing track and that it can be removed quickly for the rehang.

Record the temporary covering on the room register with the date it went up and who installed it. That record matters if a guest is moved or a room is reopened early. It also stops a temporary panel from quietly becoming permanent.

Keep the temporary covering neutral in colour and simple in fixing. Elaborate temporary arrangements take longer to install and remove than the cleaning window can absorb.

Rehang windows and the checks that close them

Rehang is where sequencing either holds or collapses. Curtains that return from cleaning may hang differently for a short period, and the hardware has usually been handled more than anyone planned for. Build a rehang window that is separate from the cleaning window, even if it is only a few hours.

At rehang, work through a short check for each room: all panels present and matched to the room register, hooks and rings accounted for, track and gliders moving freely, hem clear of the floor, and the window covering the full opening when drawn. Note anything that needs attention rather than adjusting it silently, because a track problem found in one room often appears in others on the same floor.

Only after this check should the room be returned to letting. A room that looks finished but has a stiff track will generate a maintenance request from the next guest.

Where a set does not hang correctly, keep the room on temporary covering and log the issue with the handover paperwork. A worked example: a small guesthouse with eight rooms

The following example is fictional and is offered as a planning illustration, not as a recommendation for any particular property.

A guesthouse has eight rooms, all with similar two-panel curtains on ceiling tracks. Two rooms are held as buffer. The manager decides that no more than two rooms may be without curtains at once. Each room's window runs from mid-morning checkout to late-afternoon arrival.

Day one: rooms one and two are taken down after cleaning, temporary panels go up, and the curtain sets are staged for collection. Day two: rooms three and four follow the same pattern while rooms one and two are rehung and checked. The cycle continues until all eight are done, with the buffer rooms absorbing any overrun.

The example shows the core rule: the number of rooms in progress at any time is set by the buffer, not by the size of the building. A larger property with more buffer rooms can run more sets in parallel; a small property with no buffer should run one pair at a time and accept a longer calendar.

Write your own version of this example with your real room numbers before booking. It is much easier to adjust a plan on paper than to explain a bare window to a guest.

Handover paperwork and the questions to settle in advance

The sequencing sheet is only as good as the handover around it. Before the first set leaves, settle a small number of questions in writing: which rooms are in scope, how sets are labelled, who signs items out and back in, what happens if a set returns later than expected, and who is authorised to approve a change to the schedule.

Label each set by room number and panel count, and keep the label with the set through cleaning and return. A mismatch discovered at rehang is far more disruptive than one caught at collection.
