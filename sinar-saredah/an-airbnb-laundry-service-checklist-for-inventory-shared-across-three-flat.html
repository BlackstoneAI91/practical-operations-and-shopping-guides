An Airbnb Laundry Service Checklist for Inventory Shared Across Three Flats

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical checklist for short-stay hosts running three flats on one shared linen pool: bag IDs, property-by-property delivery, turn tracking and a towel mix-up example you can plan around.

Why a shared linen pool needs its own checklist

Three flats, one linen cupboard and one laundry cycle sounds efficient until a towel set comes back to the wrong door. The problem is rarely the washing itself. It is that the inventory is shared while the guests are not. Flat A's bath towels can physically serve Flat B, so nothing in the fabric tells you where a piece belongs once it leaves the building.

A checklist fixes this by separating three questions that hosts often blur together: what is dirty, what is clean, and what is where. Dirty linen is a collection problem. Clean linen is a storage problem. Placement is a delivery problem. Each needs its own line on the sheet, and each needs a name against it.

This guide is written for Sarawak households, accommodation operators and venue managers running a small short-stay portfolio. It assumes one low-use flat and two flats that turn frequently, which is the pattern that creates the most confusion: the quiet flat's linen sits in the pool for weeks, while the busy flats cycle through stock daily. Step 1: Count the pool before you count the guests

Before any bag leaves a flat, write down the total pool. Not the number of towels you think you own, but the number you can physically find. Do this once at the start and then on a fixed day each month.

A simple pool sheet has one row per item type and one column per flat, plus a column for stock in transit or in storage. For a three-flat setup, a workable starting list is: bath towels, hand towels, face towels, bath mats, bed sheet sets, pillowcases, duvet covers, kitchen cloths and any soft furnishings you launder between guests. Add a column for items awaiting repair, because a torn sheet that is still counted as available will quietly shrink your buffer.

The low-use flat is the trap here. Its linen may sit clean and folded for a long stretch, so it looks accounted for while actually being unavailable to the two busy flats. Decide in advance whether the quiet flat holds its own reserve or draws from the shared pool. Write the decision down. A pool sheet that everyone interprets differently is worse than no sheet, because it creates false confidence.

Keep the count honest by recording what left and what returned, not what should have. If a guest took a towel, the pool is smaller, and the next turnover needs to know that before it starts.

Step 2: Give every bag an ID that survives the trip

Bag labels are the weakest link in a shared linen chain. A bag with a flat name written in marker will fade, smudge or be reused for the wrong flat. Use a two-part ID instead: a property code plus a sequence number, for example a short code for the flat followed by the bag's number in that week's cycle.

Record the ID in a bag log with four fields: bag ID, property, date out, date expected back. Add a fifth field for the count inside the bag, because a bag that returns with the right ID but the wrong count is the failure mode that a label alone cannot catch.

Two practical rules keep IDs usable. First, never reuse a bag ID within the same month, even if the bag itself is reused. Second, photograph the bag log page at handoff if you are passing linen to a driver or a counter. The photo is not evidence of quality; it is a timestamp that settles arguments about when something left.

If you use an outside laundry, ask how they record your bags and whether their reference matches yours. Step 3: Separate the two frequent turns from the low-use flat

The two busy flats should run on a predictable cycle, and the quiet flat should run on a separate one. Mixing them is how a low-use flat's linen ends up in a busy flat's cupboard and never comes back.

For the frequent-turn flats, set a collection and return rhythm tied to your checkout pattern rather than to a fixed weekday. If both flats turn on the same day, stagger the bag handoffs so the laundry receives two clearly separate bags, not one combined load. Combined loads are cheaper to move and much harder to split correctly on return.

For the low-use flat, batch its linen into a single scheduled wash rather than sending a few pieces whenever a guest leaves. Fewer, larger cycles mean fewer bag IDs to track and fewer chances to misplace a set.

A useful heuristic, not a rule from research: keep at least one full spare set per bed in the busy flats and two spare sets in the quiet flat, so a late return never forces a same-day scramble. Adjust the number to your own booking pattern and write the figure on the pool sheet so it is a decision rather than a hope.

Step 4: Deliver property by property, not pile by pile

The return trip is where shared inventory breaks. A driver with three bags and three flats will naturally unload the nearest bag first, and if the bags are not clearly coded, the towels go to whichever door opens first.

Build the delivery step into the checklist as a sequence, not an afterthought. Each bag is opened at the flat it belongs to, counted against the bag log, and signed off before the next bag is touched. If a count is short, note it on the spot rather than at the end of the round, because by then nobody remembers which bag was opened where.

A worked example shows the failure clearly. Suppose Flat A and Flat B both send bath towels in the same week, and the bags are labelled only by colour. The laundry returns two bundles. The driver delivers the bundle with the blue tag to Flat A, but the blue tag was Flat B's this month because the tags were swapped after a repair. Flat A now holds Flat B's towels, Flat B is short, and the next guest in Flat B finds three towels instead of four. Nothing was lost. Everything was simply in the wrong place, and the pool sheet will not show it until the monthly count.

Property-by-property delivery with a count at each door prevents that specific error. It also gives you a clean handoff record if you use a pickup and delivery service, because each stop produces its own confirmation instead of one signature for a whole round.

Step 5: A one-page checklist you can reuse

The checklist below is a template. Copy it into a notebook or a shared document and adapt the item types to your own flats. It is deliberately short enough to complete at every handoff.

Before collection: pool sheet updated with current counts; bag ID assigned and written on the bag and in the log; item count recorded inside the bag; property code confirmed against the flat, not against memory.

At handoff: bag log photographed; expected return date written; any known stains or damage noted in plain words; special items separated from general linen.

On return: bag ID matched to the log; count checked against the recorded number; shortfalls noted at the door; clean linen stored away from dirty linen; pool sheet updated the same day.

Monthly: full physical count across all three flats; items awaiting repair moved to a separate column; spare-set buffer reviewed against the coming month's bookings.

Two habits make the template stick. Update the pool sheet the same day, not at month end. And keep the bag log in one place, so a relief cleaner or a family member covering a turnover can read it without asking.

If your flats also contain curtains, rugs or soft furnishings that need periodic attention, those belong on a separate schedule rather than in the linen cycle, because they are removed less often and take longer to return.

Where an outside laundry fits, and what to agree first

A shared pool across three flats is manageable in-house until the busy season, when two turnovers land on the same morning. That is the point to decide what goes out and what stays in.

General linen, towels and bedding are the natural candidates for an outside laundry service. What to review after the first month

Run the checklist for one month, then review it against what actually went wrong. The review is more useful than the template, because your three flats will have their own pattern of turns.

Ask three questions. Which bag ID was hardest to trace, and why? Which flat was short at a turnover, and was the cause a late return or a misdelivery? Did the monthly count match the pool sheet, and if not, which column drifted?

Adjust one thing at a time. If misdeliveries dominate, tighten the property-by-property delivery step. If late returns dominate, revisit the buffer stock figure. If counts drift, the problem is usually that the pool sheet is being updated at month end rather than at handoff.

Keep the checklist short enough that a busy host will actually complete it. A four-field bag log that is always filled in beats a twelve-field form that is abandoned in the second week. The goal is not a perfect system. It is a shared pool where every towel has a known home, and where a mix-up between flats is caught at the door rather than by the next guest.
