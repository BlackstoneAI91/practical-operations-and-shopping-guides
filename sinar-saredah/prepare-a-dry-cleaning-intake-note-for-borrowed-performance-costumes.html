Preparing a Dry Cleaning Intake Note for Borrowed Performance Costumes

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical guide for Sarawak households, accommodation operators and venue managers who need to hand borrowed performance costumes to a dry cleaning provider with the owner's constraints, return dates and approval limits recorded in writing.

Why a Borrowed Costume Needs Its Own Intake Note

A costume you own can be cleaned on your own judgement. A costume you borrowed carries a second party's expectations, and those expectations usually live in a message thread rather than on paper. When the item leaves your hands for dry cleaning, the note that travels with it becomes the only record of what the owner permitted, what they forbade, and when the item must be back.

The intake note is not a fabric inventory. You are not expected to identify fibre content or predict how a trim will behave. The note records permission and ownership: who owns the costume, who authorised cleaning, what the owner said about finishes and alterations, and the date the item must return to them. Everything else is a question for the provider to answer before work begins.

This matters most for venue managers and accommodation operators who hold borrowed pieces between events. A costume left in a changing room after a performance is easy to misplace in a busy week. A one-page note attached to the bag keeps the ownership chain visible even when the person who borrowed it is not the person doing the handover.

The Four Fields That Do the Real Work

Most intake notes fail because they try to describe the garment instead of the arrangement. Keep the note short and concentrate on four fields.

Owner and authoriser. Name the owner of the costume and the person who gave permission to clean it. If those are different people, say so. A production wardrobe coordinator may own the piece while a venue manager authorises the clean. Record both, with the date permission was given.

Return date and consequence. Write the date the costume must be back with the owner, not the date you would like it back. Add one line on what happens if the date cannot be met, such as holding the item rather than proceeding. This is the field most often left blank, and it is the one that decides whether a provider should start work at all.

Finish preferences. Record only what the owner actually said. If they asked for the costume to be returned on its original hanger, write that. If they said nothing about pressing or folding, write nothing rather than guessing.

Changes needing approval. List the specific decisions that must come back to the owner before anyone acts: shortening, replacing a fastening, removing a decoration, or any repair. A provider cannot know your owner's limits unless you write them down.

A fifth field is optional but useful: a condition note made at the moment of borrowing, with the date. It separates wear that existed before the clean from anything raised later.

Separating Permission From Fabric Questions

It is tempting to fill the intake note with guesses about what the costume is made of. Resist that. Fabric questions belong to the provider, and permission questions belong to the owner. Mixing them produces a note that is confidently wrong in both directions.

Put the permission items on the note and put the open questions in a short list underneath, addressed to the provider. For example: the owner has approved dry cleaning but has not commented on the decorative trim; please advise whether the trim needs separate handling before proceeding. That sentence does the work without you claiming expertise you do not have.

This split also protects the relationship with the owner. If you write "dry clean only" on a note when the owner never said that, you have created a record that misrepresents them. If instead you write "owner approved dry cleaning on this date" and attach the message, the record is accurate and traceable.

Where a costume includes accessories that are not fabric, such as a separate headpiece or a pair of performance shoes, treat them as separate line items. A Worked Example: The Borrowed Costume Circular

The following is fictional and intended only to show the shape of a completed note.

A community hall in Sarawak hosts a weekend performance. The organiser borrows six costumes from a theatre group in another town. Two come back with marks on the sleeve. The organiser writes one intake note per affected costume and takes both to a dry cleaning counter.

Note one reads: owner, theatre group wardrobe; authorised by, group coordinator, message dated the Monday after the performance; return required by the following Friday because the group tours again; finish preference, returned on the hanger supplied; changes needing approval, none permitted without written reply from the coordinator; open question, the coordinator has not commented on the beaded cuff, please advise before proceeding.

Note two reads the same except the open question concerns a detachable sash, and the coordinator has already said the sash may be cleaned separately.

Notice what the notes do not contain. There is no claim about fibre, no instruction about temperature, and no promise about outcome. The organiser has recorded authority, deadline, preference and the approval boundary, and has left the technical judgement to the provider. That is the whole point of the format.

Keep a copy of each note. If the owner later asks why a decision was made, the copy answers the question without relying on memory.

Handover Mechanics for Venue and Accommodation Staff

The note is only useful if it travels with the item and survives the handover. A few practical habits make that reliable.

Attach the note to the bag rather than placing it inside. A note inside a bag is separated from the item the moment someone opens it for sorting. A note in a clear sleeve on the outside stays with the bag through the whole journey.

Count the pieces in front of the person receiving them and write the count on the note. Costumes often include small separate parts, and a count is the simplest way to catch a missing piece before it becomes a dispute.

Record who handed the item over and when. If a driver collects, the note should name the collection point and the date. Sinar Saredah's services include pickup and delivery, so a borrowed costume may never reach a counter at all; the note has to work on a collection run as well as at a branch.

Keep the owner's approval message with your copy of the note. A note that says permission was given is stronger when the message is attached, and it costs nothing to keep both together.

Finally, decide in advance who receives the returned costume and who confirms its condition. If the borrower and the receiver are different people, write both names on the note so the return does not stall.

A Short Pre-Handover Checklist

Run this list before the costume leaves your premises. It is an author's checklist, not a published standard, and it should take a few minutes rather than an afternoon.

One: is the owner named, and is the authorising person named separately if they differ? Two: is the permission dated? Three: is the return date written as a date, not as "soon"? Four: have you recorded only finish preferences the owner actually stated? Five: have you listed the changes that need approval, including repairs and removals? Six: have you written your open questions as questions to the provider rather than as instructions? Seven: is the piece count on the note? Eight: is the note attached to the outside of the bag? Nine: do you have a copy, with the approval message kept alongside it?

If any answer is no, fix that field before handover. A note with a missing return date is worse than no note, because it looks complete.

For households rather than venues, the same checklist applies with one change: the owner is often a family member or friend, and the permission may be verbal. Write down what was said and when, and confirm it in a message before the item goes for cleaning.

Where the Note Fits Alongside the Provider's Terms

Your intake note records your side of the arrangement. The provider's terms record theirs, and the two are not substitutes. Sinar Saredah's customer terms apply when it accepts garments and other items for cleaning, and they sit alongside quotations, invoices, branch notices and written service confirmations. That means a written confirmation from the branch is part of the record too, and it is worth keeping with your copy of the note.

A practical habit is to ask, at handover, whether anything on your note needs to be restated in the provider's own paperwork. If the return date matters, have it appear in both places. If an approval boundary matters, have the provider confirm in writing that they will come back to you before acting.

This is also where the distinction between laundry and dry cleaning becomes useful to state plainly. A borrowed costume that the owner has approved for dry cleaning should be logged as such on the note, and a separate bag of ordinary household laundry should not be mixed into the same handover. Keeping the borrowed item on its own note and its own bag means the owner's constraints are never buried under a general load.
