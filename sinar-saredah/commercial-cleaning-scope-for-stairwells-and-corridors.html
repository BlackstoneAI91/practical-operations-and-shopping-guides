Scoping Commercial Cleaning for Stairwells and Corridors: A Zone-by-Zone Worksheet

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical guide for Sarawak households, accommodation operators and venue managers on scoping commercial cleaning for stairwells and corridors, with a zone sheet, frequency options and a sign-off column for shared buildings.

Why stairwells and corridors need their own scope

Shared circulation areas are the parts of a building every occupant passes through but no single occupant owns. That is exactly why they are easy to under-specify. A quotation that lists 'common areas' as one line gives both sides very little to work with when a handrail feels tacky or a landing collects tracked-in grit.

A better approach is to treat the stairwell and corridor as a set of zones, each with its own surfaces, its own cleaning action and its own frequency. This turns a vague promise into a checklist that a supervisor can walk, a cleaner can follow and a building manager can sign.

Building the zone list before you talk frequency

Start with a walk, not a spreadsheet. Walk the route a resident or guest actually takes from the entrance to the furthest door and note every distinct surface. A workable zone list for a typical shared building usually includes:

Entry threshold and lobby floor. The transition point where outdoor grit arrives.

Stair treads and nosings. High-contact, high-wear, and awkward to reach.

Handrails and balustrades. Touched by nearly everyone, so worth naming separately from the stairs.

Landings and corridor floors. The largest visible area and the one occupants judge first.

Corridor walls up to hand height. Scuffs and marks concentrate here.

Lift lobby and lift interior, if present. Small area, high visibility.

Door frames, handles and push plates. Frequently touched, rarely listed.

Light switches, signage and notice boards. Easy to miss, easy to notice when missed.

Refuse or utility alcoves opening onto the corridor. A source of odour and tracked soil.

Windows, ledges and ventilation grilles in the corridor. Often excluded by default.

Write each zone as a row, not a paragraph. One row per surface keeps the scope honest, because a provider can price a row and a manager can check it. If a zone is deliberately excluded, say so in the sheet rather than leaving it blank; an explicit exclusion prevents the assumption that it was included.

Choosing a frequency that matches use, not habit

Frequency is where scoping conversations most often drift. A weekly figure copied from another building tells you little about your own. Instead, attach a frequency option to each zone and record the reason.

A simple three-tier structure works for most shared buildings:

Daily or per-operating-day. Best suited to entry floors, handrails, lift interiors and high-touch fittings in buildings with steady foot traffic.

Weekly. A reasonable starting point for stair treads, landings, corridor walls at hand height and door frames in lower-traffic buildings.

Periodic or deep-clean cycle. Suited to windows, ledges, ventilation grilles, alcove build-up and surfaces that need more time than a routine visit allows.

For each zone, note the trigger that would justify moving up a tier: visible soil at a set inspection point, a seasonal spike in occupancy, or a scheduled event. Triggers are more useful than opinions because they can be checked by whoever walks the building next.

Treat the tiers above as an author's heuristic for structuring a conversation, not as an industry standard. Your building's footfall, layout and floor finishes should decide the final numbers, and the provider's written quotation should confirm them.

The scope sheet: columns that make sign-off possible

A scope sheet only earns its place if it can be signed. Build it with these columns, one row per zone:

Zone reference and description. A short code plus plain words, for example 'C3 — corridor floor, level three'.

Surface and finish. Note material where known, because it affects method and drying time.

Cleaning action agreed. Describe the outcome expected, not a chemical procedure.

Frequency option. Daily, weekly or periodic, with the trigger noted.

Access notes. Keys, fobs, occupied rooms, times the area is busy, or equipment storage.

Condition at start. A short note or photo reference taken during the walkthrough.

Sign-off initials and date. One line for the provider's supervisor and one for the building representative.

The condition-at-start column is the quiet hero of the sheet. A dated note or photo of an existing scuff, a worn nosing or a stained landing gives both sides a shared reference point before work begins. It also keeps later conversations about condition factual rather than remembered.

Keep the sheet to one or two pages. If it grows beyond that, split it by floor or by building rather than compressing the rows.

Access, timing and the handover conversation

Scope is only half the agreement; access is the other half. For each zone, record when it can realistically be worked. A stairwell used as the only route between floors cannot be closed without notice, and a corridor outside occupied rooms has quieter windows.

Agree in advance how the provider will enter, where equipment will be staged, and who confirms that an area is ready to be worked and ready to be reopened. Name a single contact on each side so messages do not scatter across several people.

Where commercial cleaning fits alongside other services

Stairwells and corridors are one part of a wider cleaning picture, and it helps to know which parts belong together. A short guide to splitting the work:

Circulation areas. Stairwells, corridors, lobbies and lift interiors, scoped zone by zone as above.

Soft floor coverings. Corridor runners and carpeted landings need their own access and drying plan, so list them as distinct rows.

Textiles and soft furnishings. Curtains in shared areas and any fabric items that leave the building are handled under their own arrangements.

Personal and specialist items. Shoes and soft toys are examples of items with dedicated cleaning support rather than general area cleaning, and they should be logged separately from building zones.

A worked example and a review routine

Consider a fictional three-storey guesthouse in Sarawak with an open stairwell, one corridor per floor and a small lift lobby. The manager builds a sheet with eleven zones. Entry floor and handrails are set to daily; stair treads, landings and corridor walls to weekly; windows, ledges and the utility alcove to a periodic cycle. Each row carries an access note and a condition note with a photo reference.

At the first review, two rows are adjusted: the lift lobby moves up a tier because it is the first thing guests see, and the utility alcove gains a trigger based on visible build-up rather than a fixed date. Nothing else changes. The sheet is signed by both sides and filed with the quotation.

A review routine keeps the sheet alive:

Walk the route with the sheet in hand at an agreed interval.

Mark each row as met, partly met or not met, with a one-line note.

Record any zone that should be added, removed or re-tiered.

Re-sign and date the revised sheet, and attach it to the current quotation.

Treat the interval and the scoring words as examples you can adapt, not as a certified method. The value comes from doing the walk consistently and writing down what you see, so that the next conversation about commercial cleaning starts from evidence rather than memory.
