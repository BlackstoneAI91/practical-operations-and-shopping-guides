Hotel Linen Management Par-Level Worksheet: Calculating Minimum Stock per Room Type

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical worksheet for Sarawak accommodation operators to calculate minimum hotel linen stock per room type against pickup frequency, with worked examples, buffer rules and a handover log.

What a Par Level Actually Answers

A par level is the minimum quantity of each linen item you keep on hand so that rooms can be made up between laundry cycles without running short. It is not a wish list and it is not a purchasing target. It answers one question: if a batch leaves today and comes back on a known day, how many pieces must stay on the shelf to cover every bed make-up in between?

The worksheet below is built around that question. It separates three pools of linen: stock in use in rooms, stock in the linen room ready to use, and stock in transit or being processed. A par level only works when all three are counted honestly, because a towel that is folded on a shelf and a towel that is in a bag on a van are both unavailable to a housekeeper.

This guide is written for small hotels, guesthouses and venue managers in Sarawak who handle their own linen room. It is a planning tool, not a promise about turnaround times. Your actual cycle depends on your provider, your route and your volume, so treat every number here as an example to replace with your own.

One useful starting point is to write down what you send and how often. Step 1: Count Rooms by Type, Not by Total

Par levels calculated from a single room count hide the differences that cause shortages. A suite with two beds and four towels per guest behaves differently from a single room with one bed and two towels. Split the property into room types and count each type separately.

Build a simple table with one row per room type and these columns: number of rooms, beds per room, sheets per bed, pillowcases per bed, bath towels per room, hand towels per room, bath mats per room, and any item specific to that type such as a duvet cover or a pool towel.

Multiply across to get the pieces in use at full occupancy. For example, a fictional 18-room guesthouse might have 12 standard rooms with one queen bed and 6 family rooms with one queen and two singles. If each bed takes one sheet set and two pillowcases, and each room takes two bath towels, two hand towels and one bath mat, the full-occupancy count is straightforward arithmetic. Write the total at the bottom of the table. That figure is your baseline, not your par level.

Keep the table on one page. If it spills over, you will stop updating it, and an out-of-date count is worse than a rough one you actually maintain.

Step 2: Add the Changeover Multiplier

Linen is consumed by changeovers, not by rooms. A room that turns over twice in a week uses two sets even though it is one room. Estimate your changeover rate per room type per week, then multiply the full-occupancy count by that rate to see weekly demand.

Use a range rather than a single figure. A fictional 18-room guesthouse might run 1.5 to 2.5 changeovers per room per week in a quiet month and 3 to 4 in a peak month. Record both. The peak figure is what your par level must survive, because that is when a shortage actually hurts.

This is also the point to note items that are replaced mid-stay rather than at checkout, such as towels in a long-stay room. Add those to the weekly demand separately so they are not lost inside the changeover average.

If you are unsure of your changeover rate, pull two weeks of housekeeping sheets and count. Two weeks of real records beats a guess, and it takes less time than reconciling a shortage later.

Step 3: Divide by Pickup Frequency

This is the step most worksheets skip. Your par level is driven by how many days of demand sit between one clean batch arriving and the next leaving.

Write down your pickup frequency in days. If linen leaves on Monday and returns on Wednesday, you need enough stock to cover Monday, Tuesday and the gap until the clean batch is back on the shelf. If your pickup is twice weekly, the gap is shorter and the par level can be lower. If it is weekly, the par level rises sharply.

Now divide weekly demand by the number of pickups per week to get demand per cycle, then multiply by the number of cycles you must cover at once. A property with three pickups a week and a two-cycle cover needs roughly two-thirds of a week's peak demand on the shelf at any time, plus what is in rooms.

This is where a laundry service with a predictable schedule earns its place. Step 4: Set the Buffer and the Spare Pool

A par level with no buffer is a schedule that must never slip. Add a buffer expressed as a percentage of cycle demand, and write down why you chose it. A fictional property might hold 15 percent buffer for standard rooms and 25 percent for family rooms because family linen is harder to substitute.

Keep a separate spare pool for damage and staining. This is not buffer; it is replacement stock held aside so that a torn sheet does not eat into the working par level. Record the spare pool as a fixed count per item and review it monthly.

Finally, set a reorder trigger. If the working stock plus spare pool falls below the par level for any item, that item goes on a restock list the same week. A trigger written on the sheet is more reliable than a memory held by one supervisor.

Label suggested percentages as your own working assumptions. They are starting points to adjust against your own loss records, not industry standards.

Step 5: A Worked Example You Can Copy

Here is a fictional worked example for a 20-room property with two room types, three pickups a week and a two-cycle cover.

Standard rooms: 14 rooms, one queen bed each. Full occupancy needs 14 sheet sets, 28 pillowcases, 28 bath towels, 28 hand towels and 14 bath mats. At a peak of three changeovers per week, weekly demand is 42 sheet sets, 84 pillowcases, 84 bath towels, 84 hand towels and 42 bath mats.

Family rooms: 6 rooms, one queen and two singles. Full occupancy needs 18 sheet sets, 36 pillowcases, 36 bath towels, 36 hand towels and 18 bath mats. At a peak of four changeovers per week, weekly demand is 72 sheet sets, 144 pillowcases, 144 bath towels, 144 hand towels and 72 bath mats.

Combined weekly peak demand: 114 sheet sets, 228 pillowcases, 228 bath towels, 228 hand towels and 114 bath mats. With three pickups a week, one cycle is roughly a third of that. A two-cycle cover is roughly two-thirds, before buffer. Add a 15 percent buffer and a fixed spare pool, and you have a par level per item.

The point of the example is the shape of the calculation, not the numbers. Replace every figure with your own counts and your own confirmed pickup schedule.

Step 6: Handover Log and Weekly Review

A par level only holds if the count is checked. Keep a one-page handover log with columns for date, item, count out, count in, shortfall and initials. The person receiving the clean batch signs it, and the person who sent the dirty batch signs it too. Two signatures close the loop.

Review the log weekly against the worksheet. If shortfalls cluster on one item, the par level for that item is probably too low or the buffer is being consumed by damage. If shortfalls are spread evenly, the cycle itself may be too long.

Keep the worksheet and the log together in the linen room. When you discuss your schedule with a provider, bring both.
