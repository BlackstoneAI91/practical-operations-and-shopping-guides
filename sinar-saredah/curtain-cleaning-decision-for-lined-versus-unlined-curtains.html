Curtain Cleaning Decision for Lined Versus Unlined Curtains: A Comparison Sheet

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical comparison sheet for Sarawak households, accommodation operators and venue managers deciding how to clean lined and unlined curtains, with a simple condition rating and pre-booking checks.

Why Lined and Unlined Curtains Need Different Decisions

Curtains are made to fit a specific opening, so a panel that is damaged or shrunk rarely has a second home. That makes the decision before booking more important than the speed of booking. Lined and unlined curtains behave differently in three practical ways: weight, layers and what the lining hides.

An unlined panel is usually a single visible face. You can see the whole surface, which makes condition checks straightforward, but it also means every mark is on show and there is no second layer to absorb dust or light exposure.

A lined panel has at least two layers joined at the edges. The face fabric carries the look; the lining carries much of the weight and the structural work. The two layers can move, shrink or age at different rates, and the join between them is where problems tend to appear first. A lining can also conceal the back of the face fabric, so a mark you cannot see may still be present.

This guide is a comparison sheet, not a set of cleaning instructions. It helps you record what you have, rate its condition, and ask better questions before handing panels over. The Pre-Booking Comparison Sheet

Use one row per curtain set. A set means the panels that hang together on one window or opening, because they usually need to be handled as a group.

Fields to record for every set:

Set name or room reference. Use the name your staff already use, such as a room number or a hall side, so the record matches your housekeeping or venue list.

Lined or unlined. If you are unsure, check the reverse face and the bottom hem. A separate backing layer joined at the edges indicates a lining.

Panel count and approximate drop. Count panels and note the drop in your own units. This helps you describe the set consistently when you request a quotation.

Fixing type. Note the track, rail or rod and how the panels attach. This matters for takedown and rehang planning, not for the cleaning decision itself.

Visible condition. Record marks, fading, fraying, loose hooks, split seams and any odour. For lined sets, note whether the lining is attached, loose or torn.

Last known cleaning. If nobody remembers, write unknown rather than guessing.

Access notes. Note anything that affects takedown, such as height, furniture in the way or a shared space that cannot be closed during the day.

Keep the sheet with your other soft furnishing records. When you contact a provider, the sheet becomes the basis of the conversation rather than a description from memory.

A Simple Condition Rating You Can Apply Yourself

A rating only helps if two people would give the same set the same score. Use these four bands and write the reason next to the score.

A: Sound. No visible marks, no fraying, lining attached and intact, fixings complete. The set looks and hangs as expected.

B: Minor wear. Light marks, slight fading on the sun-facing side, a few loose hooks or a small area of lining separation. Nothing that changes how the set hangs.

C: Needs attention. Clear marks or odour, visible fading across a panel, frayed hems, a lining that has come away along a seam, or fixings that no longer hold reliably.

D: Uncertain or fragile. You cannot inspect the full panel, the fabric feels brittle, the lining is torn in several places, or the set is old enough that you are unsure it will survive handling.

Two rules keep the rating honest. First, rate the worst panel in the set, not the average, because the set will be judged together. Second, write the reason. A score without a reason cannot be reviewed later.

This is an author heuristic for organising a conversation, not a tested industry scale. Its value is consistency across rooms and buildings, not precision.

What to Check on Unlined Curtains

Unlined panels are easier to inspect, so the checks focus on the visible face and the structure that holds it.

Check the full face in daylight, not under a single lamp. Move along the panel from top to bottom. Marks near the leading edge and the bottom hem are common because those areas are handled and brushed most.

Check the heading. Look at the tape, rings, hooks or pockets. A heading that is already failing may not survive takedown and rehang, so note it before booking.

Check the hems. A dropped hem or loose stitching is a repair question, not only a cleaning question. Record it so the provider can tell you what they will and will not handle.

Check for fading by comparing a sun-exposed panel with one that hangs in shade, if your set has both. Fading is not removed by cleaning, and knowing about it prevents a disappointing conversation afterwards.

Check odour at the fabric, not the room. Cooking, smoke and damp smells can sit in the weave. Note where the smell is strongest.

Because there is no lining, the back of the face fabric is also visible. Inspect it too, especially if the panels are reversible or unlined on both sides.

What to Check on Lined Curtains

Lined sets need the same checks plus a few that only apply to multi-layer panels.

Check the lining attachment. Run your hand along the edges and the bottom. A lining that has separated in one corner is a small repair note; a lining that has separated along a full seam changes how the panel will behave when handled.

Check for colour transfer between layers. Where the lining meets the face fabric, look for a shadow or tint that does not match either layer. Record it rather than trying to interpret it.

Check the interlining, if present. Some lined sets have a middle layer. Note whether it feels even or has shifted into a lump near the bottom.

Check the weight. A lined panel is heavier, so the heading and fixings carry more load. Look at the track or rod fixings and note anything loose before takedown.

Check the drop on both layers. If the lining hangs lower or shorter than the face, note it. This is a construction observation, not a cleaning instruction.

Check what the lining hides. You cannot see the back of the face fabric, so record that the reverse face was not inspected. That single note prevents overconfident assumptions later.

Turning the Sheet Into a Booking Conversation

Once the sheet is filled in, the booking conversation becomes specific. Bring the set list, the condition ratings and the access notes.

Ask what the provider needs from you before the panels are collected or dropped off. Ask how the panels should be prepared, whether fixings stay on or come off, and how the return will be labelled so each set goes back to the right opening. Ask what happens if a set is rated D on arrival, and how that will be communicated.

For accommodation and venue work, ask how the timing fits your room availability. A set that is out of the building cannot be hung, so the cleaning window and the rehang window need to be planned together.

A Worked Example: Two Sets in One Small Guesthouse

This example is fictional and is included to show how the sheet is used, not to describe a real property.

Set one, a front room, is unlined. The panels are a single layer, the heading is sound, and there is light fading on the sun-facing panel. The rating is B, with the reason recorded as light fading, no marks. The access note says the window faces the road and the room is booked for most of the month.

Set two, a shared lounge, is lined. The lining has separated along one bottom corner, the heading fixings are loose, and the reverse face of the fabric could not be inspected. The rating is C, with the reason recorded as lining separation and loose fixings. The access note says the lounge is used every evening, so takedown has to fit around that.

With both rows filled in, the guesthouse manager can ask focused questions: what preparation the lined set needs, how the loose fixings should be handled, and which window in the calendar can take a set out of use. The unlined set, rated B, may need a different conversation from the lined set, rated C, because the issues are different.

The sheet does not decide the answer. It makes the question clear enough to ask.

Keeping the Record Useful After the Curtains Return

The sheet has a second life after the panels come back. Add a return line to each row: the date returned, the set reference, and whether the condition rating changed.

Check the rehang against the original notes. If a fixing was loose before, confirm what was done about it. If a lining separation was recorded, confirm how it now looks. If the reverse face was not inspected before, note whether it was inspected on return.

Keep the completed sheet with your soft furnishing records so the next decision starts from evidence rather than memory. Over time, the pattern of ratings across rooms tells you which sets are ageing fastest and which need attention first.

For households, the same discipline works at a smaller scale: one sheet per window, kept with the household paperwork. For venue managers, the sheet becomes part of the handover record when staff change.
