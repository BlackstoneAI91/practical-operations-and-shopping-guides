A Joint Walkthrough Agenda for a New Cleaning Contractor

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A room-by-room walkthrough agenda with sign-off lines for Sarawak households, accommodation operators and venue managers agreeing commercial cleaning scope before the first shift.

Why the walkthrough matters more than the quotation

A quotation tells you what a contractor intends to do. A walkthrough tells both sides what the building actually contains. The gap between the two is where most early disagreements start: a corridor counted as one zone but treated as three, a store room nobody mentioned, a set of soft furnishings that was never in scope.

The practical fix is to walk the site together before the first shift and write down what you both see, room by room. This guide gives you an agenda for that walk, a sign-off line for each area, and a short worksheet you can fill in as you go. It is written for households with a large property, accommodation operators and venue managers in Sarawak who are about to hand over commercial cleaning work for the first time.

Treat the walkthrough as a scope conversation, not a negotiation about price. If you settle the areas, frequencies and access notes first, the commercial discussion afterwards is grounded in something concrete. Before the walk: assemble the paper you already have

Arrive with documents, not impressions. The walkthrough is faster and more honest when both sides are looking at the same starting material.

Bring a simple floor plan or a hand-drawn sketch with room numbers. Bring any previous cleaning schedule, even an informal one, so you can point to what changed. Bring a list of access constraints: which keys exist, who holds them, which doors lock behind you, where a vehicle can stop, and what hours the building is quiet. Bring a note of any surfaces you already know are sensitive, and any items you would rather not have touched at all.

Ask the contractor to bring their own scope template and their standard terms. Reading the terms before the walk, rather than after, means you can ask about anything unclear while you are still standing in the room it affects. If a contractor publishes customer terms and conditions that apply alongside quotations, invoices and written service confirmations, those documents are part of the handover, not paperwork to file later.

One small habit pays off: number every room on your sketch and use those numbers for the rest of the walkthrough. Verbal descriptions like "the back corridor" mean different things to different people.

The room-by-room agenda

Walk in a fixed order and do not skip ahead. A workable sequence for most buildings is: entrance and reception, shared washrooms, corridors and stairwells, guest or living rooms, kitchen or pantry, store rooms, and finally external edges such as walkways and bin areas.

At each stop, cover the same five points and write the answers down:

1. Surfaces. What is being cleaned here, and what is being left alone? Name the floor, the fixtures and any fabric items. 2. Frequency. How often does this area need attention, and does that change by season or by occupancy? 3. Access. Who opens the room, when, and where does equipment live between visits? 4. Consumables and linens. Does anything leave the building, such as linen, curtains or soft furnishings, and who counts it out and back? 5. Sign-off. Who confirms the room is acceptable, and against what standard?

Keep each stop short. If a discussion about one room runs long, note it and move on; you can return at the end. The goal of the first pass is coverage, not resolution.

For accommodation operators, the linen and soft furnishing questions matter as much as the surfaces. Items that leave the property need their own record, because a count that is not written down at handover is hard to reconcile later.

Where specialist items fit into the scope

A general cleaning scope often stops at surfaces and floors, while fabric and specialist items sit in a separate conversation. Decide during the walkthrough which side of the line each item falls on, and write it down.

Carpets, curtains and upholstery are the usual grey area. Ask directly whether they are in the routine scope, handled as a separate scheduled job, or excluded. If they are separate, note who decides when they are due and what triggers that decision.

Sign-off lines: what each one should contain

A sign-off line is not a signature on a blank page. It is a short record that both sides agree on what was seen. Each line should carry five fields: area reference, surfaces in scope, frequency, access note, and the initials of both people with the date.

Here is a fictional example of one completed line for a small guesthouse in Sarawak:

Area: Room 04, first floor. Surfaces: floor, bathroom fixtures, window ledge; curtains excluded. Frequency: after each checkout. Access: housekeeping key, equipment stored in ground-floor cupboard. Agreed by: operator and contractor, dated.

And a second, for a shared corridor:

Area: Stairwell B and first-floor corridor. Surfaces: floor, handrail, light switches. Frequency: twice weekly, plus spot attention after events. Access: open during office hours. Agreed by: operator and contractor, dated.

Notice what the lines do not contain: prices, promises about outcomes, or vague words like "regularly". Keep those out. A sign-off line that could be read two ways is worse than no line at all, because it creates false confidence.

If a room is genuinely out of scope, write a line saying so. An explicit exclusion is easier to defend than a silence.

A short worksheet to complete during the walk

Use this as a working sheet. It is an author's checklist, not a standard, and you should adapt the columns to your building.

Column one: area reference. Use the numbers from your sketch. Column two: surfaces in scope. Be specific enough that a new staff member could follow it. Column three: frequency. Write the interval and any trigger, such as after checkout or after an event. Column four: access and keys. Note who holds what and where equipment is kept. Column five: items leaving the building. List linen, curtains, soft furnishings or anything else that travels, with a count. Column six: exclusions. Write down what is deliberately not covered. Column seven: sign-off. Initials and date for both sides.

A simple scoring habit helps you spot gaps before the first shift. Give each area one point if surfaces are named, one if frequency is written, one if access is recorded, and one if sign-off is initialled. An area scoring three or four is ready. An area scoring one or two needs a follow-up conversation. This scoring is a heuristic for your own use, not a measure of any contractor's performance.

Finish the sheet the same day, while the walk is fresh. A worksheet completed a week later tends to drift towards what people remember rather than what they saw.

After the walk: turning notes into a first-shift brief

The walkthrough produces notes. The first shift needs a brief. Convert your completed worksheet into a one-page summary that a supervisor can carry: the area list in walking order, the frequency for each, the access notes, and the exclusions.

Agree two practical points before the first shift. First, how a completed area is confirmed, whether by a walk at the end of the shift or a written note. Second, who to contact when something is unclear, and how quickly a reply is expected. Put both in the brief.

Keep the signed worksheet with your other handover documents. If the contractor's terms apply to the work, they sit alongside quotations, invoices and written service confirmations, so store them together rather than in separate folders. That way, when a question arises about a specific room, you can find the scope note and the terms in one place.

Finally, schedule a short review after the first few shifts. The purpose is not to grade the contractor but to check the worksheet against reality: which areas took longer than expected, which exclusions turned out to be wrong, and which access notes need correcting. Update the sheet, re-initial it, and the next shift starts from a better document than the first one did.
