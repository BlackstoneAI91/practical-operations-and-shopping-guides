Checking a Contractor's Proposed Proof of Nightly Cleaning Completion

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical template for Sarawak households, accommodation operators and venue managers to review how a cleaning contractor records nightly completion, names exceptions, handles non-sensitive photos and escalates recurring issues.

Start With the Record, Not the Promise

When a cleaning contractor proposes how they will prove that nightly work is finished, the useful question is not whether they promise to do a good job. It is where the completion record lives, who writes it, and what a reader can see the next morning. A verbal assurance at handover tells you nothing about the shift that ran at 2am while your front desk was closed.

Ask for the proposed record format before the first shift, not after the first complaint. A workable proposal usually names four things: the place where completion is recorded, the person who signs it off, the way exceptions are written down, and the route for escalating a problem that repeats. If any of those four is missing, the proof method is incomplete regardless of how polished the sample looks.

This guide is written for households, accommodation operators and venue managers in Sarawak who are comparing proposals. It offers a review template and example wording. It does not claim that any particular proof method is required, standard or superior, and it does not describe what any named provider must do. Treat the template as a set of questions to put to a contractor, then judge the answers against your own building and your own risk.

One useful framing: proof of completion is a handover document, not a marketing document. It should be boring, repeatable and readable by someone who was not on shift.

Where Completion Is Recorded

The first decision is location. Common proposals include a paper log kept at the site, a shared spreadsheet, a messaging group thread, or a simple form on a phone. Each has trade-offs worth naming in the review.

A paper log at the site is easy to sign and hard to search later. A shared spreadsheet is searchable but depends on someone updating it after a long shift. A messaging thread is immediate but scatters the record across dates and participants. A phone form can capture a timestamp and a location field, but only if the contractor has agreed what those fields mean.

Ask the contractor to show you a filled-in example, not a blank template. Then ask three follow-up questions. Who can edit an entry after it is submitted? How long is the record kept? Who can retrieve it if you ask for last Tuesday's entry in three months' time?

For operators with more than one building, add a location field to the record and agree the naming convention in advance. A record that says "block A done" is less useful than one that names the floor, the zone and the shift. If your site has shared areas, agree whether those are recorded separately from rooms.

Where the contractor also handles laundry, dry cleaning or specialist work, keep the nightly completion record separate from the item-level paperwork. Mixing the two makes both harder to audit. The brand's published terms note that customer terms apply when items, linen, garments, curtains, carpets and similar requests are accepted, and that they sit alongside quotations, invoices and written service confirmations. That is a reminder to keep each record type distinct rather than folding everything into one sheet.

How Exceptions Are Named

A completion record that only has a tick box will hide the interesting information. The exceptions are what you actually need to read. Agree a short, fixed vocabulary for exceptions so that entries are comparable week to week.

A practical set of exception labels might include: area not accessed, key not available, item left in place, consumable not restocked, damage observed, and work deferred pending instruction. Six labels are usually enough for a small site. The point is that the person writing the entry picks from a list rather than composing a free-text sentence at the end of a long shift.

Each exception entry should answer four questions in a line or two: what was not completed, why, what was done instead, and who needs to decide next. For example: "Room 12 not accessed, guest declined entry at 22:40, corridor and bathroom left for morning shift, duty manager to confirm re-entry time." That is a fictional example, but it shows the shape.

Agree in advance which exceptions must be reported immediately rather than logged overnight. A missed area in a back corridor can wait until morning. A spill in a guest-facing lobby probably cannot. Write that threshold down so the night team is not guessing.

Finally, agree how an exception is closed. A log full of open exceptions is a sign that nobody owns the follow-up. Each entry should have a named person responsible for closing it and a date by which it should be reviewed.

Non-Sensitive Photo Options

Photos are often proposed as proof, and they can be useful, but they need rules. The aim is a neutral record of condition or completion, not a gallery of guests, documents or personal belongings.

Agree what may be photographed: empty rooms after cleaning, made beds, restocked shelves, a completed corridor, a signed log sheet. Agree what may not be photographed: people, identity documents, screens, luggage contents, personal items, and anything that would identify a guest. If a photo would need blurring to be acceptable, it is probably the wrong photo.

Agree where photos are stored and who can see them. A shared album with a link that anyone can forward is different from a folder restricted to the site manager and the contractor's supervisor. Ask how long photos are kept and how they are deleted.

Agree the minimum useful set. One wide shot of a finished area plus one close shot of any exception is usually more informative than a dozen near-identical images. Ask the contractor to caption each photo with the date, the zone and the shift.

If the contractor also handles items such as shoes or soft toys, keep those photos in the item record rather than the nightly completion log. The brand's published service notes describe cleaning support for sneakers, sports shoes, school shoes and everyday pairs, and separately for soft toys, plushies and similar comfort items. Those are item-level services, and their records belong with the item, not with the building's nightly sign-off.

Who Escalates a Recurring Issue

A single missed area is an incident. The same missed area three weeks running is a process problem, and it needs a different owner. Agree the escalation path in writing before the first shift.

A simple three-step path works for many small sites. Step one: the night supervisor logs the exception and informs the site contact at the next handover. Step two: if the same exception appears twice in a rolling period you define, the contractor's account manager and your site manager review it together. Step three: if it appears again after that review, it goes to a named senior contact on both sides with a written action and a review date.

Name the roles, not just the companies. "The contractor's supervisor" is clearer than "the contractor". "The duty manager on shift" is clearer than "the venue". Where a role is vacant, name the person covering it.

Decide how a recurring issue is closed. A review meeting that ends without a written change to the schedule, the checklist or the access arrangement is likely to produce the same exception again. Record the change and the date it takes effect.

Keep the escalation log short. A one-page monthly summary of exceptions, closures and open items is easier to act on than a long narrative. If you want a broader starting point for scope conversations, a joint walkthrough before the first shift pairs well with this template.

A Review Worksheet You Can Use

The following worksheet is an author heuristic, not a standard or a certification. Score each line from zero to two: zero means the proposal does not address it, one means it is mentioned but vague, two means there is a worked example you can read. A total of twelve or more across the eight lines suggests the proposal is specific enough to discuss further. A lower score is not a rejection; it is a list of questions to send back.

Line one: the record location is named and a filled example is shown. Line two: the person who signs off each shift is named by role. Line three: the exception vocabulary is fixed and short. Line four: each exception entry names an owner and a review date. Line five: the immediate-report threshold is written down. Line six: photo rules cover what may and may not be captured, plus storage and retention. Line seven: the escalation path names roles on both sides and a review cadence. Line eight: a monthly summary format is agreed.

Run the worksheet against two or three proposals side by side. The comparison usually surfaces the differences faster than reading each proposal in full. Where a contractor offers commercial cleaning alongside laundry and dry cleaning, ask whether the nightly record is the same document for both, or whether they are kept separate. The brand's published service list covers laundry, dry cleaning, commercial cleaning, specialist cleaning, pickup and delivery, which is a reminder that a single contractor may be running several record types at once.

Putting the Template to Work

Before the first shift, hold a short session with the contractor and your own site contact. Walk through the worksheet, agree the record location, and fill in one example entry together. That single exercise tends to expose ambiguities that a written proposal leaves out.

After the first week, read the record as a reviewer rather than as a participant. Are the entries specific enough that you could act on them without calling anyone? Are exceptions being closed, or only logged? Is the escalation path being used, or is everything sitting at step one?

Adjust the template rather than abandoning it. A record that is too detailed will not be maintained on a busy night; a record that is too thin will not answer the question you actually have. The right level is usually the smallest set of fields that lets a morning reader understand what happened and who needs to act.
