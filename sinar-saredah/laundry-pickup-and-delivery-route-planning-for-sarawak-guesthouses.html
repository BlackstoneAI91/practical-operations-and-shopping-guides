Laundry Pickup and Delivery Route Planning for Sarawak Guesthouses

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical route-planning worksheet for Sarawak guesthouse operators who need pickup windows, bag counts and access points to line up across several small properties without collections colliding.

Why route planning starts with the handover, not the map

A guesthouse route is not really a driving problem first. It is a handover problem. Before anyone draws a line between properties, each stop needs a clear answer to four questions: who hands the bag over, where it waits, how it is counted, and who signs for the return. When those answers are vague, two collections can arrive in the same narrow lane at the same time, or a bag can sit in a lobby that a different shift has already cleared.

This guide is written for operators running several small properties rather than one large hotel: a shophouse guesthouse, a converted terrace house, a pair of short-stay flats and perhaps a venue room used for events. The aim is a worksheet you can fill in once, review weekly, and hand to whoever is on duty. It is a planning aid, not a promise about any provider's schedule. Timings below are author heuristics and examples, not researched benchmarks.

Build the property register before the timetable

Start with a register, one row per property. Columns that earn their place: property name, street or area, type (guesthouse, short-stay flat, venue), number of beds or rooms, typical bag count per collection, access notes, and the person who signs.

Bag count is the column people skip and later regret. Count bags by type rather than by weight: linen bags, towel bags, garment bags for dry cleaning, and a separate line for odd items such as curtains or soft furnishings. A guesthouse with six rooms and a guesthouse with six rooms plus a laundry room can produce very different volumes, so record what actually left last time rather than what the room count suggests.

Access notes should be concrete. Which entrance is used before the shophouse awning goes up? Is there a lift, a stairwell, or a back lane? Where can a vehicle stop without blocking a neighbour? If a property has no safe waiting point, that is a planning fact, not a detail to resolve on the day.

Keep the register short enough to read on a phone. A register nobody opens is worse than a single index card taped inside the store cupboard.

Map pickup windows against your own turnover rhythm

A pickup window only works if it sits after stripping and before the next arrival. For each property, note the checkout time, the realistic time by which rooms are stripped, and the earliest next check-in. The gap between stripping and check-in is your usable window.

A worked fictional example: a three-room guesthouse strips linen between 11am and 12.30pm, and the next arrival is at 3pm. A second property two streets away strips between 10am and 11am but has no arrival that day. If both are offered the same midday window, the first property is rushed and the second is idle. Staggering them, with the earlier-stripping property first, gives each stop room to breathe.

Treat any suggested window as an example to test, not a rule. Run the proposed timetable for a week, note where it slipped, and adjust. The point of the worksheet is to make slippage visible early rather than to design a perfect first draft.

If your properties also handle short-stay turns, the same logic applies with tighter margins; a dedicated short-stay turnaround timeline is worth building alongside this route sheet rather than folding both into one document.

Group stops by reader intent, not by postcode alone

It is tempting to sort stops purely by distance. Distance matters, but grouping by the kind of handover often saves more time than shaving a few minutes off a drive. Three useful groups:

Group one, routine linen: properties with a regular bag count, a fixed storage spot and a staffed handover. These are the predictable stops.

Group two, mixed loads: properties that occasionally add dry cleaning, curtains or soft furnishings to the same collection. These need a second count and a note on the bag, because a garment bag and a linen bag do not belong in the same tally.

Group three, access-sensitive stops: properties where the vehicle cannot wait, where the entrance changes by time of day, or where the handover depends on one named person.

Ordering the route so that group one stops cluster together, group two stops are flagged for extra counting time, and group three stops sit where a delay does least harm is a practical starting pattern. It is a heuristic, not a universal rule; a small route may only need two groups.

The worksheet: columns, counts and a signed handover line

Use one sheet per week, with a row per collection. Suggested columns:

Date and day; property; proposed window; actual arrival; bags out (by type); bags in (by type); variance; access note used; handover name; signature or initials; follow-up needed.

The variance column is the quiet hero. If three bags go out and two come back, the variance is minus one, and the follow-up line records what happened next. Do not use the sheet to assign blame; use it to find where counting breaks down. A missing towel is usually a counting gap, not a person.

A signed handover line does two jobs. It confirms the exchange happened, and it gives the next shift a name to ask. Keep signatures simple: initials and time are enough for an internal sheet.

For a fuller treatment of access points, shelter and where clean items wait, a separate collection-point checklist pairs well with this route sheet. Keep the two documents linked by property name so a reader can move between them without re-learning the layout.

Review cadence and what to change first

Review the sheet weekly, not daily. Daily review turns into firefighting; weekly review shows patterns. Three questions are enough for a first pass: which window slipped most often, which property had the most variance, and which access note was wrong.

Change one thing at a time. If you move two windows and rewrite an access note in the same week, you will not know which change helped. A reasonable cadence, offered as an example rather than a standard, is one adjustment per fortnight and a fuller review each quarter or before a seasonal peak.

Keep old sheets for a few months. They are the only record of what actually happened, and they are more useful than memory when a new staff member asks why a window is set where it is.

Where a property also books specialist work, such as dry cleaning for venue soft furnishings or curtain cleaning between events, note it on the same sheet under a separate line so the route plan does not silently absorb a job that needs its own handover.
