Comparing a Hotel Laundry Quote: Weight Versus Piece Pricing

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical guide for Sarawak accommodation operators and venue managers on comparing hotel laundry quotes priced by weight against quotes priced by piece, using a fictional linen mix and tracking handling separately.

Why the Two Quote Units Cannot Be Compared at Face Value

A hotel laundry quote usually arrives in one of two shapes: a rate per kilogram or a rate per piece. The instinct is to put the two numbers side by side and pick the smaller one. That comparison is misleading because the two units measure different things. A weight rate charges for mass, so a heavy bath mat and a light pillowcase are priced by what they weigh rather than by the effort of handling them. A piece rate charges per item, so a large heavy item and a small light one may cost the same even though their bulk differs.

Neither unit is inherently better. The useful question is which unit matches the way your property actually generates laundry, and what each quote leaves out. Handling, sorting, folding, packaging, transport and minimum charges often sit outside the headline rate, and those are the lines that decide the real comparison. Before you compare anything, ask each provider to state the unit, the inclusions and the exclusions in writing. Build a Fictional Linen Mix to Test Both Units

To compare units fairly, you need a fixed basket of items that represents your property. Use a fictional example so the arithmetic stays transparent. Suppose a small guesthouse sends a weekly bundle of 120 pillowcases, 60 sheets and 30 bath mats. Assign each item a plausible dry weight for the exercise only, and keep the weights clearly labelled as assumptions rather than measured facts.

With that basket you can do two things. First, convert the weight quote into a cost for the whole basket by multiplying total assumed weight by the rate per kilogram. Second, convert the piece quote into a cost for the same basket by multiplying each item count by its per-piece rate. Now both quotes describe the same bundle, and the numbers can be compared honestly. The exercise also exposes where the units diverge: if bath mats dominate the weight, a weight quote will be sensitive to how many you send, while a piece quote will be sensitive to item count regardless of size.

Run the basket at three volumes, such as a quiet week, a normal week and a peak week. A quote that looks competitive at low volume may behave differently when the mix shifts toward heavy items. Record your assumptions in a column beside each figure so a colleague can check the logic later.

Track Handling and Inclusions Separately From the Rate

The headline rate is only part of the quote. Handling is where weight and piece quotes often differ most, and it deserves its own column in your comparison sheet. List the tasks that surround the cleaning itself: collection from your premises, sorting by item type, folding or pressing, bagging, labelling, and return delivery. For each quote, mark whether the task is included, charged separately, or not offered.

This is also where a laundry service with pickup and delivery changes the shape of the comparison. If one quote assumes you deliver and collect the linen yourself, the labour and vehicle time sit with your staff rather than on the invoice. If another quote includes collection and return, that convenience is part of what you are paying for. Neither is automatically cheaper; the point is to put the same tasks on both sides of the sheet so the totals describe equivalent work.

A Comparison Worksheet You Can Reuse

A simple worksheet keeps the comparison disciplined. Use these columns, one row per provider:

Quote reference and date. Unit offered (per kilogram, per piece, or a mix). Rate or rates as written. Assumed basket cost at quiet, normal and peak volume. Handling inclusions (collection, sorting, folding, bagging, labelling, return). Items excluded from the rate. Minimum charge or minimum volume, if stated. Turnaround stated in the quote. Who confirms a change to the order, and how. Notes on anything the provider could not confirm.

The last two rows matter more than they look. A quote is a snapshot, but a hotel's linen flow changes with occupancy. Knowing in advance who authorises an extra bundle or a changed item count prevents disputes later. Keep the worksheet as a living document and date each version, because a quote revised after a site visit should replace the earlier figure rather than sit beside it.

Treat any turnaround figures as the provider's stated terms, not as a promise you can rely on without checking. If a provider gives no turnaround, record that gap rather than filling it in yourself.

Where Specialist Items Break the Unit Comparison

A hotel's laundry is not only bed linen. Guest requests, staff uniforms and venue textiles can pull in items that do not fit neatly into a per-kilogram or per-piece linen rate. A formal gown, a pair of shoes or a soft toy left behind by a guest are handled differently from sheets, and a quote built around linen weight may not cover them at all.

Those are useful reference points when you are deciding which items belong in the linen contract and which should be quoted separately. The practical move is to keep a short list of non-linen items your property actually encounters and ask each provider whether those are covered, quoted per item, or declined. Do not assume a linen rate extends to them.

If your property also handles curtains, carpets or upholstery, those belong in a separate conversation about specialist cleaning rather than in the weight-versus-piece linen comparison. Mixing them into one quote makes the arithmetic harder to audit.

Questions to Put to Every Provider Before You Decide

Once the worksheet is filled, a short round of questions closes the remaining gaps. Ask each provider to confirm the unit in writing, to list what the rate includes and excludes, and to say how a change in volume or item mix is handled. Ask who inspects items on receipt and what happens when an item is outside the agreed scope. Ask how the provider records what was collected and what was returned, so your housekeeping team can reconcile counts.

Making the Decision and Keeping It Auditable

When the worksheet is complete, the decision usually becomes clearer than the headline rates suggested. A weight quote may suit a property whose linen mix is stable and dominated by similar items. A piece quote may suit a property that needs predictable per-item budgeting across a varied mix. Many operators end up with a blend: a linen rate for the bulk of the flow and separate arrangements for specialist items.

Whichever unit you choose, keep the comparison sheet, the written quotes and the confirmed terms together in one file. Date every version and note who approved the change. When occupancy shifts or the linen mix changes, rerun the fictional basket with updated volumes and compare again against the same columns. That habit turns a one-off quote comparison into a repeatable procurement process.
