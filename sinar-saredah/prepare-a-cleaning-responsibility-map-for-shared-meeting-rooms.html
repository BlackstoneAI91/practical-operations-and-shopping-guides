Preparing a Cleaning Responsibility Map for Shared Meeting Rooms

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical guide for venue managers and accommodation operators in Sarawak to separate tenant, building and contractor duties for shared meeting rooms, with a fictional diagram description and a sign-off worksheet.

Why a shared meeting room needs a written responsibility map

A shared meeting room is used by several parties but owned by none of them during the working day. The tenant books the room, the building manages the corridor outside it, and a contractor may be engaged for periodic work. When a mark appears on the carpet or a bin overflows, the first question is usually who should have dealt with it. A responsibility map answers that question before it is asked.

The map is not a cleaning schedule. It is a one-page agreement that names, for each zone and each task, who acts, who checks and who pays. Keep it short enough to read at a handover meeting and specific enough that two people reading it would reach the same conclusion.

This guide is written for venue managers, accommodation operators and tenants in Sarawak who share rooms rather than own a dedicated facility team. It focuses on the handoff between parties, not on cleaning methods. Where a specialist task falls outside the in-house routine, the map should say so and name the route to arrange it, rather than describing how the work is done.

Start with zones, not tasks

Most disputes about shared rooms come from overlapping zones. One party assumes the table surface is theirs, another assumes the whole room is. Draw the room as a set of zones before listing any task.

A workable zone set for a typical shared meeting room is: the room floor and carpet, the table and chairs, the presentation wall and screen, the storage cupboard, the doorway and threshold, and the corridor immediately outside. The threshold matters because it is where two parties meet. Decide explicitly whether the inside face of the door belongs to the room occupant or the building.

For each zone, record three things: who uses it, who is responsible for routine attention, and who is responsible for periodic or specialist work. A zone can have different answers for routine and periodic work, and often does. The room occupant may wipe the table after use while the building arranges periodic carpet attention.

Keep the zone list stable across rooms so that a tenant moving between rooms does not have to relearn the map. If rooms differ, note the difference rather than rewriting the whole document.

A fictional diagram description you can adapt

The following describes a fictional diagram for illustration only. It is not a real building or a real contract.

Picture a rectangle representing a meeting room for eight people. A dashed line runs around the inside of the walls, labelled room occupant. A second dashed line runs along the corridor outside the door, labelled building. The doorway itself is shaded grey and labelled shared threshold, with a note that the occupant clears the inside mat and the building clears the corridor side.

The table is drawn as a solid block labelled occupant, routine wipe after each booking. The carpet is drawn with a dotted hatch labelled building, periodic attention, with a small arrow to a box marked specialist arrangement. The cupboard is labelled occupant, contents only, with a note that the building does not open it.

A legend at the bottom lists the three roles: occupant, building and specialist. Each zone carries one role label for routine work and, where different, a second label for periodic work. The diagram fits on one page and is signed by both parties.

The value of the diagram is that it forces the threshold question into the open. A written list of tasks often hides the doorway, because neither party thinks of it as a zone.

Separating routine, periodic and specialist work

Once zones are agreed, sort the work into three layers. Routine work happens after each use or each day. Periodic work happens on a schedule. Specialist work happens when a material or item needs attention beyond the routine.

Routine work in a shared meeting room is usually small: clearing cups, wiping the table, resetting chairs, emptying a small bin, checking the screen is off. These tasks are best assigned to the occupant because they depend on knowing when the room was last used.

Periodic work covers floors, high surfaces and soft furnishings. This is where a building or an engaged provider usually sits. The map should state the intended interval and who confirms that the work happened, without turning the interval into a promise about outcomes.

Specialist work is the layer most often left blank. Carpets, curtains and upholstery may need attention that the routine team is not set up to give. The map should name the route rather than the method: who is authorised to arrange it, what approval is needed, and where the record is kept. Writing the map so it survives staff changes

A responsibility map fails quietly when the person who wrote it leaves. Build it so a new coordinator can pick it up without a briefing.

Use role names, not personal names. Write occupant coordinator, building facilities contact and specialist provider, then keep a separate contact sheet that can be updated without touching the map. Date the map and add a review line, for example a review every six months or whenever the room layout changes.

Give every task a single accountable role. Shared accountability reads well in a meeting and fails in practice. If two parties genuinely share a task, split it into two lines with a clear handoff point, such as the occupant clears the table and the building clears the corridor.

Add a short exceptions section. It should say what happens when a room is left in poor condition, when a booking overruns, and who decides that a mark needs specialist attention rather than routine cleaning. Keep the exceptions to behaviour and decisions, not to treatment steps.

Finally, record where the map lives. A shared folder that both parties can open is more useful than a signed copy in a drawer. The signed copy confirms agreement; the shared copy is what people actually use.

A sign-off worksheet for the first walkthrough

Use this worksheet during a joint walkthrough of each shared meeting room. It is an author heuristic, not a standard, and the timings are examples only.

Step one, fifteen minutes: walk the room with both parties present and read the zone list aloud. Mark any zone where the two parties disagree. Do not resolve disagreements in the room; note them and settle them in writing afterwards.

Step two, ten minutes: for each zone, write the routine owner and the periodic owner. Leave a blank where the answer is unknown rather than guessing.

Step three, ten minutes: list the specialist items in the room, such as carpet, curtains and upholstered chairs, and name the authorised route for each. Note who approves the arrangement and where the written confirmation is filed.

Step four, five minutes: agree the exceptions section and the review date. Both parties sign and date the map.

A simple completeness check you can apply afterwards: every zone has a routine owner, every specialist item has a named route, and every task has exactly one accountable role. If any line fails, the map is not ready to circulate.

Where specialist cleaning fits into the map

The map should point to specialist work without pretending to define it. Two common items in a shared meeting room are the carpet and any curtains or blinds.

For carpet, the map needs to record who reports a mark, who decides whether routine attention is enough, and who arranges periodic attention. The decision about when a mark needs professional attention belongs in the exceptions section, framed as a reporting and approval step rather than a cleaning instruction.

For curtains, the map should note who may take them down, who arranges the work and who confirms the rehang. Curtain work takes a room out of use, so the map should also record how the closure is communicated to other users of the shared room.

Keep the specialist layer short. Its job is to name the route and the approver, not to describe the work. A map that stays on one page is more likely to be read than one that grows into a manual.

Reviewing the map after the first month

A map written before use will contain at least one wrong assumption. Review it after the first month of operation, then move to a longer cycle.

At the review, ask three questions. Which zone caused the most confusion? Which task was done by the wrong party? Which specialist item was raised but had no clear route? Record the answers and amend only the lines that failed.

Keep a short log of exceptions raised during the month. The log does not need detail; a date, a zone and a one-line description is enough to show whether the map is working. If the same zone appears repeatedly, the map is wrong, not the people.

Finally, confirm that the contact sheet is current and that the review date is set. A responsibility map is a living document, and its value comes from being updated when the room, the users or the arrangements change.
