Preparing a Carpet Cleaning Brief for a Venue with a Rented Runner

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical guide for Sarawak venue managers and accommodation operators on separating a rented runner from fixed carpet, recording condition, confirming who may authorise cleaning, and writing a brief that a provider can act on.

Why a Rented Runner Needs Its Own Line in the Brief

A venue floor is rarely one thing. There is the fixed carpet that belongs to the building, and there is the temporary runner laid over it for an event, a season or a photo shoot. They may look like a single surface to a guest, but they have different owners, different replacement routes and different permission chains. A brief that treats them as one item invites two predictable problems: the wrong party is asked to approve work, and the condition record becomes ambiguous when the runner is rolled up and returned.

Start by listing every floor textile in the room as a separate line, even if two lines describe similar material. For each line, record what it is, where it sits, who owns it and whether it is fixed or temporary. A runner that arrived in a rental crate is a different entry from the broadloom it covers. If the runner is only present for part of the week, note the days it is down, because a cleaning window that falls on a day without the runner is wasted.

This guide is written for venue managers, accommodation operators and households in Sarawak who are preparing a brief before approaching a provider. It does not describe cleaning methods. It focuses on the paperwork and permissions that make a request clear. Separating the Temporary Runner from the Fixed Carpet

Write a short inventory before you write a request. The inventory is the backbone of the brief, and it prevents the most common confusion: a provider arriving to assess one surface and being pointed at another.

Use a table with one row per item. Suggested columns, offered as an author heuristic rather than a standard:

Item reference: a short code you will reuse in photos and emails, such as RUN-A or FIXED-MAIN. Type: temporary runner, fixed broadloom, mat, or other. Owner: building, tenant, rental supplier, or household. Placement: which room and which part of the floor. Presence: always down, event days only, or stored between uses. Condition note: a neutral description of what you can see. Permission contact: the person who may approve work on that item.

Keep the condition note factual. "Dark patch near the north entrance, roughly the size of a dinner plate" is useful. "Badly damaged" is not, because it invites disagreement about severity and gives a provider nothing to plan around. If you are unsure whether a mark is soiling or wear, say so and label it as needing assessment rather than deciding for the provider.

For the runner specifically, add two fields that fixed carpet does not need: the rental agreement reference and the return date. A runner with a return date creates a deadline that shapes the whole brief. If the return date is close, the brief should say plainly whether cleaning is expected before return, after return, or not at all, and who decided that.

Photographing Condition So Both Parties See the Same Thing

Photographs are the cheapest way to reduce disagreement, provided they are taken consistently. The goal is a neutral record, not a persuasive one.

A repeatable photo set, again as a suggested routine rather than a rule:

One wide shot of the room showing the runner in place relative to doors and furniture. One shot per corner of the runner, taken from standing height. Close shots of each noted mark, with something for scale such as a coin or a ruler laid beside it, not on it. One shot of any label, backing or edge binding that identifies the runner. One shot of the runner rolled or folded as it will be handed over, if it is leaving the venue.

Name the files with the item reference and the date so they sort together. Keep the original files rather than only messaging-app copies, which may be compressed. Store them where the person approving the work can reach them without asking you.

Two cautions. First, avoid photographing guests, staff faces, screens, booking boards or documents in the background; crop or reshoot instead. Second, do not use photographs to imply a diagnosis. A photo shows a mark; it does not establish what caused it or what should be done. The brief should present photos as evidence of condition and let the provider respond.

If the runner is rented, check whether the rental agreement restricts photography or requires the supplier to be notified of any cleaning. That is a contract question for the owner, not something to assume.

Confirming Who May Authorise Cleaning of a Rented Item

This is the section that most often decides whether a brief succeeds. A rented runner usually sits between at least three parties: the venue that uses it, the supplier that owns it, and sometimes a client or tenant who booked the event. Cleaning a rented item without the owner's agreement can create a dispute that no amount of good work resolves.

Build a permission line into the brief with three named roles, not names of companies alone:

Who requests the work: the venue contact who will send the brief. Who owns the item and must agree: the rental supplier's responsible contact. Who receives the item back: the person who signs the return, who may be different from the requester.

Then write one sentence stating what has been agreed so far. For example: "Supplier has confirmed in writing that cleaning may be arranged before the return date; venue will share condition photos before booking." If nothing has been agreed, say that instead, and mark the brief as pending permission. A brief that is honest about an open permission question is more useful than one that implies consent.

Where a provider's own terms apply, they attach to the items accepted and sit alongside quotations, invoices and written confirmations. Read those terms before you assume what a quotation covers, and keep the quotation, the confirmation and the condition record together in one folder. This is a documentation habit, not legal advice; where the rental agreement and the cleaning terms appear to conflict, ask the parties involved rather than deciding yourself.

Writing the Brief Itself: A Short Template

A brief that fits on one page is more likely to be read. The following structure is a suggested format you can adapt.

1. Purpose. One sentence: what you are asking for and why now. Example: "Request for assessment and cleaning of a rented runner before its return on the stated date."

2. Room and access. Room name, floor, entry point, lift or stair access, and the hours the room can be made available. Note any furniture that must be moved and who will move it. If the room is booked, give the gaps rather than a single preferred slot.

3. Item list. The inventory table from earlier, trimmed to the items in scope. State clearly that the fixed carpet is out of scope, or in scope, so there is no ambiguity at the door.

4. Condition record. Reference the photo set by file name and date. Summarise the noted marks in one or two lines each.

5. Ownership and permission. The three roles and the current status of agreement, as described above.

6. Handover and return. Who will be present at collection, how the runner will be rolled or packed, what identification travels with it, and who signs it back in. If the runner must return to a specific position in the room, say so.

7. Questions for the provider. List what you need answered before work begins: whether the item is within their scope, what they need from the owner, and what they will report back. Keep questions open rather than assuming answers.

8. Contacts and references. Role titles and the reference numbers already in use. Avoid putting personal contact details in a document that will be forwarded widely; point to the agreed channel instead.

A brief built this way also serves as the handover record. When the runner comes back, the same document tells the receiving person what to check.

Where Commercial Cleaning Fits, and Where It Does Not

Venues often hold more than one cleaning need at once, and it helps to keep them in separate requests even when one provider can handle both. A runner is a textile item with an owner and a return date. The surrounding room, corridor and shared areas are a different scope with different frequencies and different sign-off.

If the brief is part of a wider venue arrangement, describe the two scopes side by side rather than merging them. The runner line should state its owner and permission status. The room line should state the areas, the access window and who confirms completion. Mixing them can lead to a quotation that appears to cover the runner when it only covers floors, or the reverse.

A Pre-Send Check Before the Brief Leaves Your Desk

Run through this short check before sending. It is a working checklist, not an audited standard, and you should adjust it to your venue.

Is every floor textile in the room listed separately, with the runner clearly marked as temporary? Does each line name an owner and a permission contact? Is the fixed carpet explicitly in or out of scope? Do the photos match the item references and the date, and are they free of guests and documents? Is the current permission status stated honestly, including any open questions? Does the brief give access windows rather than a single demand? Is there a named person for collection and a named person for return? Are the questions for the provider open, with no assumed answers? Are the provider's terms and any quotation stored with the brief?

If any answer is no, fix that line before sending. A brief with one honest gap is easier to act on than a brief that looks complete but hides an unresolved permission.

Finally, keep the returned document. After the runner is back in place, add a short closing note: what was collected, what was returned, who signed, and any mark that remains. That note becomes the starting point for the next event season, and it means the next brief begins with a record rather than a memory.
