Aligning Laundry Service With a New Hotel Room-Turnover Timetable

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical guide for small hotel and venue operators in Sarawak testing a new room-turnover timetable: two checkout windows, a dated bag log, buffer stock and a clear laundry handoff.

Why a New Timetable Breaks the Old Linen Habit

Changing a room-turnover timetable is rarely a housekeeping problem first. It is a linen-flow problem. When checkout times shift, the moment linen leaves a room and the moment it must return both move, and the gap between them is what your laundry arrangement has to survive.

Start by writing down the timetable as it actually runs, not as the front desk wishes it ran. For each room type, note the checkout time, the realistic cleaning start, the time the room must be ready, and the linen set that leaves the room. Then mark the single point where clean linen must be on the shelf. That point is your cutoff, and everything in this guide is built around protecting it.

A useful discipline is to treat the timetable as a trial with a start and end date. Give it two weeks, record what happens each day, and decide at the end whether to keep it. A trial framing keeps the conversation about evidence rather than blame, which matters when housekeeping, front desk and an outside laundry provider all depend on the same bags.

If you are still deciding how the laundry side of the operation should be structured, the hotel laundry service page is a sensible starting point before you commit to a timetable. The rest of this guide assumes you have a timetable to test and need to make the linen flow fit it.

Design the Trial Around Two Checkout Windows

The clearest way to test a new timetable is to run two checkout windows rather than one. A common example is an early window for guests leaving before mid-morning and a later window for the rest. Two windows create two linen waves, and two waves are easier to staff than one large surge.

For each window, define four times: when the room is vacated, when housekeeping strips it, when the bag is sealed and logged, and when the bag must be collected or dropped off. Keep the four times visible on a single sheet in the housekeeping office. If the sheet lives only in someone's phone, the timetable is not really in force.

Two windows also let you test the timetable against real variation. A late checkout, a room held for inspection, or a same-day re-let all land differently depending on which window they fall into. Record those exceptions daily; they are the data that tells you whether the timetable is workable or merely tidy on paper.

Short-stay operators face a different pattern. A serviced apartment or homestay may have one guest leaving and another arriving within hours, with no housekeeping team on site. If that describes part of your building, the Airbnb and short-stay laundry page covers the tighter, smaller-volume rhythm that sits alongside hotel turnover.

Keep a Dated Bag Log, Not a Mental Count

The bag log is the single most useful document in a turnover trial. It does not need to be complicated. One row per bag, with the date, the room or floor it came from, the item count, the time it left the building, the expected return, and the name of the person who sealed it.

Dated entries matter because linen disputes are almost always about time. A bag logged on Tuesday and returned on Thursday tells a clear story; a bag remembered vaguely does not. The log also gives you a simple way to spot a pattern, such as one floor consistently running late or one item type always short at the end of the week.

Pair the log with a physical label that matches the row. A bag without a label is a bag whose contents belong to nobody in particular, and that is how shortages begin. Number the bags in a series and reuse the numbers so the log and the shelf speak the same language.

Where the trial involves items beyond bed linen, such as curtains or soft furnishings taken out of a room for attention, note them on the same log rather than a separate one. Set Buffer Stock From the Log, Not From a Guess

Buffer stock is the linen you hold back so that a late return does not close a room. The right amount is not a fixed number; it is whatever covers your worst realistic day during the trial.

A practical method is to review the log weekly and find the largest gap between the linen that left and the linen that came back on time. Add a small margin to that gap and treat the result as your buffer for the following week. This is an author's heuristic, not a published standard, but it keeps the buffer tied to your own records rather than to habit.

Buffer stock only works if it is stored separately and counted. Linen that drifts into general circulation stops being a buffer. Keep the reserve on its own shelf, mark it, and restock it first when clean linen returns.

Make the Handoff a Named Event

A handoff is not a bag left by a door. It is a moment with a person, a count and a signature. During the trial, name the person handing over and the person receiving, and record both on the log.

At handover, count the items against the bag label and note any item that is stained, torn or otherwise flagged for attention. Flagging at handover protects both sides: it separates wear that arrived with the item from anything that happens later. Keep the flagged items in a separate bag so they do not hold up the main wash cycle.

Agree in advance what happens when a bag is short. The simplest rule is that a short bag is logged and reported the same day, not saved up for a weekly reconciliation. Same-day reporting keeps the count close to the event and makes the log trustworthy.

If your trial includes rooms with curtains, rugs or upholstery coming out for cleaning, the handoff needs a second track. Curtain cleaning is priced by weight, with the final cost depending on fabric type, condition and whether removal or rehanging support is needed, so those items should be logged separately from bed linen even when they travel on the same day.

Where Room Cleaning Changes the Cutoff

Laundry is only one half of turnover. The other half is the room itself, and some cleaning tasks move the cutoff in ways linen never does.

Carpet cleaning is the clearest example. A room with a carpet that needs attention cannot be returned to service on the same schedule as a room that only needs linen and a bathroom. If a small rug or a compact carpet needs separate cleaning, confirm the scope and allow for any effect on room availability. Build those rooms into the trial as planned exceptions rather than discovering them on the day.

Soft furnishings follow the same logic. Curtains taken down for cleaning leave a room unfinished until they are rehung. If your trial timetable assumes every room returns on the same day, add a rule for rooms with fabric work in progress: they are marked out of service on the board, not counted as late.

For buildings that also run shared areas, corridors or back-of-house spaces, the commercial cleaning side of the operation can be planned alongside the room schedule so that public areas are not cleaned at the same moment housekeeping needs the lifts and service corridors.

Contrast Hotel Turnover With Short-Stay Turnover

A hotel timetable and a short-stay timetable look similar on a spreadsheet and behave very differently in practice.

A hotel has volume, a housekeeping team and a linen store. Its risk is a wave of rooms arriving at once and a laundry return that misses the wave. A short-stay property has small volume, often no on-site team, and a guest arriving at an unpredictable hour. Its risk is a single missed set closing the only room available.

That difference changes the buffer. A hotel can hold a reserve across many rooms; a short-stay operator may need a complete spare set for each unit, because there is no pool to draw from. It also changes the handoff: a hotel hands over through a housekeeping supervisor, while a short-stay operator may hand over directly at a counter or through a pickup and delivery arrangement.

If your building mixes both, run the two rhythms on separate logs for the trial period. Combining them hides which one is causing the late returns.

Review the Trial and Decide What to Keep

At the end of the trial, review the log rather than the memory of the week. Three questions are enough. Did clean linen reach the shelf before the cutoff on the days that mattered? Were the exceptions recorded, and were they the same exceptions each time? Did the buffer hold, or was it used every day?

If the buffer was used every day, the timetable is asking for more linen than the building holds, and the answer is either more buffer or a slower timetable. If exceptions cluster around one room type or one floor, the fix is local. If the log is thin or incomplete, the trial has not really been tested and should run again with a stricter handoff.

Keep the documents that worked: the two-window sheet, the dated bag log and the buffer count. They are the basis for any future change, and they make a conversation with a laundry provider concrete rather than general.
