Preparing a Photo Addendum When Short-Stay Textiles Are Marked Out of Service

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical guide for Sarawak accommodation operators and venue managers on building a photo addendum that records unavailable short-stay textiles, flags substitute stock from another room, and closes the record once a provider has checked the items.

Why a Photo Addendum Is Not an Inventory Count

An inventory count answers one question: how many items are in the building. A photo addendum answers a different question: which specific items are unavailable, why, and what is standing in for them right now. The two records can sit side by side, but they should not be merged. A count that says twelve bath towels tells you nothing about the two towels pulled from Room 4 because the Room 7 set was set aside. If those two facts live in the same spreadsheet column, the next person to read it will assume twelve usable towels exist when only ten are in circulation.

Treat the addendum as an exception record. It opens when a textile leaves normal service and closes when someone with the authority to check it has done so. Ordinary counting continues on its own sheet, on its own rhythm. The addendum only carries the items that a count would otherwise misrepresent.

This distinction matters most for short-stay properties, where a room can be rebooked the same day. A missing pillowcase is not a stock number problem; it is a room-turn problem, and the record needs to show which room is affected and what was substituted. Keep the addendum short, dated and tied to a room or area, so housekeeping and whoever handles the laundry service handoff are reading the same story.

What Belongs in the Addendum Header

Before any photograph is taken, fix the header fields. A photo without a header is just a picture; a photo with a room reference, a date, an item description and a reason for withdrawal becomes evidence that can be checked later.

A workable header set, offered as an example rather than a standard:

Record reference and date opened. Property and room or area (for example, a fictional unit such as Unit 12B or the first-floor corridor). Item type and quantity withdrawn, described plainly (two pillowcases, one hand towel, one cushion cover). Reason for withdrawal, in neutral words: torn seam, mark noticed at changeover, colour run, item held back pending a question. Who withdrew it and who currently holds it. Substitute issued, with the source room or store noted. Status: open, awaiting provider check, or closed.

Keep the reason factual. "Mark noticed at changeover" is a record; "ruined by previous guest" is a conclusion the addendum is not entitled to draw. If the cause is unknown, write that it is unknown. The point of the addendum is to preserve what was seen, not to assign blame before anyone has looked at the item.

Where the property already keeps customer terms and conditions on file for cleaning handoffs, the addendum should reference the same item descriptions used there, so a provider checking the record is comparing like with like. Photographing the Item and the Substitute

Two photographs usually do more work than ten. The first shows the withdrawn item in a way that makes its condition legible: laid flat, in even light, with something for scale if size matters. The second shows the substitute in place, so a reader can see which room is now using which stock.

A short shooting routine, again as an example:

Photograph the item before it is bagged, not after. Include a handwritten or printed card with the record reference in the frame if your process allows it, so the image cannot drift away from its entry. Take one close frame of the specific area in question and one wider frame showing the whole item. For the substitute, photograph the made bed or the shelf position, not just the folded item, so the room context is visible. Name the files with the record reference and a sequence number.

Avoid editing beyond cropping and rotation. A record that has been brightened or filtered invites the question of what was changed. If a mark is faint, say so in the text rather than enhancing the image.

This is also where substitute stock from a different room needs explicit labelling. If a towel from Unit 9 is now serving Unit 12B, the addendum should say so in words and show it in the second photograph. Otherwise the next count will find Unit 9 short and nobody will know why. The same discipline applies to any specialist item pulled from another area, whether that is a cushion cover or a soft toy left in a family unit; toy cleaning support covers soft toys, plushies and similar handled items, and an item of that kind leaving a room deserves its own line rather than being folded into a general linen note.

Separating Substitute Stock From Ordinary Transfers

Not every item that moves between rooms is a substitute. A deliberate transfer, agreed in advance and recorded as a transfer, is a different event from an emergency substitution made at changeover because the correct stock was unavailable. The addendum should only carry the second kind.

A simple test helps. Ask whether the move was planned. If housekeeping decided last week that Unit 9 would supply Unit 12B for a long booking, that is a transfer and belongs in the transfer log. If a housekeeper discovered at 2pm that the Unit 12B set was unusable and took the nearest equivalent, that is a substitution and belongs in the addendum, with the source room named.

Why keep them apart? Because they close differently. A transfer closes when the planned arrangement ends. A substitution closes when the original item returns to service or is formally retired, and the borrowed item goes home. Mixing the two produces a record that never quite closes, and an operator who stops trusting it.

For properties that also send commercial fabrics and heavier textiles out for cleaning, the same logic applies to any item borrowed from a function room or shared store. Name the source, name the destination, and note the date the arrangement should be reviewed. If the borrowed item is itself due for cleaning, say so, so the two events do not collide.

Handing the Record to a Provider for Checking

The addendum reaches its most useful point when a cleaning provider has actually looked at the item. Until then, the record describes what your staff saw. After the check, it can describe what was found, and that is what allows a decision about returning the item to service or retiring it.

A practical handoff sequence:

Attach the addendum reference to whatever paperwork travels with the items, so the provider can match the record to the physical item. State clearly what you are asking for: a condition check, a view on whether the item can be cleaned, or simply confirmation that the item was received. Ask for the response in writing, even if it is a short note, and file it against the record reference. Record the date the response arrived and who logged it.

Closing the Record

A record that is never closed becomes clutter, and clutter is what makes people stop reading addenda. Set a closing rule and apply it consistently.

Three closing outcomes cover most cases:

Returned to service. The item came back, was checked, and is usable. Note the date and who confirmed it. Retired. The item will not return. Note the date, the reason in neutral terms, and what replaced it permanently. Reassigned. The item is now used somewhere else, for example as a spare rather than a guest-facing piece. Note the new location.

In each case, the substitute stock should be released back to its home room or store, and that release should be recorded on the same line. An addendum that closes the original item but forgets the borrowed one leaves a second, quieter problem behind.

A short review rhythm helps. Once a week, look at open records and ask two questions: is anything waiting on a provider response that has not arrived, and is any substitute still in place longer than intended? A One-Page Worksheet You Can Copy

The following layout is offered as an example format, not a standard. Adapt the fields to your property.

Line 1: Record reference, date opened, property, room or area. Line 2: Item type and quantity, described plainly. Line 3: Reason for withdrawal, in neutral words, with "cause unknown" where that is true. Line 4: Withdrawn by, currently held by. Line 5: Substitute issued, source room or store, date issued. Line 6: Photographs attached, with file names matching the record reference. Line 7: Provider check requested on, response received on, response summary. Line 8: Outcome (returned, retired, reassigned), date closed, substitute released on.

Two habits make the worksheet work. First, write the reason before taking the photograph, so the image is taken to illustrate a stated fact rather than to discover one. Second, close the line the same day the outcome is known. A record closed a month later is a record reconstructed from memory.

Keep the addendum separate from your inventory count, separate from your transfer log, and separate from the provider's own paperwork. It is a narrow record with a narrow job: to show what was unavailable, what stood in for it, and how the matter was settled. Done that way, it stays short enough that housekeeping will actually fill it in, which is the only test of a record that matters.
