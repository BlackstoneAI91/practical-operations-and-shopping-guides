Laundry Service Specification Checklist for a Small Hotel Tender in Sarawak

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical guide for small hotel and accommodation operators in Kuching and Miri on writing a laundry service specification for a tender, with a worked hypothetical and a reusable checklist.

Why a Written Specification Matters More Than a Price Sheet

A small hotel tender usually arrives as a stack of documents with a price schedule at the back. It is tempting to fill in the rates and send it back. But the price schedule only works if the specification in front of it is clear about what the laundry service is expected to do. Two bidders can quote very different numbers simply because they read the scope differently: one assumes bed linen only, another assumes linen plus towels plus staff uniforms; one assumes daily collection, another assumes three collections a week.

For a small property in Sarawak, the specification is where you protect yourself. It tells every bidder what to count, how to handle stains, what happens to damaged items, and how the handover is recorded. It also gives you a fair basis for comparing bids, because you are comparing the same job rather than two interpretations of it.

This guide is written for facilities managers and accommodation operators preparing a tender, not for bidders. It sets out the sections a specification should contain, a worked hypothetical to show how the numbers fit together, and a reusable checklist you can adapt to your own property. Treat the figures in the example as illustrations, not benchmarks.

Start With an Item Inventory, Not a Word Template

Before writing a single clause, walk the property and count what actually gets laundered. Group items by type: bed sheets, pillowcases, duvet covers, bath towels, hand towels, bath mats, face cloths, table linen, and staff uniforms. For each type, record the quantity in circulation, the average weight or size band, and the fabric composition from the care label. Note which items are owned by the hotel and which are supplied by a third party, because ownership changes who bears replacement cost.

This inventory does two things. First, it converts vague language like "all hotel linen" into a countable list that bidders can price. Second, it exposes items that are easy to forget: pool towels, spa robes, kitchen cloths, and the curtains and carpets that are not part of the daily laundry cycle but still need periodic specialist cleaning. A specification that mentions only bed linen will leave those items unaddressed.

Keep the inventory as an appendix to the tender and require bidders to price against it line by line. Define the Service Cycle, Turnaround and Pickup Terms

The heart of the specification is the service cycle. State how often linen is collected, how many sets are in circulation, and what turnaround time the property needs. A small hotel with one set per room and no buffer is far more exposed to a missed collection than one holding three sets. Write the buffer into the specification so bidders understand the volume they must absorb.

Spell out the pickup and delivery arrangement: which entrance is used, who signs items in and out, what time window applies, and how discrepancies are recorded. If the property expects laundry pickup and delivery as part of the contract, say so explicitly and describe the access constraints, such as a service lift, a narrow back lane, or a reception desk that is unstaffed overnight.

Include the handling rules that matter to you: how soiled linen is bagged, how clean linen is protected in transit, and how items are separated by department. Quality, Stain and Damage Clauses

Quality clauses are where many small tenders stay vague. Replace "items returned clean" with observable criteria. For example: no visible staining under normal lighting, no residual odour, no excessive shrinkage beyond the fabric's stated tolerance, and no colour transfer between items. State how the property will inspect and what happens when an item fails inspection.

Stain handling deserves its own clause. Decide whether the bidder is expected to attempt stain removal on every item or only on items flagged at handover. Require a record of items that could not be restored, and agree in advance who bears the cost when an item is damaged beyond use. The same logic applies to dry cleaning of delicate items such as uniforms with structured shoulders or guest garments, where the care label governs the process.

Worked Hypothetical: A Twelve-Room Guesthouse

Picture a hypothetical twelve-room guesthouse in Kuching with a smaller sister property in Miri. Each room holds one bed sheet set, two pillowcases, one duvet cover, two bath towels, and two hand towels. The property keeps three sets per room in circulation, giving a buffer for peak weekends. Staff uniforms and restaurant linen are added to the same pool.

In the specification, the operator lists each item type, the quantity in circulation, and the expected weekly volume. Collection is set at three times a week, with a stated turnaround of two working days and a named person at reception who signs the manifest. Stain handling is limited to items flagged at handover, and the operator asks bidders to describe how they record and return unrestorable items.

The operator also adds a separate line for periodic specialist work: curtain cleaning for the function room twice a year, and carpet cleaning for the lobby and corridor on a scheduled basis. These are not part of the daily laundry cycle, but leaving them out of the tender means negotiating them later. A laundry service for hotels tender is stronger when it names the full textile estate, including the items that are cleaned occasionally rather than weekly.

Reusable Checklist Before You Issue the Tender

Use this checklist as a final pass before the tender goes out. It is an author's heuristic, not an industry standard, so adapt it to your property.

One: item inventory attached, with quantities, fabric notes, and ownership. Two: service cycle stated, including collection frequency, turnaround, and buffer stock. Three: pickup and delivery access described, with signing responsibility named. Four: quality criteria written as observable checks, not adjectives. Five: stain and damage process agreed, including who bears replacement cost. Six: bidder terms requested for comparison. Seven: periodic specialist work listed separately, covering curtains, carpets, and any delicate garments. Eight: a manifest template included so every handover is recorded the same way.

If your property also runs short-stay units, add a line for higher-turnover linen and reference the relevant service page.
