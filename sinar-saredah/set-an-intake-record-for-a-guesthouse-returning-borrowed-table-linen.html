Set an Intake Record for a Guesthouse Returning Borrowed Table Linen

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical guide for Sarawak guesthouses and venue managers on recording borrowed table linen at intake, separating it from lodging stock, and using a short signoff worksheet before handing it to a laundry service.

Why borrowed table linen needs its own intake record

A guesthouse usually runs two kinds of linen through the same back corridor: lodging stock that belongs to the property, and event stock borrowed from a caterer, a hall, a neighbour or a hire company. When both arrive at the counter in the same bag, the person counting has no way to tell which pieces must go back to an owner and which simply return to the cupboard. That is how a borrowed cloth ends up in the general rotation and disappears from the owner's count.

An intake record fixes this by giving borrowed table linen a separate identity at the moment it re-enters the building. It is not a cleaning instruction and it is not a legal document. It is a short written trace of what came back, from whom, in what condition, and who is responsible for the next step. Keep it beside the handover point rather than in an office drawer, because the record is only useful if the person receiving the bag can complete it.

Separate lodging stock from event stock before counting

The first decision is a sorting decision, not a cleaning one. Before anyone counts a single cloth, agree on a physical divider: a labelled trolley shelf, two coloured bags, or two crates that never travel together. Borrowed table linen goes into the owner's container; guesthouse linen stays in the property container. If a piece is ambiguous, treat it as borrowed until the owner confirms otherwise, because an item wrongly returned to the cupboard is harder to trace than one held back for a question.

Write the owner's name on the record, not on the fabric. Labels pinned or written onto hired linen can breach a hire agreement, so keep identification on the bag tag and the intake sheet. Record the owner's reference exactly as they wrote it, even if it looks like a code, and add your own internal reference beside it. Two references let you match the piece to the hire paperwork and to your own storage position without guessing.

This is also the moment to note what the item is: a round tablecloth, a rectangular cloth, a runner, a napkin set, a chair cover. Use the owner's words where they exist. A description that matches the hire list is far easier to reconcile at return than a house shorthand nobody else recognises.

Build the intake worksheet: six fields that do the work

A workable worksheet fits on one page. Six fields cover most guesthouse situations, and each one answers a question someone will ask later.

First, the date and time of receipt, plus the name of the staff member who accepted the bag. Second, the owner and their reference. Third, the item description and count, written as a number and a noun so it can be checked at a glance. Fourth, the condition as observed at intake, described in plain words such as creased, marked, damp or intact, without diagnosing a cause. Fifth, where the item is being stored and in which container. Sixth, the next action and who owns it, for example hold for owner inspection, send for cleaning, or return unwashed.

Add a signature line for the person handing over and the person receiving. If the handover happens at a back door with no desk, use a phone photo of the completed sheet as a temporary copy and file the paper version the same day. The worksheet is a record of custody, so the signature matters more than the neatness of the handwriting.

Record condition without turning it into a cleaning brief

Condition notes are the part of the intake record most likely to drift into instructions. Keep them observational. A note that says a cloth has a dark patch near one corner is a record. A note that says how to remove the patch is a treatment decision that belongs with the owner and the cleaning provider, not with the person at the counter.

Where the owner has supplied written care information, attach or reference it rather than paraphrasing it. If the owner has said nothing, record that fact plainly: no care information supplied. That single line protects everyone later, because it shows the question was noticed at intake rather than after the item came back changed.

For guesthouses that also send their own lodging linen out, keep the two records apart. A commercial laundry service handling hotel and guesthouse linen works from its own scope and confirmation, and mixing borrowed event stock into that flow makes both records harder to reconcile. If you are comparing how a provider documents collection and return, look at what confirmation they give at handover rather than assuming a standard.

Decide who authorises the next step

Before the bag is closed, name the person who can authorise cleaning, replacement or return of borrowed table linen. In a small guesthouse this is often the owner-manager; in a larger venue it may be a housekeeping supervisor acting within a stated limit. Write the role on the worksheet, not just a name, so the record still makes sense when staff change.

Then decide the default. Two sensible defaults exist: hold all borrowed stock until the owner inspects it, or release it for cleaning only when the owner has confirmed in writing. Either works, provided the intake sheet shows which default applied on that date. A default that lives only in someone's memory is not a record.

Where the owner wants the item cleaned before return, ask them to confirm the scope in writing and keep that confirmation with the intake sheet. The provider's own terms and any quotation or written service confirmation form part of that picture, so store them together rather than in separate folders. This is a documentation habit, not legal advice, and any hire agreement should be read on its own terms.

Close the loop when the linen comes back

The intake record is only half the loop. When borrowed table linen returns from cleaning or from storage, complete a matching line on the same sheet: date returned to owner, count returned, condition at return, and the name of the person who accepted it back. If the count differs, note the difference and who was informed, without speculating about the cause.

Keep completed sheets for a period your guesthouse sets and states, then review them together at a quiet point in the calendar. A short monthly look at the sheets shows which owners' stock recurs, which items are consistently short, and where the handover point is being bypassed. That review is a management habit, not a compliance requirement.

If your property also handles other specialist items, the same custody logic applies to curtains, carpets and soft furnishings, and providers in Kuching and Miri may offer related services under one confirmation. The intake sheet does not need to cover those categories, but it should point to where their records live so nothing is orphaned.

A short pre-handover checklist

Run this checklist before any borrowed table linen leaves the building, whether it goes to an owner or to a laundry service. It is an author's heuristic, not an audited standard, and a guesthouse should adjust it to its own size.

One: is the item in the owner's container, physically separate from lodging stock? Two: does the sheet carry the owner's reference and an internal reference? Three: is the count written as a number and a noun? Four: is the condition described in observation only, with no treatment wording? Five: is the care information either attached or recorded as not supplied? Six: is the authorising role named? Seven: has the handover been signed by both sides?

If any answer is no, hold the item and resolve the gap before it moves. A two-minute pause at the counter is cheaper than a disputed count a week later. For properties that also rely on pickup and delivery, agree in advance where the signed sheet travels with the bag and who files the returned copy.
