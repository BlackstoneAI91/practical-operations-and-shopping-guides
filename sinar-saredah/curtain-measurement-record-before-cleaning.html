Recording Curtain Measurements and Fixings Before Cleaning

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical record sheet for Sarawak households, guesthouses and venue managers to log curtain measurements, headings and fixings before curtain cleaning, so rehang after cleaning is predictable.

Why a measurement record matters before curtain cleaning

Curtains are one of the few household or venue items that are made to fit a specific opening. A sofa can be moved to another room; a curtain panel cut for a particular window rarely fits anywhere else. Once panels leave the building for cleaning, the only reliable memory of how they hung is the one you wrote down.

A measurement record does three jobs. First, it protects the fit: if a panel comes back shorter, wider or with a heading that no longer matches the track, the record shows what was sent. Second, it speeds up rehang: whoever puts the curtains back does not have to guess which panel went where or how the pleats were spaced. Third, it supports any later conversation about condition, because the record captures the state of the fabric and hardware at handover rather than after the fact.

This guide is written for Sarawak households, accommodation operators and venue managers who are sending curtains out for cleaning. It sets out a record sheet you can fill in before handover, with fields for measurements, headings, fixings and room notes. Setting up the record sheet: fields that earn their place

A useful record sheet is short enough to complete in a few minutes per window but detailed enough that a stranger could rehang the curtains from it. The fields below are a starting point; add or drop columns to suit your building.

Identification: a record number, the date, the property or unit, the room name, and who completed the sheet. For accommodation, use the same room naming as your housekeeping list so the record can be matched to a room turn later.

Window and panel details: window reference, number of panels, and which panel is left, right or centre. If panels are a pair, note whether they are mirror images or identical.

Measurements: finished drop (top of heading to bottom hem), finished width per panel, and the width of the opening the curtains cover. Record whether the drop was measured with the curtain hanging or laid flat, because the two can differ.

Heading and pleat details: heading type as you see it, pleat spacing if visible, and the position of any tie-backs or holdbacks.

Fixings: track or pole type, bracket positions, hook type, and the number of hooks or rings per panel.

Condition notes: existing marks, fading, loose stitching, missing hooks, bent brackets. Photograph these rather than describing them in prose alone.

Return expectations: who will rehang, and the room or storage location the panels should return to.

A worked fictional example: Room 12 at a small guesthouse has two panels on a ceiling track, drop 210 cm, each panel 140 cm wide, pencil-pleat heading, 14 hooks per panel, one bracket noted as slightly loose on the left. The sheet records all of this, plus a photo of the loose bracket, before the panels are bagged.

Measuring drop and width consistently

Inconsistent measuring is the most common reason a record sheet fails. Two people measuring the same curtain can produce different numbers if one measures from the hook and the other from the top of the heading. Agree a convention before you begin and write it on the sheet.

For drop, the practical convention is to measure from the point where the curtain hangs on the fixing down to the bottom hem, with the curtain hanging if possible. If the curtain must be taken down first, measure it laid flat and note that on the sheet. Record to the nearest centimetre and do not round in a way that hides a difference between panels.

For width, measure each panel separately rather than doubling a single panel. Panels in a pair are sometimes slightly different, especially after previous cleaning or alterations, and the record should show the real numbers. Also record the opening width so a future reader can see how much fullness the curtains were made with.

For venue managers with many identical windows, measure one representative window carefully and then confirm the others match rather than assuming. A single mismatched panel in a long corridor is easy to miss until rehang day.

Keep the tape measure and the sheet together, and write measurements as you go rather than from memory afterwards. If a second person checks the numbers, note who verified them.

Logging headings, hooks and hardware

Measurements alone will not get curtains back on the track. The heading and hardware record is what makes rehang predictable, particularly when panels from several rooms are cleaned together.

For the heading, describe what you see in plain words: pencil pleat, pinch pleat, tab top, eyelet, rod pocket, or another type. Note the number of hooks or rings per panel and, where visible, the spacing between pleats. If the heading has a gathered tape, note whether the cords are intact and whether the gathering was set before removal.

For the fixings, record the track or pole type, the number and position of brackets, and any end stops or returns. Note anything already loose, bent or missing before handover. This is not a repair instruction; it is a record so that a pre-existing fault is not mistaken for damage that appeared during cleaning.

Bag hardware separately from fabric where practical. Hooks, rings and small brackets have a habit of disappearing when panels are folded, and a labelled bag attached to the record number keeps them with the right set. For a venue with many rooms, a simple convention such as room number plus panel letter on the bag label prevents mix-ups at return.

If the curtains are being sent as part of a wider bundle that includes other specialist items, keep the curtain record separate so it is not lost among unrelated notes. A handover checklist you can score

Before curtains leave the building, run through a short checklist and score each line as complete, partial or missing. The scoring below is an author heuristic for internal use, not a published standard.

Record number assigned and written on the sheet and on each bag: complete, partial or missing. Room and window identified using the same naming as your housekeeping or facilities list. Panel count matches the number of panels actually removed. Drop and width recorded for every panel, with the measuring convention noted. Heading type and hook or ring count recorded. Hardware bagged and labelled, with pre-existing faults noted. Condition photos taken, including any existing marks and loose fixings. Return location and rehang responsibility agreed in writing.

A simple rule of thumb: if any line is missing, do not send the curtains yet. A few extra minutes at the window is cheaper than a rehang that has to be redone because nobody knows which panel went where.

For accommodation operators, add one line for room availability: the date the room needs the curtains back. That single field turns the record into a scheduling tool as well as a condition log.

Matching the record to the cleaning handover

The record sheet is most useful when it travels with the curtains and comes back with them. Ask the cleaning provider what intake information they need, and make sure your sheet and their notes describe the same panels, counts and condition points. Where the two differ, resolve it before cleaning begins rather than after.

That means the written record you keep should align with whatever confirmation you receive. If you are sending curtains from several rooms, list them on the confirmation in the same order as your record sheet so a return can be checked line by line.

On return, use the sheet in reverse. Count the panels, check the hardware bag against the recorded hook and ring counts, compare the drop and width if the provider reports any change, and confirm the condition notes still match. Then rehang and note the date. A completed record with a return date becomes a useful history for the next time those curtains need attention.

For households, the same approach works at a smaller scale: one sheet per window, one bag per set of hardware, and a note of who will rehang. For venue managers, keep completed sheets in a folder by room so patterns become visible over time, such as a particular window whose brackets loosen repeatedly.

Where this record fits in a wider cleaning plan

Curtain records rarely stand alone. In a guesthouse or venue, curtains are cleaned alongside linen, soft furnishings and other items, and the same discipline of counting, labelling and noting condition applies across all of them.

If you are planning curtain cleaning as part of a room refresh, decide first whether the room can be out of service for the rehang window, then build the measurement record around that date. The record sheet described here is the input; the rehang check is the output. Keep the two documents linked by the same record number so a future reader can follow the whole cycle.
