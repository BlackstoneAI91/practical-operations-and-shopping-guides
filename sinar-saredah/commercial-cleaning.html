Commercial Cleaning Scope Comparison for Shared Buildings in Sarawak

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical comparison of common-area, washroom and floor-care scope options for venue managers, accommodation operators and households in Sarawak who share a building and need to choose a coverage level that matches how the space is actually used.

Start With How the Building Is Used, Not With a Cleaning Package

Before comparing scope options, write down who uses the shared parts of your building and when. A shophouse with a ground-floor office and two upstairs tenants has a different pattern from a guest property with back-to-back checkouts, and both differ from a venue that fills on event nights and sits quiet midweek. List the shared entrances, corridors, stairwells, lifts, washrooms, pantries, bin areas and any carpeted or curtained zones. For each, note the busiest hours, the number of people passing through, and whether visitors or only residents use it. This short inventory does the work that a generic package cannot: it tells you which areas need daily attention and which can be handled on a longer cycle. Only then does it make sense to look at coverage levels. Coverage Level One: Common Areas Only

The lightest useful scope covers the parts every occupant walks through: entrance lobbies, corridors, stair treads and handrails, lift interiors, shared door handles and light switches, and bin collection points. The work is mostly touch-point hygiene, spot sweeping and mopping, and keeping clutter from accumulating. This level suits a building where individual tenants clean inside their own units and only the shared circulation space is in question. It also fits a venue between events, when the priority is resetting public areas rather than deep work. When you write the brief, be specific about frequency and about what counts as done: which floors are mopped, whether stair edges are included, whether external five-foot ways are in or out. A short written list prevents the common mismatch where a manager expects corridors twice a day and the schedule covers once. If your building also holds carpets in these shared zones, note them separately, because carpet care is a different task from hard-floor mopping and is usually quoted on its own basis.

Coverage Level Two: Common Areas Plus Washrooms and Pantries

Shared washrooms and staff pantries are where scope decisions matter most, because they are used by everyone and owned by no one. Adding them to the brief means agreeing on restocking expectations, bin emptying, surface wiping, floor care and how often a deeper clean happens. Decide who supplies consumables and who reports faults such as a leaking tap or a blocked drain, since cleaning staff should be recording problems rather than quietly working around them. A customer-facing building raises the bar further. Coverage Level Three: Floor Care and Sensitive Zones

Floor care is the part of a scope most often under-specified. Hard floors, carpeted meeting rooms and raised or cable-heavy areas each need a different approach, and a single mop-and-bucket routine will not serve all three. Where a building houses technical equipment, the brief should say so plainly. The brand's published notes on its IRIX Sarawak work describe a structured site cleaning programme for a data centre and technology-driven environment, covering office cleaning, shared-space hygiene, meeting room care, surface sanitisation, dust control, HEPA-filtered vacuuming, anti-static cleaning practices, low-moisture floor care and environmentally mindful cleaning products, with work planned around sensitive equipment, access-controlled rooms, cable routes, raised floors and operational teams. You do not need a data centre to borrow the logic: identify any room where dust, moisture or movement is a risk, mark it in the brief, and agree what cleaning method and access window applies before work starts. For ordinary carpeted shared areas, a separate carpet cleaning scope keeps expectations clear about which pieces are included.

A Scope Comparison Worksheet You Can Fill In

Use a simple table with one row per shared area and five columns: area, who uses it, desired frequency, tasks included, and tasks excluded. Fill it in with the building in front of you rather than from a template. A worked fictional example: a three-storey shophouse in Sarawak with a ground-floor office, two rented upper units and a shared stairwell. The manager lists the lobby and stairwell as daily sweep and mop with handrail wipe; the shared washroom on each floor as daily with consumables supplied by the landlord; the small carpeted meeting room as vacuum twice weekly with a separate annual carpet clean; and the back corridor as three times weekly. Excluded tasks are written down too: no internal unit cleaning, no external window work, no pest control. That last column is the one people forget, and it is the one that causes disputes. When the table is complete, it becomes the basis of a quotation request and, later, of the written confirmation that accompanies the job. Matching Frequency to Occupancy and Event Cycles

Frequency should follow the calendar, not habit. A guest property with weekend turnover needs heavier common-area attention on changeover days and lighter midweek. A venue with evening events needs a reset after each booking and a check before the next one. An office building may need almost nothing at weekends. Map your busiest and quietest weeks and set the schedule against them, then agree how a one-off deep clean is requested when something unusual happens. Treat any published range as a starting point for a conversation about your site, not as a fixed quote, since area, frequency and access all change the work involved.

Handover, Access and the Paper Trail

A scope is only as good as its handover. Agree access arrangements in writing: who holds keys, which doors are opened at what hour, where equipment is stored, and who signs off completed work. Keep a simple log of visits, issues and follow-ups so that a missed corridor or a recurring washroom problem is visible rather than remembered. Where a building also sends textiles or soft furnishings for cleaning, keep those jobs in the same file, because the same terms and confirmations govern them.
