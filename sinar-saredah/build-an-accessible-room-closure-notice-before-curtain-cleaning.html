Build an Accessible Room-Closure Notice Before Curtain Cleaning

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical guide for Sarawak households, accommodation operators and venue managers on drafting a clear, readable room-closure notice before curtain cleaning, covering scheduling, alternate shared spaces, staff briefing and who to ask about changes.

Why the notice comes before the booking

Curtain cleaning takes a room out of use, and the notice is what turns that disruption into something people can plan around. The order matters: decide the closure window, write the notice, then confirm the booking. If you book first and write the notice afterwards, you often discover that the dates clash with a guest stay, a family event or a staff roster, and the notice becomes an apology instead of a plan.

A useful notice answers four questions in plain language: which room is closed, when it is closed, where people should go instead, and who to ask if something changes. Everything else is decoration. Keep the notice short enough to read in a corridor and specific enough that nobody has to guess.

This guide treats accessibility as a writing and placement discipline, not a compliance claim. The aim is a notice that a wide range of readers can find, read and act on: large clear text, plain wording, more than one location, and a named contact route. Whether a particular building meets any formal standard is a separate question for the people who manage it.

Decide the closure window before you write a word

The notice can only be as precise as the schedule behind it. Before drafting, settle three things with the people who control the room.

First, the earliest possible start. Curtains usually have to come down before they travel, so the room is effectively unavailable from the moment takedown begins, not from the moment the cleaning is finished. Write the closure from takedown, not from collection.

Second, the latest possible reopening. Rehanging, checking fixings and airing the room all take time after the curtains return. Build that back into the window rather than promising a reopening the same hour the items come back.

Third, a buffer you are willing to state. A notice that says a room reopens on a given day and then slips is worse than a notice that names a slightly later day and beats it. Treat the buffer as an author heuristic, not a rule: for a single room in a small property, half a day of slack is a common starting point, but the right figure depends on your own experience of how long rehanging takes.

Write the window as a start date and an end date, with times if the room is shared during the day. Avoid vague phrases like "sometime next week". If the exact date is genuinely unknown, say what the notice will be replaced with and when, so readers know the current version is provisional.

Write the notice in plain, readable language

A closure notice fails when a reader has to decode it. Use short sentences, everyday words and a consistent structure. A workable pattern is: heading, what is closed, dates, where to go instead, who to contact.

Make the heading do the work. "Room 4 closed for curtain cleaning, 3 to 6 March" tells a reader almost everything before they read the body. Put the heading at the top in the largest text on the page.

Use a readable type size and strong contrast between text and background. Avoid long paragraphs in all capitals, decorative fonts and pale grey text on white. If the notice is printed, laminate it or place it in a sleeve so it stays legible in a humid corridor.

Write dates in full, with the day of the week, so a reader cannot confuse day and month. State times using a 24-hour clock or a clear am/pm label, and use the same format throughout.

Add a short line explaining why the room is closed. "The curtains are being taken down for cleaning" is enough. People tolerate disruption better when the reason is visible, and it reduces the number of people who assume the room is closed by mistake and try the door.

Finally, read the notice aloud once. If a sentence trips you, rewrite it. That single check catches most of the wording that confuses readers under time pressure.

Place the notice where people actually look

One copy on a distant noticeboard is not a plan. Place the notice at the points where a reader's route changes: on the closed door itself, at the entrance to the corridor or wing, and at the main reception or shared lobby where people first arrive.

On the door, keep the notice at a height that suits both seated and standing readers, and avoid placing it where a door handle, signage or furniture obscures it. If the door has a small window, do not cover it entirely; people use the window to confirm the room is empty.

At the corridor or wing entrance, a slightly larger copy with a simple direction to the alternate space is more useful than a duplicate of the door notice. At reception, keep a copy that staff can point to and, if relevant, a short spoken script so the message is consistent.

Consider a second format for readers who do not pass the door: a short message in the same channel you already use for building updates, whether that is a printed sheet at the front desk or a notice in a shared area. Do not assume a single channel reaches everyone.

If the property serves guests who read more than one language, a short translated summary beneath the main text helps, provided someone competent checks the translation. A machine translation left unchecked can invert a date or a direction.

Name the alternate space and describe how to reach it

The most common gap in a closure notice is the alternative. Telling people a room is closed without telling them where to go pushes the problem onto whoever they find first.

Name the alternate space specifically: not "another room" but "the shared lounge on the same floor" or "the meeting room beside reception". Then add one line on how to get there, using landmarks a reader can see rather than compass directions.

Describe what the alternate space offers and what it does not. If it has fewer seats, say so. If it is only available during certain hours, state them. A reader who arrives expecting equivalent facilities and finds none will distrust the next notice you post.

For accommodation operators, the alternate space may need to cover the functions the closed room performed: somewhere to sit, somewhere to store belongings, somewhere to wait. For venue managers, it may be a holding area or a rearranged layout. Write the notice around the function, not the room number.

If no alternate space exists, say that plainly and give the reader a next step, such as speaking to the named contact. An honest "there is no substitute space during these dates" is more useful than silence.

Brief staff and decide who answers questions about changes

A notice is only half the communication. The other half is the person standing in front of it when a reader asks a question the notice does not answer.

Before the notice goes up, brief the people who will field questions: front desk staff, housekeeping, caretakers, volunteers or whoever holds the keys. Give them the same facts as the notice, plus the answers to the two questions most likely to follow: what happens if the dates change, and who decides.

Name a single point of contact for changes, and say how that person can be reached during the closure. Avoid listing several names with no indication of who decides. A reader who is passed between three people will assume nobody is in charge.

Agree a change procedure in advance. If the closure window moves, who updates the notice, by when, and how are people who already read the old version told? A short written line such as "any change will be posted at this door and at reception by the day before" sets a clear expectation without promising anything you cannot control.

Keep a copy of the final notice and the date it was posted. If a question arises later about what was communicated and when, that record settles it quickly. This is ordinary documentation practice, not a legal requirement, and it costs almost nothing to maintain.

A short pre-posting checklist

Run this checklist before the notice goes up. It is an author heuristic, not an audited standard, so adapt the items to your building.

One: the heading names the room and the dates. Two: the closure starts at takedown, not at collection. Three: the reopening date includes time for rehanging. Four: the alternate space is named, with one line on how to reach it. Five: the notice states what the alternate space does and does not offer. Six: a single contact for changes is named, with a route to reach them. Seven: the text is large, high-contrast and free of all-capital paragraphs. Eight: copies are placed at the door, the corridor or wing entrance, and reception. Nine: staff have been briefed and can answer the two likely follow-up questions. Ten: a dated copy is kept.

If any item is missing, fix it before posting. A notice that scores eight out of ten is usually better held back for an hour than posted incomplete and corrected twice.
