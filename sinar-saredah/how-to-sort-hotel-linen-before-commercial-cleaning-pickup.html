How to Sort Hotel Linen Before Commercial Cleaning Pickup

Published by Sinar Saredah Sdn Bhd.

A practical pre-pickup sorting checklist for Sarawak accommodation and venue teams: separate stained, torn and standard linen into labelled batches, keep a rejection log, and hand over cleanly.

Why sorting happens before the bag is tied, not after

Sorting hotel linen before commercial cleaning pickup is a handover decision, not a washing decision. By the time a bag leaves the property, the person receiving it should be able to tell what is inside, which items are standard stock, which need attention and which should not travel at all. That single discipline protects three things: the linen count, the condition record and the time your team spends explaining a mixed bag later.

The practical rule is to sort at the point of stripping, not at the loading bay. A housekeeper who pulls a stained pillowcase in a guest room already knows which room it came from and what the mark looks like. Once that pillowcase is folded into a general pile, that context is gone. A small trolley with three labelled compartments, or three clearly marked bags at the linen room door, is usually enough to keep the decision close to the source.

This guide is written for small hotels, guesthouses and venue managers in Sarawak who hand linen to an outside laundry service. It assumes you already have a collection arrangement and want the handover to be cleaner. The three-batch sort: standard, stained, torn

Start with three batches and resist adding more until the team is consistent with three. More categories sound thorough but tend to collapse under a busy checkout morning.

Batch A, standard: items that are clean enough to travel, complete, and free of visible damage. These are counted and bagged together by item type, so sheets with sheets and towels with towels.

Batch B, stained: items with a visible mark that the team wants assessed. Do not attempt to describe the chemistry of the stain. Record what you can see and where: room number or outlet, item type, position of the mark, and the date it was pulled. A short note such as "bath towel, room 204, brown mark near hem, pulled Tuesday" is more useful than a guess about the cause.

Batch C, torn or unserviceable: split seams, missing buttons on duvet covers, holes, frayed hems, or items so thin they no longer meet your own standard. These should not be sent for cleaning as if they were normal stock. Decide locally whether they go to repair, to a rag pile, or to retirement, and record the decision.

Keep the batches physically separate. A stained item that drifts back into the standard pile defeats the whole exercise, and a torn item that travels and returns unchanged costs everyone a second handling cycle. If your property uses colour-coded bags, make sure the colour meaning is written on the wall of the linen room, not held in one supervisor's memory.

Labelling that survives a van ride

A label is only useful if it is still readable when the bag is opened at the other end. Three details do the most work: a batch ID, an item count and a property or outlet name.

Use a simple batch ID format that a person can read aloud over the phone, for example a date plus a running number. Write the count as a number per item type rather than a single total, because a total of forty tells you nothing about whether the missing item was a sheet or a towel. Attach the label to the outside of the bag in a way that will not fall off, and put a duplicate note inside if your arrangement allows it.

For properties with more than one building or outlet, include the collection point in the label. A venue with a function room and a guest wing may generate two separate batches on the same day, and mixing them makes reconciliation harder than it needs to be.

Keep a paper or spreadsheet log at the collection point. The log should record the batch ID, the date, the item counts, who handed the bag over and who received it. This is the document you return to when a count does not match. It is also the document that makes a commercial cleaning pickup predictable, because the person collecting knows what to expect before the bag is opened.

The rejection log: recording what should not travel

A rejection log is a short record of items your team decided not to send, and why. It is not a complaint about the laundry service. It is an internal quality record that shows where linen is failing and how often.

A workable log has five columns: date, item type, batch or room reference, reason code, and decision. Reason codes can be as simple as stain, tear, wear, or other. The decision column records what happened next: sent anyway with a note, held for repair, retired, or returned to stock after a second look.

Review the log weekly, not daily. Patterns matter more than single entries. If one room type keeps producing torn duvet covers, the issue may be how the room is made up rather than how the linen is washed. If stains cluster around one outlet, the cause may sit in service rather than in the laundry cycle. The log gives you a factual basis for that conversation instead of an impression.

Keep the log for a defined period that matches your own record-keeping policy, and make sure it does not contain guest names or personal details. Room numbers and item types are enough for the purpose.

Matching the sort to your collection rhythm

Sorting works best when it is tied to a fixed collection rhythm rather than done in a rush before the van arrives. Decide how many days of linen the property holds, and sort on the day before collection so the batches are settled and counted.

If your property operates every day, including public holidays, the sorting routine needs to survive a holiday rota. Confirm your own collection timing in writing rather than assuming it matches the drop-off hours.

For properties in Kuching and Miri, the practical question is usually access rather than distance. A collection point that is blocked by a delivery van at the agreed time will delay the batch regardless of how well it was sorted. Walk the route from the linen room to the loading point at the time of day the collection actually happens, and note any pinch points. If the route is shared with guests, agree a service corridor or a time window that keeps bags out of guest sight.

Where a property is also handling specialist items such as curtains, carpets or soft furnishings, keep those out of the hotel linen batches entirely. They follow a different assessment and should be recorded separately.

A pre-pickup checklist you can reuse

Use this as a short walkthrough before the bag is sealed. It is an author's checklist, not an industry standard, and you should adapt the items to your own property.

One: are the three batches physically separate and clearly labelled? Two: does each bag carry a batch ID, a property or outlet name and a count by item type? Three: has the stained batch been described by position and date rather than by guessed cause? Four: have torn items been removed from the standard batch and logged? Five: does the rejection log have an entry for every item held back? Six: has the handover log been signed by the person releasing the bag? Seven: is the collection point clear of guest traffic and vehicle obstruction at the agreed time? Eight: are specialist items such as curtains or carpets kept out of the linen batches?

A simple scoring approach is to treat any unanswered item as a reason to pause rather than a reason to proceed. Teams that score eight out of eight on a quiet Tuesday may score five on a busy Saturday, and that gap is exactly what the checklist is for.

If your property is reviewing the wider arrangement rather than just the sorting step, it helps to look at the laundry service options alongside your internal routine, so the handover expectations and the service scope are decided together rather than separately.

What good looks like after the first month

After a month of consistent sorting, three things tend to become visible. The count reconciliation gets faster because the batches are already itemised. The rejection log starts to show whether problems are concentrated in one area. And the conversation with the laundry provider shifts from "something is missing" to a specific batch, date and item type.

None of this requires new equipment. It requires a labelled trolley or three bags, a printed log, and a habit of sorting at the point of stripping. The value is in the record, not in the sorting itself.
